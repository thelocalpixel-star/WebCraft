A Website for a Cleveland Law Firm

A person looking for a lawyer in Cleveland is usually dealing with something that matters: a family matter, a criminal charge, an injury, a business dispute, or a will that cannot wait. They are not browsing. They are deciding whether this firm is the right one to trust, and they are deciding it from a phone screen. A law firm website earns that trust by being specific, honest, and careful about what it promises.

One page per practice area

A firm that handles family law, criminal defense, personal injury, and estate planning should not put all four on one page. Each practice area has different searchers, different worries, and different next steps, and a single page that tries to serve them all serves none of them.

One page per practice area lets the firm explain, in plain words, what it does in that area, who it helps, and what the first step is.

Each practice page should answer the questions a client in that situation actually asks. A family law page should explain the process and the timeline in general terms, and what to bring to the first meeting. A criminal defense page should explain what happens after an arrest and why calling early matters.

An estate planning page should explain the documents involved and who needs them. None of these pages should give legal advice to a stranger, and none should predict an outcome. They should explain the process and invite the conversation.

Attorney bios that earn trust

A client hires a person, not a firm, and the attorney bio page is where that decision is made. A good bio says where the attorney went to school, how long they have practiced, and what they actually do day to day — the courts they appear in, the kinds of cases they handle, the approach they take.

It should be written in a voice the client can imagine talking to, not in the stiff language of a resume.

The bio should also say what the attorney does not do. A firm that declines cases outside its practice areas is not turning business away; it is telling the visitor that the firm knows its limits, and that honesty is itself a reason to trust it.

A photo helps, but only a real one: a stock photo of a lawyer in a suit, presented as a member of the firm, is a small dishonesty that a careful client will notice and remember.

Intake that checks for conflicts first

Before a firm can help a new client, it has to know whether it can: whether the firm already represents the other side, or has a conflict that prevents it from taking the case. The website's intake should be built around that check.

The first questions should be the names of the parties involved and the kind of matter, so the firm can run the conflict check before anyone shares details of their case.

The form should also say, plainly, that filling it out does not create an attorney-client relationship, and that the visitor should not send confidential details until the firm has agreed to take the matter. That warning protects the visitor and the firm alike.

A form that invites a visitor to describe their whole case in an open text box, with no warning and no conflict check, is a risk the firm does not need — and a sign to a careful reader that the firm is not careful.

Advertising rules are part of the design

Lawyers in Tennessee practice under advertising rules that limit what a firm can claim, and the website has to follow them. No misleading statements, no claims the firm cannot support, no testimonials that create an unjustified expectation, and no language that promises a result.

A firm that has won cases can say so in general terms, with the facts behind the statement; it cannot promise the next client the same outcome.

The safest rule for a law firm website is to describe the work and let the reader judge it. Explain the process, name the practice areas, show the attorneys, and invite the call.

A site that claims to be the best, the fastest, or the most aggressive is not persuading a careful client; it is telling that client the firm values the claim more than the work. The firms that earn trust online are the ones that write like they practice: precisely, and without overstatement.

The first call, and what happens after it

The website's job ends when the phone rings, and its last section should prepare the visitor for that call. Say what the first consultation costs, if anything, and how long it lasts. Say what to bring, and what will happen in the meeting: the firm listens, explains the options in general terms, and says whether it can help.

Say how quickly the firm returns calls, because a person in a legal problem measures a firm by its first response.

Keep the contact details where a worried visitor can find them without searching: the phone number at the top of every page, the office hours, and the address for the Cleveland office. A person who has just been arrested, or just been served papers, does not have the patience for a contact page hidden behind a menu.

Make calling easy, make the next step clear, and let the firm's care show in the details the website gets right.
