A Five-Page Website Plan for Rome, GA

A Rome business rarely serves only Rome. Customers come from Floyd County — Silver Creek, Lindale, Cave Spring, Shannon — and from the towns along the highways into town. A five-page website should say Rome first and serve the county honestly, without claiming Atlanta reach it cannot deliver. This plan covers the five pages, what each one should say, and the county line that keeps the site honest.

Rome first, Floyd County second

A Rome website should say Rome in its first sentence, because that is what a customer searches for. A person in Rome types “plumber Rome GA” or “roofing Rome” when they need work done, and the page that answers is the page that names Rome. But the second sentence should name the county, because a Rome business that only claims the city turns away customers from Silver Creek, Lindale and Cave Spring who would happily drive in — or who expect the business to drive out.

The county line is also the honest limit. A Rome business should not claim Atlanta, an hour and a half away, or Chattanooga, an hour north, unless it genuinely serves those cities. Claiming them brings enquiries the business cannot take, and visitors who leave when they learn the truth. Rome and Floyd County is a real service area; Rome plus everywhere is a promise the business cannot keep.

Page one: the service, named plainly

The first page is the service itself, named the way a customer would say it. “Roof Repair in Rome, GA” is a page. “Quality Roofing Solutions” is a slogan, and it could belong to any business in any town. The page should say what the service includes, how long it usually takes, and what it costs to find out more — the three things a customer wants before they call.

The first page should also say, in one line, that the website is a five-page plan, not a brochure. A five-page website built for $500 covers the service, the area, the proof, the questions and the call — five pages that do five jobs, instead of twenty pages that do none of them. A customer who finds the service page first should not have to hunt for the rest.

Page two: the area you actually serve

The second page is the area, and it should name the places the business genuinely serves: Rome, Silver Creek, Lindale, Cave Spring, Shannon, and the communities along the highways in between. Each place named should be a place the business will actually go. A list of twenty towns copied from a map is not a service area; it is a wish list, and customers can tell the difference when they call and are told no.

The area page should also say what distance changes. If a job in Cave Spring costs more than a job in Rome, say so before the customer calls. If the business only serves the far end of the county on certain days, say that too. An area page that is honest about its limits brings the right customers; an area page that claims everywhere brings the wrong ones.

Page three: proof a neighbour would recognise

The third page is proof, and the best proof is specific to Rome. A roofer can describe how a roof holds up to a northwest Georgia summer and what the winter rain does to flashing. A plumber can say what the older houses near downtown Rome usually need, and what the newer builds on the edge of town do not. Those details are proof because only a business that works in Rome would know them — and they are also the words a Rome customer uses when they search.

Proof is not a list of adjectives. Experienced, reliable, trusted and committed are words every business uses, and they prove nothing. Proof is what the work includes, how long it takes, what the guarantee covers, and what happens if something goes wrong. One paragraph of that is worth more than a page of claims.

Pages four and five: the questions and the call

The fourth page answers the questions a Rome customer asks before calling: how much, how soon, and what happens if it goes wrong. A short list of honest answers — even “it depends, and here is what it depends on” — saves the business a phone call and earns the customer’s trust. A page that hides its answers behind a contact form is a page that sends the customer to a competitor who answers.

The fifth page is the call: one phone number, one form, one next step. A visitor who has read four pages should not have to decide how to get in touch; the site should decide for them. The number should be tappable on a phone, the form should ask for the service, the town and a way to reply, and the page should say when the business will answer. Five pages, five jobs, one call at the end.
