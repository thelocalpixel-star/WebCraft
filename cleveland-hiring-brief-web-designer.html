A Cleveland Hiring Brief for a New Website

A good website project starts before anyone designs anything. A short written brief — what the site must do, what you will provide, and who owns what when it is finished — turns three different quotes into three answers to the same question. This guide walks through the brief a Cleveland service business should write first, and the questions to ask every designer who replies.

Write the brief before you call anyone

A website project goes wrong most often before it starts, when three designers quote three different jobs. A short written brief fixes that. One page is enough: the services you want inquiries about, the pages the site needs, the photos and words you can provide, the area you serve, and the one action a visitor should take — a phone call, or a quote request. For most Cleveland service businesses, five pages cover it: home, services, service area, about, and contact.

Add two lines that matter more than the rest: who owns the domain, the files and the content when the work is done, and what happens if you stop paying for monthly care. A brief that names those two points turns a sales call into a straight answer. Keep the brief to one page and send the same version to everyone you ask.

The eight questions to ask every designer

Use the same eight questions with every designer, and write the answers down. Who owns the domain name, the hosting account, the website files and the written content? What is included in the quoted price — how many pages, how many rounds of changes, photos, a contact form, inquiry tracking? How long from brief to launch, and what usually delays it? How do you review a draft before it goes live? Who edits the site after launch, and what does that cost? What happens to the site if you stop the monthly care? And what happens if the designer stops trading?

That last question sounds awkward, and it is the one that saves the most trouble. A one-person business can close, move or retire. The answer you want is a handoff list: domain registrar login, hosting account, site files, and a written note of what runs where. If a designer cannot describe the handoff, the price is not the problem — the exit is.

What a good answer sounds like

A good answer is specific. “You own the domain, the hosting account and the files, and here is the handoff checklist you receive at launch” is an answer. “Don’t worry, we handle everything” is not — it tells you nothing about what you own. The same test works for price: “$500 for a five-page site, two rounds of changes, and $20 a month optional care” can be compared with another quote. “It depends” cannot, until the designer says what it depends on.

Cleveland shoppers have one advantage: some local firms publish their prices. One Cleveland-area agency, Candor, publishes a $399-a-month local SEO retainer with a $499 setup fee, and other firms publish monthly website plans around $99 to $130 a month. Use those published numbers to test any quote you receive — not to copy them, but to ask what the same money buys in each case, and what you own at the end of it. Published prices change; check the source before you decide.

Compare quotes on the same scope

A quote for a five-page site and a quote for a monthly plan are not the same product, and the headline numbers hide it. Put every quote on the same basis: what it costs in year one, what it costs in year two, and what you own when the payments stop. A $500 one-time website with optional $20-a-month care costs at most $740 in year one and $240 a year after that. A $99-a-month plan costs $1,188 every year, for as long as you keep it.

The monthly plan is not automatically the worse deal — it can include care, edits and support that a one-time price does not. The point is to compare the totals and the ownership, not the first number on the page. Decide on the handoff, not the homepage

Before you agree to anything, ask to see the handoff. It should list the domain registrar and login, the hosting account, the website files, and who to call for changes. Then test the draft on your own phone before it goes live: can you find the phone number, the service list, the area served and the contact form without hunting? A site that fails that test on your phone fails it for your customers too.

A hiring brief is not paperwork for its own sake. It is the difference between a website you own and understand, and a monthly bill you cannot explain. Write the one page, ask the eight questions, and choose the designer whose answers are specific enough to write down.
