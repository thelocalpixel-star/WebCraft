A Cleveland Analytics Monthly Routine

A Cleveland business does not need to live in its analytics. It needs one routine, once a month, that answers three questions: how many inquiries came in, where they came from, and which pages earned them. Everything else in the reports is either detail for those three questions or noise. This guide is the routine, in the order to read it, for a city of 47,356 people where most customers still arrive by phone.

Start with the inquiry, not the visit

Open the month's inquiry count before opening the analytics at all: the calls logged, the forms received, the bookings taken. That number is the month, and the analytics exist to explain it, not to replace it.

A month with more visits and fewer inquiries is a worse month than a month with fewer visits and more inquiries, and no chart in the analytics will say that as plainly as the two numbers side by side. Write the inquiry count at the top of the page before reading anything else.

The reason the order matters is that analytics is very good at offering impressive numbers that mean nothing. A spike in visits from a page that brings no inquiries is not a success; it is a curiosity, and it should be treated as one until it produces a caller.

The routine starts with the inquiry count because the count is the only number the business would miss if it disappeared. Everything after it is an explanation of that number, and if a report cannot be tied back to it, it can be skipped.

Where the inquiries came from

For each inquiry, note where it came from: search, a direct visit, a referral from another site, or a paid campaign.

The analytics will show the same split for visits, but the split that matters is the one for inquiries, because the channels behave differently — search may bring the most visits while direct visits bring the most bookings, and a campaign may bring clicks that never call.

Count the inquiries per channel, not the visits per channel, and the business will see which channel is earning its keep.

The pattern to watch is a channel that brings visits and no inquiries for two months running.

That channel is either reaching the wrong people or sending them to the wrong page, and the fix is different in each case: the wrong people means the targeting or the search terms need narrowing, while the wrong page means the landing page needs a clearer next step.

Do not judge a channel on one month — a single month can be moved by a holiday, a storm, or a local event near Lee University, where about 3,714 students shape the town's calendar. Two months of the same pattern is a signal.

Which pages earned the inquiry

Next, look at the pages the inquiries came through.

The analytics will show which pages visitors landed on and which pages they visited before contacting the business, and the two lists are not the same: a visitor may land on the home page and contact from a service page, or land on a service page and contact from the contact page.

The page that earned the inquiry is the one that carried the next step the visitor used, and that page deserves to be studied and copied.

The pages to fix are the ones with visits and no inquiries. Open each one and read it as a stranger would: is the next step visible without scrolling, does the page answer the question its title promises, does it say which towns the business serves?

A Cleveland service page that hides the phone number, or that talks about the business instead of the job the searcher typed, will keep collecting visits and losing callers. One fix per page, one page per month, and the list of pages that earn inquiries grows while the list that only earns visits shrinks.

What to read, and what to ignore

Not every report deserves the same attention. The reports worth reading each month are the ones that touch the three questions: the inquiry count, the channels that brought the inquiries, and the pages that earned them.

Inside those reports, read the landing pages that produced contact, the search terms that led visitors in, and the time visitors spent on the service pages — because a service page that is read carefully and still produces no inquiry is telling the business something different from a page nobody reads at all.

What to ignore is as much a part of the routine as what to read. Ignore the total number of page views, which rises whenever one curious visitor reads ten pages. Ignore the real-time visitor count, which is a snapshot, not a trend.

Ignore the bounce rate on its own, which cannot tell a satisfied visitor who found the phone number from a visitor who left in disgust. Read the inquiries, the channels, and the pages — and let the rest of the reports wait until one of those three numbers asks a question only they can answer.

The monthly routine, written down

The routine fits on one page: the inquiry count, the split by channel, the pages that earned inquiries, and the one page to fix next month.

Same day each month, same order, same three numbers, and the record becomes a history the business can actually use — which months the inquiries rose, what changed in those months, and whether the change was the page, the season, or the channel.

A business that keeps this record for a year knows its own market; a business that does not is guessing, however good the charts look.

Keep the routine to the same day each month, and resist the urge to check the numbers in between. A week is too short to judge a channel, and a day is too short to judge a page.

The monthly rhythm is what makes the record comparable: the same questions, asked the same way, month after month, until the pattern is visible. Analytics rewards the business that reads it on a schedule and acts on what it finds — not the one that watches it every day and changes something every time it moves.
