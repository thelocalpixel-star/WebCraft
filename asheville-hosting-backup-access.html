Asheville Hosting Backups and Access

An Asheville business owner is often away from the desk — at a market, a show, a studio, a supplier — and the website still has to work while they are gone. This guide covers backups and access for a business whose owner travels: access that belongs to the business, backups that happen without being remembered, and a handover the person on duty can actually use.

The owner is away, and the site still has to work

Makers, artists, hospitality and service businesses in Asheville spend their days out — at markets and shows, with clients, on the road — and the website is the one employee that never leaves. It has to keep answering while the owner is away: the number, the hours, the form, the booking link. A site that only works when the owner is at the desk is a site that stops working on the busiest days of the year.

That means someone other than the owner has to be able to change the site — and they have to be able to do it without the owner, who may be unreachable on the day the hours change or the form breaks. Access should belong to the business, not to one person, and the person on duty should know the one rule that matters: back up first, change second, check the site immediately after.

Access that belongs to the business, not one person

The hosting account, the domain registrar, the email — each should be reachable by two people, in a password manager the business controls, not in one person’s head or phone. Two people should be able to reach the account, not one, because the day only one person can reach it is the day that person is away, or ill, or has left the business.

When a person leaves, change one password in the manager and the handover is done, instead of spending a week locked out of the business’s own website. And when the owner is away at a show, the person on duty can log in and change the hours without waiting for a reply that may not come until Monday. Access that belongs to the business is access that works when the business needs it, not only when the owner is at the desk.

Backups that happen without being remembered

A backup that depends on someone remembering is a backup that stops the week the owner travels. The backup should run on its own, on a schedule, and someone should check that it ran — once a month, ten minutes, a look at the date of the last backup and a look at what it contains. A backup nobody checks is a backup nobody knows is broken.

A backup has two parts, and both matter: the files, which are the design and the pages, and the content, which is the words, the form entries and the settings. A backup that holds only the files can rebuild the look of the site, but not the words. A backup that holds only the content can rebuild the words, but not the look. The business needs both, from the same date, stored somewhere the host does not control — a folder on the office computer, updated once a month, is a second copy.

The change that happens while the owner is away

The change that matters most is the one made while the owner is away: the hours change for a holiday, the offer changes for a market weekend, the event page goes up the day before the event. The person on duty needs the login to make it, and the rule to make it safely — back up first, change second, check the site immediately after. A change made without a fresh backup is a gamble, and the odds of that bet get worse every time the owner travels.

The handover is one page, kept where the owner can find it and where the person on duty can find it too: the hosting provider’s name, the account email, the domain registrar, and who holds each password. One page, written down, checked once a month. The businesses that write it down never need it urgently. The businesses that do not, need it all at once, on the day the owner is away and the site is wrong.

The restore test, done in the quiet month

A backup that has never been restored is a promise, not a plan. The time to test it is the quiet month, not the busy one: restore the copy to a test address and check that the pages load, the form sends, and the phone number is right. If the restore works, the business has a backup. If it does not, the business has learned something cheaply instead of expensively, on a quiet day instead of the morning the site is down.

The off-season test also catches the slow decay nobody notices in the busy months: the backup that stopped running when the card expired, the password that changed and was never written down, the domain renewal date creeping closer, the login details that live only in the owner’s phone. Ten minutes, once in the quiet month, is the cheapest insurance a travelling owner’s website has. The businesses that test in the quiet month never need the backup urgently in the busy one.
