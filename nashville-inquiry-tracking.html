A Nashville Inquiry Tracking Guide

A Nashville business in a large metro can receive many inquiries in a week: calls, forms and messages from across the city and the surrounding communities. Without a simple record, those inquiries blur together. The website, the map listing and word of mouth all get the credit, and nobody knows which one actually earned the work. This guide sets up a tracking habit sized for a busy market.

Separate a real inquiry from noise first

In a big city the phone rings for many reasons that are not customers: sales calls, wrong numbers, automated messages and form spam. Before counting anything, decide what counts as an inquiry for your business. A useful definition is simple: a person asking about work you do, with a way to reach them back.

Write that definition down where the people who answer the phone can see it, and count only what fits it. Clicks, page views and form starts are not inquiries. A tally padded with sales calls will tell you the wrong story about which channel works, and the wrong story leads to the wrong spending.

Record where each inquiry came from

Ask every inquirer the same short question: how did you find us? Then write the answer down in the same words each time, so the tally can be added up later. Website form, map listing, referral from a past customer and repeat customer are four honest categories; a fifth, “not sure,” is better than a guess.

The question costs ten seconds and the record costs less. After a month the answers show a pattern no analytics dashboard states plainly: whether the website or the map listing is doing the work, and whether referrals still carry the business. That pattern is the basis for the next decision about where to spend time.

Note the service area the caller is in

Nashville is a large market, and an inquiry from across town is a different job from one in your own neighbourhood. Record the caller’s area in a few words — the part of town, or the nearby community such as Franklin or Brentwood when the caller is outside the city. Over a month, the areas pile up into a picture of where your work actually comes from.

That picture matters for the website too. If most inquiries come from two or three areas, the service-area wording should name those areas plainly and stop promising coverage it cannot deliver. If inquiries come from further out than you can serve, the wording needs to say so before the call, not after.

Give every inquiry one owner and one reply time

In a competitive market, a slow reply is a lost customer: the next business on the list is one tap away. Decide who replies to inquiries, and by when, and write it down. A simple rule works well: every inquiry gets a named owner and a reply the same business day, with the owner’s name written next to the inquiry on the list.

Keep one short list that the whole team can see, and check it once a day for anything unanswered. The list does not need software; a shared sheet with the date, the source, the area and the owner is enough. What it needs is the daily check, because an inquiry with no owner is an inquiry nobody answers.

Review the month, then change one thing

At the end of the month, add up the tally by source and by area, and ask what it says. If the map listing brings most calls and the website brings few, the website’s contact path needs work. If one area produces inquiries you keep turning down, the service-area wording needs to change. Then change one thing — a page, a form field, a listing detail — and watch the next month’s tally.

Resist changing everything at once. One change at a time is the only way to know what worked. The measure is inquiries that fit your definition, not visits or clicks, and the habit is the monthly review itself: a small business that reads its own numbers every month stops guessing about its marketing.
