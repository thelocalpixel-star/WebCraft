Choosing a Domain Name in Cleveland, TN

A domain name is the one part of a website a business keeps for years, through redesigns, new phones, and new owners. It is also the part most businesses choose in ten minutes and regret for a decade. A Cleveland business picking a domain should pick the way it would pick a shop sign: short, easy to say, easy to spell, and owned outright.

.com first, your business name first

Start with the business name, with .com after it. A Cleveland business called by its own name, at its own .com, is the easiest name to remember, the easiest to say on the phone, and the easiest for a customer to type without thinking.

If the exact name is taken, add the town or the service — cleveland, or the trade — rather than reaching for a different ending, because customers will type .com first whether you tell them to or not.

The other endings have their place, but they are a compromise, not a first choice.

A .net, a .co, or a .biz will work, and a customer who clicks a link will arrive just the same — but a customer who hears the name on the radio, on a truck, or in conversation will type .com, and land on someone else's site.

Pay the small extra effort to get the .com, or accept that some of your customers will find your competitor first.

Short beats clever

Short beats clever, every time. A domain should be easy to say once, over the phone, to a customer who is writing it on the back of a receipt. If you have to spell it out letter by letter, it is too long. If it needs a hyphen to make sense, it is too long.

If it needs a number, the customer will forget whether it was a word or a digit — and the digit will lose.

Clever is the other trap. A pun, a play on words, or a name only the owner understands is a name the customer has to decode before they can use it, and most customers will not bother. The best domain names are boring: the business name, the town, the trade, in that order, as short as they can be.

Boring is memorable. Clever is forgettable, and forgettable is expensive.

Check the trademark before you fall in love

Before you fall in love with a name, check whether someone else already owns it — not just as a domain, but as a business name and a trademark. Search the name on the web, search it in the trademark database, and search it on the social networks where customers will look for you.

A name that belongs to another business in Tennessee, or another business in your trade, is a name that will cost you a letter from a lawyer and a rebuild of everything you printed.

The check takes an afternoon and saves a year. If the name is clear, register it. If it is not, change the name now, while it is still cheap — add the town, add the trade, pick a different word — rather than after the signs are printed, the cards are ordered, and the website is live.

A domain dispute is the most expensive way to learn that a name was taken, and the cheapest lesson is the search you do before you buy.

Register it in your own name

Register the domain in your own name, on your own account, with your own email address — never in a provider's name, and never on an account you cannot reach. The domain is the business's address on the internet, and a business that does not own its own address is renting its own name.

If the provider registers it for you, ask for the login, change the password, and confirm that your name is on the registration.

Turn on auto-renewal, and keep the email address on the account one you actually read. Domains expire quietly, and an expired domain is a website that stops working, an email address that stops receiving, and a name that anyone can buy the day after it lapses.

A domain registered in your own name, renewing automatically, on an email you read, is a domain you will still own in ten years — and that is the whole point of choosing carefully in the first place.

What to do with the old name

If the business already has a domain — an old one, a long one, a misspelled one — do not throw it away. Keep it, point it at the new site, and let the old links, the old cards, and the old customers find their way to the new name.

A redirect costs nothing and saves every customer who still has the old address.

And do not let the old name lapse while the new one is still earning its place. Renew it for another year, keep the redirect in place, and check once a year whether anyone is still using it.

The old name is a bridge, not a burden — and the day nobody crosses it anymore is the day you can let it go, not the day the new site launches.
