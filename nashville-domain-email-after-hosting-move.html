Nashville Domains After a Hosting Move

A Nashville business that moves hosting has more to move than a website. The domain is the address every customer types, the email is the inbox every order lands in, and the booking or ordering system is the till. This guide covers the move in the order that keeps all three working: the booking link first, the domain second, the email before the switch — and the switch last.

The move that breaks the booking link

Nashville businesses — venues, services, restaurants, tourism — take bookings and orders through their websites, and the booking link lives at the domain. A move that copies the website perfectly and mishandles the domain sends customers to the wrong place, or nowhere at all — and a customer who cannot book does not call to ask why. They book with the next business on the list.

Before any move, list every link a customer can use to book, order, or pay: on the site, on the Business Profile, on social media, in the email signature. Check each one after the move, by clicking it, not by assuming it was updated. A booking link that still points at the old host is a booking the business loses quietly, and the first sign of it is a week with no bookings and no explanation.

Who holds the domain, and whose name is on it

Look up the domain’s registration and read three things: who the registrar is, whose name and email the domain is registered under, and when it expires. The domain should be registered in the business’s name, with a business email address, at a registrar the business can reach directly. If it is registered in the designer’s name, or the host’s name, or under a personal email address that nobody checks, the business does not fully own its own address.

If the domain is not in the business’s name, the time to fix it is before the move, while the hosting is still paid and the current holder is still answering. Transferring a domain to the business’s own registrar account takes days when the current holder cooperates, and weeks when it does not. A business that discovers it does not own its domain in the middle of a hosting move has two problems at once, and the domain is the worse of the two.

The sixty-day lock, and the move that has to wait

Domains cannot always be moved when the business wants them moved. After a domain is first registered, or after it is transferred to a new registrar, it is locked for sixty days — a rule that stops a domain being stolen by a quick transfer. The lock is real, it is not negotiable, and a move planned inside those sixty days has to wait. A business planning a hosting move should check the lock first, because a move planned for next week that cannot happen for two months is a plan, not a crisis, if it is known in advance.

The lock is also a reason to separate the two moves. A hosting move — changing where the website files live — does not need a domain transfer. The domain can stay with its current registrar, and only its settings change to point at the new host. The domain transfer, if the business wants one, can happen later, after the sixty days, on its own timetable. One change at a time, tested before it goes live, is the whole method — and the domain is the change that should never be rushed.

Email first, website second, switch last

Email is the part of a move that breaks most often, and it should be asked about first, not last. Where do the business’s mailboxes live now — with the old host, or with a provider like Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. Where will they live after the move. Who copies the mail records — the entries attached to the domain name that say where email goes — and who checks that they are copied exactly. A single mistyped character sends email nowhere, and the error gives no warning: messages simply stop arriving, and the business hears about it from a customer who was ignored.

The move itself has a safe order, and it is worth following exactly. First, move the mailboxes, and the old messages with them, while the old hosting is still running. Second, copy the files and the content to the new host and build the site there completely. Third, test the copy on a temporary address: every page loads, the booking link works, the form sends, the phone number is right, and the email still arrives. Fourth, and only then, change the domain to point at the new host. The switch is the last step, not the first, because everything that can be tested should be tested before customers see it.

The week after: bookings, email, forms, old host kept

Keep the old hosting running for at least a week after the switch. Some visitors and some mail servers will still hold the old address in their memory, and the old server quietly catching them is what keeps the move invisible. Cancelling the old hosting on the day of the switch is the one mistake that turns a smooth move into a week of lost messages and lost bookings.

In that week, watch four things: the booking and ordering links work from a phone, not just a desk; the contact form sends and the reply arrives; email arrives at every address; and the search results show no sudden errors. A move on the same domain with the same addresses and the same content is invisible to Google — rankings drop when addresses change without redirects, or when a staging copy left behind a setting that blocks search engines. Check those two, and the move is done. The domain, the email, the bookings and the website all survived, because the domain was treated as the move itself.
