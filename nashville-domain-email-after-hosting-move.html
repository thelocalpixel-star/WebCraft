Nashville Domains After a Hosting Move

A Nashville business that moves hosting has more to move than a website. The domain is the address every customer types, the email is the inbox every order lands in, and the booking or ordering system is the till. This guide covers the move in the order that keeps all three working: the booking link first, the domain second, the email before the switch — and the switch last.

The move that breaks the booking link

Nashville businesses — venues, services, restaurants, tourism — take bookings and orders through their websites, and the booking link lives at the domain. A move that copies the website perfectly and mishandles the domain sends customers to the wrong place, or nowhere at all — and a customer who cannot book does not call to ask why.

They book with the next business on the list.

Before any move, list every link a customer can use to book, order, or pay: on the site, on the Business Profile, on social media, in the email signature. Check each one after the move, by clicking it, not by assuming it was updated.

A booking link that still points at the old host is a booking the business loses quietly, and the first sign of it is a week with no bookings and no explanation.

Who holds the domain, and whose name is on it

Check the domain’s registration and note three things: which registrar holds it, whose name and email it sits under, and when it runs out. It should sit in the business’s name, on a business email address, with a registrar the business can reach directly.

If it sits in the designer’s name, or the host’s name, or on a personal email nobody checks, the business does not fully own its own address.

Fix the ownership before the move, not during it — while the hosting is still paid and the person holding the domain still answers the phone. Moving a domain into the business’s own registrar account takes days when the current holder cooperates, and weeks when they do not.

A Nashville business that finds out mid-move that it does not own its own domain has two problems at once — the move, and the name every booking link points to — and the name is the worse of the two.

The sixty-day lock, and the move that has to wait

A domain is not always free to move when the business wants it moved. Once a domain is first registered, or once it has been transferred to a new registrar, it stays locked for sixty days — the rule is there so a domain cannot be stolen by a quick transfer.

The lock is real, and no one can talk their way around it, so a move planned inside those sixty days has to wait. Look at the lock before planning anything: a move set for next week that cannot happen for two months is a plan rather than a crisis, as long as the business knows in advance.

The lock is also why the two moves should stay separate. Shifting the hosting — moving where the website files live — needs no domain transfer at all. The domain can stay where it is, with only its settings changed to point at the new host.

The transfer itself, if the business wants one, can wait until the sixty days run out and happen when it suits. Take one change at a time, test each before it goes live, and never rush the domain — it is the one change that cannot be hurried.

Email first, website second, switch last

Email is the part of a move that breaks most often, and it should be asked about first, not last. Where do the business’s mailboxes live now — with the old host, or with a provider like Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. Where will they live after the move.

Who copies the mail records — the entries attached to the domain name that say where email goes — and who checks that they are copied exactly. A single mistyped character sends email nowhere, and the error gives no warning: messages simply stop arriving, and the business hears about it from a customer who was ignored.

The move has a safe order, and it pays to follow it exactly.

  1. Move the mailboxes — old messages included — while the old hosting is still running.
  2. Copy the files and the content onto the new host and build the site there in full.
  3. Test that copy on a temporary address: every page loads, the booking link works, the form sends, the phone number is right, and the mail still arrives.
  4. And only then, point the domain at the new host.

The switch goes last, not first, because everything that can be tested should be tested before customers see it.

The week after: bookings, email, forms, old host kept

Keep the old hosting alive for at least a week past the switch. Some visitors and some mail servers still remember the old address, and the old server quietly catching them is what makes the move invisible.

Switch the new hosting on and cancel the old the same day, and that one mistake turns a smooth move into a week of lost messages and lost bookings.

In that week, keep an eye on four things: the booking and ordering links work from a phone, not just a desk; the contact form sends and the reply comes back; mail reaches every address; and the search results show no sudden errors.

Google notices nothing when the domain, the addresses and the content all stay the same — rankings fall when addresses change with no redirects, or when a staging copy left behind a setting that blocks search engines.

Check those two, and the move is finished: the domain, the email, the bookings and the website all made it, because the domain was treated as the move itself.
