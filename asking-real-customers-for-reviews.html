Asking Real Customers for Reviews

A review is the closest thing to a referral a website can show — and the business cannot write its own. The reviews that convince a customer are the ones real customers wrote in their own words, after real work. This guide covers asking for them: when to ask, how to make it easy, what never to offer, and the monthly routine that keeps them coming.

Ask when the work is still fresh

The best time to ask for a review is right after the work is done, while the customer can still picture it — the finished job, the problem solved, the day it happened. A week later the detail is gone, and the review, if it comes at all, is a polite sentence with nothing in it.

Ask on the day the work finishes, or the day after, when the customer is still glad it is done.

Do not wait for a perfect moment, because there is not one. One ask, in plain words, at the end of the job: the work is done, here is the link, a few honest sentences help the next customer decide.

If the customer says they will do it later, give them the link now — later rarely comes, and a link in a text message is easier to find than a promise remembered a week on.

Make the ask easy to say yes to

A review the customer has to hunt for is a review that never happens. One link, one sentence, in person or by text or email — the customer should reach the review form in one tap, without creating an account, without a lecture about how to write it.

The link goes to the place the business wants reviews: its Business Profile, where customers already look.

Say what the review is for, in one sentence: it helps the next customer decide. Do not tell the customer what to write, and do not hand them a script — a review the business dictated is not a review.

A short honest review in the customer’s own words is worth more than a long polished one, because the next customer is reading for the customer’s voice, not the business’s.

Let customers use their own words

No scripts, no suggested sentences, no “feel free to say” followed by the words to say. A review that sounds like the business wrote it convinces nobody, and a customer asked to copy a script will either refuse or write something stilted that reads like an advertisement.

The customer’s own words — the job, the town, what went right — are the whole value of the review.

Never pay for a review, offer a discount for one, or give anything in exchange for one. Paying for reviews breaks Google’s rules, and customers can tell when a review was bought — the words are too smooth, the detail too thin.

A bought review is not proof, and a business caught buying them loses the trust the reviews were supposed to build. The honest way is the only way that lasts: ask real customers, after real work, and publish what they say.

What to do with the review once it arrives

Reply to every review, good or bad. Thank the customer, answer the point they made, and keep it short — a reply is a conversation, not a press release. A critical review is a repair list, not an attack: read what it names, fix what can be fixed, and say what changed.

A business that answers a critical review well earns more trust than a business with only praise.

Display real reviews on the website only with the customer’s permission — the words belong to the customer, not the business. One detailed review on the service page, naming the job and the town, beats a wall of stars with no detail, because the visitor is not counting stars.

They are deciding whether to trust this business with their own job, and a real customer’s words, in their own voice, are what decide it.

A routine that keeps reviews coming

Once a month, list who finished work, who was asked, and who replied. The ask is part of finishing the job, not an afterthought for a quiet day — and a month with no asks is a month with no new reviews, whatever the website says.

The list is short, the time is ten minutes, and the habit is what keeps reviews coming instead of arriving in bursts and stopping.

Measure inquiries, not review counts. Reviews are proof for the next customer, and the proof compounds: each real review makes the next visitor a little more likely to call, and each call is a chance to earn another review.

A business that asks every customer, every month, builds a record no competitor can copy — because the record is its own customers, in their own words.
