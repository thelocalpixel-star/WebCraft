The two ways websites are sold

A Cleveland business shopping for a website will see two kinds of price. A one-time payment: you pay for the build, you own the site, and any monthly care is optional. A monthly plan: you pay every month for the site, the hosting and the care bundled together. The headline numbers look nothing alike, and neither tells the whole story — because the question is not only what you pay, but what you own when the payments stop.

WebCraft sells the first way: a $500 one-time website, with $20-a-month hosting and care that is optional, not required. Other firms in this market sell the second way, with published monthly prices around $99 to $130 a month, and one firm publishing a $4,000 one-time option. This guide compares them with year-one and year-two totals, using published prices only.

Year one, side by side

Put every option on the same basis. A WebCraft site costs $500 once, plus optional care at $20 a month — at most $740 in year one, and $500 if you arrange hosting yourself. A monthly plan at $99 a month — the published price of two firms in this market — costs $1,188 in year one. At $130 a month, the published price of another, it costs $1,560. A hosting-only plan at $9.99 a month costs about $120 a year, but it is hosting alone: no design, no pages, no care.

The $4,000 one-time option published by one firm is the most expensive year one on this list, and it is also a purchase rather than a rental — the distinction that matters most. Prices are published prices, and they change; check the source before you decide. The point of the comparison is the method: same scope, same twelve months, totals in writing.

Year two changes the answer

Year two is where the two models separate. A one-time site costs $240 a year in optional care — or nothing beyond hosting you arrange yourself — because the build is already paid for. A $99-a-month plan costs another $1,188, and a $130-a-month plan another $1,560, every year, for as long as you keep it. Over five years, the one-time route costs $500 plus five years of optional care: $1,700 in total. The $99-a-month route costs $5,940.

That gap is the real price of the monthly model, and it is worth paying only if the monthly fee buys work you actually need — regular edits, support, and someone watching the site. If the site rarely changes, the monthly fee is mostly rent on a site you could have owned. Ask any monthly provider what the fee covers in a normal month, and what happens to the site if you cancel.

What the monthly fee actually buys

A monthly fee can buy three different things, and a quote should say which. Care: updates, backups, and someone to call when the site breaks. Edits: changes to pages, photos and prices when you ask. Or rent: the right to keep using a site that is not yours. The first two are services worth paying for. The third is the one to spot, because a rented site disappears when the payments stop — along with the pages Google had started to find.

Ask it plainly, in writing: if I cancel, is the website mine, and can I move it to another host with my domain? A provider who answers yes is selling care. A provider who answers no, or who will not answer, is selling rent. The answer decides whether the monthly total is a service bill or a lease.

The honest case for each

Neither model is a scam, and each fits a different business. A monthly plan fits a business that wants one bill, no involvement, and someone else to handle every change — and is happy to keep paying for that. A one-time site fits a business that wants to own the asset, pay for care only when it is needed, and keep the option of moving on with the site in hand.

The wrong choice is the one made without the totals. Add up year one and year two, ask the ownership question, and choose the model whose answers you can live with for five years, not five weeks. A Cleveland hiring brief walks through the questions to ask every designer before you sign.
