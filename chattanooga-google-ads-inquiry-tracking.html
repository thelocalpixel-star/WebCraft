Tracking Google Ads Inquiries in Chattanooga

A Chattanooga business running Google Ads should judge the campaign on inquiries, not clicks. Chattanooga is a bigger market than the towns around it, so the clicks come faster and the spend grows faster — and the only way to know whether the money is working is to count the calls and forms the ads actually produce.

Clicks are not customers

The ads report will show clicks, impressions, and click-through rate, and none of those numbers is a customer. A campaign can have hundreds of clicks and no calls, and the business will only find out when the invoice arrives and the phone has stayed quiet.

The three numbers that matter are how many real inquiries the ads brought, what each inquiry cost, and how many became booked jobs.

An inquiry is a call from a person who wants the service, or a form from someone who left a real name and number. A wrong number, a job seeker, and a sales call are not inquiries, even though they all arrived through the same ad. Count only the real ones, and the campaign gets honest fast.

Give every call a source

The simplest way to track calls is to give the ads their own phone number — a number that forwards to the business line and shows up in the ads and on the landing page, nowhere else. Every call to that number came from the ads, and every call to the main number did not.

Without that split, the business is guessing, and guessing is how a bad campaign survives for months.

The number needs a routine behind it: somebody answers it, somebody writes down whether the caller was a real customer, and somebody checks the list weekly. A tracking number nobody reviews is just a second phone number. The review takes ten minutes, and it is the difference between knowing the ads work and hoping they do.

Forms need the same treatment

Forms need the same split as calls. The landing page form should carry the source with it — the campaign, the service, the page — so the inbox shows where each inquiry came from without anyone having to ask. A thank-you page that confirms what happens next, and when, keeps the visitor from calling three other businesses while they wait.

The inbox needs the same routine as the phone: somebody reads every form the same day, calls back the same day, and marks whether it became a job. A form that sits unread for two days is a customer lost to the business that answered first, and no amount of ad spend fixes a slow reply.

Cost per inquiry, not cost per click

The math is simple: the month's ad spend divided by the month's real inquiries is the cost per inquiry. That number is the campaign's real price, and it is the number to compare against the value of one booked job.

A campaign that looks expensive per click can be cheap per inquiry, and a campaign that looks cheap per click can be ruinous if none of the clicks become customers.

No cost per inquiry is good or bad on its own. A $50 inquiry is cheap for a roofing job and expensive for a lawn mowing. The business should know its own number — what one job is worth, and what it can afford to pay to win one — before it decides whether the ads are working.

Without that number, every decision is a guess.

The weekly ten-minute review

Once a week, for ten minutes: how many calls and forms came from the ads, how many were real inquiries, what each one cost, and what changed since last week. One page of notes, the same shape every week, so the trend is visible.

A campaign that improves slowly is working; a campaign that stays flat for a month after an honest fix is not.

Change one thing at a time — the page, the keywords, the geography — and give the change a week to show. Changing everything at once teaches nothing, because the business cannot tell which change did the work. And when the numbers say the campaign is not working, stop it.

A stopped campaign is not a failure; it is a decision made on evidence, and the money goes to the page or the service that will earn it back.
