A Dalton Google Ads Landing Page Brief

Google Ads clicks cost money, so a Dalton business should send them to a page built for the advertised service, not to the homepage. Dalton is the Carpet Capital of the World, a working town of mills and warehouses along the I-75 corridor, and a visitor deciding on a phone here has less patience than anywhere else.

One page, one job, one market

Dalton sits in Whitfield County, just off Interstate 75, about an hour north of Atlanta and twenty minutes south of Chattanooga — and most Dalton service businesses work Dalton plus the towns around it: Chatsworth, Ringgold, Calhoun, and the Chattanooga edge.

A landing page that names those towns tells the visitor the business will come. A page that names none of them tells the visitor nothing at all.

The job matters as much as the town. Dalton is a carpet town, home to more than 150 carpet and flooring plants, and a lot of local searches are trade searches — flooring, installation, outlets, wholesale — mixed in with the ordinary searches for plumbers, roofers, and electricians.

One page should serve one of those searchers, not both — the ad names one service, and the page should deliver that one service and nothing else.

The first screen has three jobs

A visitor on a phone decides in seconds, and the first screen has to answer three questions: what the service is, whether the business serves their town, and how to reach a person. The service goes in the headline, the area in the first line, and the phone number where a thumb lands without hunting.

Anything that pushes those three things down the page is costing calls, because a Dalton searcher with three other businesses one tap away will not wait to be convinced.

Proof belongs on the first screen too — but only proof a stranger in Whitfield County can check: a photo of a real job nearby, the towns served in plain words, or a review with a real town attached. A stock photo and a slogan prove nothing.

The first screen makes the promise — the service, the place, the person — and everything below it is the evidence.

Dalton has two searchers: the trade and the town

The trade searcher is looking for flooring: a mill, an installer, an outlet along the I-75 exits, a supplier who answers the phone and knows the product. That searcher wants specifications, stock, and a person who knows carpet — and a page full of generic home-service copy will lose them in seconds.

If the ad is aimed at the trade, the page should talk like the trade: the product, the turnaround, and the number to call.

The town searcher is different: a homeowner in Dalton, or in Chatsworth or Ringgold, who needs a plumber, a roofer, or an electrician this week. That searcher wants to know the business serves their town, when it can come, and what it costs to start.

The page should answer those three things in the order the searcher asks them, and it should not waste the first screen on the company history.

What to leave out of a Dalton landing page

Leave out the other services. A page that advertises carpet installation, plumbing, roofing, and lawn care is a page that advertises nothing, because the visitor came for one of them and now has to sort the list.

Leave out the company history, the mission statement, and the photo gallery — they belong on the About page, not on the page someone paid to reach.

Leave out anything the visitor cannot check. A claim like the best in Dalton is not proof, and a searcher who has seen the same claim on five other sites will not believe it on the sixth.

Replace the claim with something checkable: the towns served, the years in Whitfield County, a real photo of a real job, a review with a real town attached. The page earns trust with specifics, not with adjectives.

Test the page on a phone before you pay for clicks

Try the page yourself before the first ad runs: open it on your own phone, on the connection a visitor has on the I-75 corridor, and do the one job the page exists for.

The test is a minute long: the service, the area, and the phone number should all be reachable inside it. If they are not, fix the page first — the clicks can wait.

After a week of ads, the numbers tell the truth: visitors, calls, forms. If the page converts poorly, the page is the problem — fix it before raising the budget.

Clicks become calls or bounces, and the page is what decides. A business that blames the ads for a bad page is blaming the wrong thing.
