A Dalton Google Ads Landing Page Brief

A Dalton business paying for Google Ads clicks should send those clicks to a page built for the advertised service, not to the homepage. One page, one job, one town

Dalton sits in Whitfield County, just off Interstate 75, about an hour north of Atlanta and twenty minutes south of Chattanooga — and most Dalton service businesses work Dalton plus the towns around it: Chatsworth, Ringgold, Calhoun, and the Chattanooga edge. A landing page that names those towns tells the visitor the business can actually come, and a page that names none of them tells the visitor nothing at all.

The job matters as much as the town. Dalton is a carpet town, home to more than 150 carpet and flooring plants, and a lot of local searches are trade searches — flooring, installation, outlets, wholesale — mixed in with the ordinary searches for plumbers, roofers, and electricians. One page should serve one of those searchers, not both: the ad promises one service, so the page should deliver that service and nothing else.

The first screen answers three questions

A visitor on a phone wants three things before scrolling: what the service is, whether the business serves their town, and how to reach a person. Put the service in the headline, the area in the first line, and the phone number where a thumb lands without hunting. Anything that pushes those three things down the page is costing calls, because a Dalton searcher with three other businesses one tap away will not wait to be convinced.

Proof belongs on that first screen too, but only proof a stranger in Whitfield County can check: a real photo of recent work nearby, a service area named in plain words, or a review from a customer in the same town. A stock photo and a slogan prove nothing. The first screen is a promise — the service, the place, the person — and everything below it is the evidence.

Dalton has two searchers: the trade and the town

The trade searcher is looking for flooring: a mill, an installer, an outlet along the I-75 exits, a supplier who answers the phone and knows the product. That searcher wants specifications, stock, and a person who knows carpet — and a page full of generic home-service copy will lose them in seconds. If the ad is aimed at the trade, the page should talk like the trade: the product, the turnaround, and the number to call.

The town searcher is different: a homeowner in Dalton, or in Chatsworth or Ringgold, who needs a plumber, a roofer, or an electrician this week. That searcher wants to know the business serves their town, when it can come, and what it costs to start. The page should answer those three things in the order the searcher asks them, and it should not waste the first screen on the company history.

What to leave out of a Dalton landing page

Leave out the other services. A page that advertises carpet installation, plumbing, roofing, and lawn care is a page that advertises nothing, because the visitor came for one of them and now has to sort the list. Leave out the company history, the mission statement, and the photo gallery — they belong on the About page, not on the page someone paid to reach.

Leave out anything the visitor cannot check. A claim like the best in Dalton is not proof, and a searcher who has seen the same claim on five other sites will not believe it on the sixth. Replace the claim with something checkable: the towns served, the years in Whitfield County, a real photo of a real job, a review with a real town attached. The page earns trust with specifics, not with adjectives.

Test the page on your own phone before you pay for clicks

Before the first ad runs, open the page on your own phone, on the kind of connection a visitor has on the I-75 corridor, and try to do the one job it exists for. If you cannot reach the service, the area, and the phone number in under a minute, fix the page before you pay for the clicks that land on it.

Test it again after the first week of ads, with the real numbers in front of you: how many visitors, how many calls, how many forms. If the page converts poorly, change the page before you change the budget. A good page turns a click into a call; a bad page turns the same click into a bounce, and the business blames the ads for what the page did.
