A Nashville Analytics Monthly Routine

Nashville is Tennessee's capital city, with 689,447 people in the city itself and a calendar full of events that move the numbers — conventions, concerts, weekends that double the visitors and then vanish. A monthly routine for a Nashville business has to read past all of that. This guide is a routine for a Davidson County business: one day a month, the same order every time, the inquiries first, the events noted next to the numbers, and one page fixed before the next month begins.

Note the events next to the numbers

A Nashville routine starts with the calendar, not the report. Before opening the analytics, write down what happened this month: the conventions, the big weekends, the holidays, the storms. The city's events move the numbers more than any change to the website ever will, and a routine that does not note them will spend its time praising the calendar or blaming it. A month with a convention in it is not a better month for the website; it is a month with a convention in it.

The same goes for the quiet months. A January with no events will look slow next to a June full of them, and the comparison is meaningless unless the events are written down. The record's job is to make each month comparable to the months like it — the same season, the same kind of calendar — so the business can tell whether the website improved or the city just got busier. Note the events first, then open the report.

The inquiry log comes first

Open the business's own books before the analytics: the phone log, the form inbox, the booking system. Tally the month in three separate columns — calls, forms, bookings — because each one means something different. A call is urgent and immediate. A form is deliberate: the writer read the page and still had a question. A booking carries a date and sits closest to a sale. Folded into one number, the three lose the detail that points at the fix.

Read the tally beside the month's events. A convention week will lift the calls; a quiet week will not, and neither movement says anything about the website. The question is whether the inquiries rose after allowing for the calendar — and whether the kind of inquiry changed. A month whose forms doubled while its calls stayed flat is telling the business something different from a month where everything rose together. The event note and the tally belong on the same page, or the tally will be misread.

Channels, counted by inquiry

For every inquiry, note how it arrived: search, a direct visit, a referral from another site, or a paid campaign. The reports will show the same split for visits, but the visits version is the wrong one to read — the channels behave differently, and only the inquiry split shows it. Search may deliver the most visits while direct visits deliver the most bookings. A campaign may deliver clicks that never ring. Judge each channel by the inquiries it produced, not by the traffic it produced.

Events distort the channel split, and the routine has to say so. A big weekend will flood the search channel with people who were already coming to town, and a campaign running that week will look brilliant for reasons that have nothing to do with the campaign. Note which campaigns ran during which events, and compare the channel's inquiries with the same event last year, not with last month. A channel that delivers visits and no inquiries for two ordinary months in a row is the one to fix — not the channel that had one strange week.

Pages that earned, pages that collected

Turn next to the pages the inquiries travelled through. The reports show both where visitors landed and which pages they passed before getting in touch, and the two lists differ: someone may land on the front page and call from a service page, or land on a service page and call from the contact page. The page that carried the step the visitor used is the page that earned the inquiry, and it deserves to be studied and copied.

Event months reveal which pages are event pages and which are business pages. A page that draws crowds during a convention and nothing the rest of the year is serving the event, not the business — and it should not be judged by its quiet months, or praised for its loud ones. The pages to repair are the ones with steady visits and no inquiries in ordinary months: open each as a stranger would, and ask whether the next step is visible, whether the page answers what its title promises, and whether it says which parts of the city the business serves.

One page fixed, then stop

Close the routine by repairing a single page. Not a redesign, not a rewrite of the whole site — one improvement, finished in the time left after the checks. The weakest page is usually obvious: the service page with visits and no inquiries in an ordinary month, the page describing a service the business changed, or the page a customer recently said confused them. One page, one repair, and the site is better at the end of the month than at the start.

Note which page changed and what changed, beside the month's figures and the month's events, so that twelve months from now the business can see twelve pages improved one at a time, without ever running a project. Then stop. The routine is done when the page is fixed and the numbers are written down. The site will be checked again next month, in the same order, and the small improvements will keep adding up while the business gets on with serving Nashville.
