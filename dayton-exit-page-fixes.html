Dayton Exit Pages: Find Them, Fix Them

Every visitor leaves a website from some page, and that page is the exit page. Sometimes the exit is a success — the visitor found the hours and left to drive over. Sometimes it is a failure — the visitor read the service page, found no next step, and left to call someone else. This guide is about telling the two apart, for a Dayton business in the county seat of Rhea County, a town of 7,065 people where a lost caller is a caller the business may not get back.

What an exit page actually says

An exit page is simply the last page a visitor saw before leaving, and on its own it says very little. A visitor who leaves from the contact page may have copied the phone number and be calling now. A visitor who leaves from a service page may have read everything and decided, or read everything and given up. The number of exits from a page is not a verdict; it is a question, and the answer is on the page itself, in what the visitor could and could not do there.

The mistake is treating every exit as a failure and every low exit count as a success. A page nobody visits has no exits and no value. A page that answers a quick question — the hours, the address, a single price signal — will have many exits and may be doing its job perfectly. The routine is to look at the exits from the pages that matter most, the service pages and the booking pages, and to ask of each one: did the visitor have a clear next step here, and did the page give them a reason to take it?

Finding the exits that matter

Sort the pages by exits and look at the top of the list, but read it with the page in mind. A service page at the top of the list deserves attention; a contact page at the top of the list probably does not. The pages that matter are the ones the business wants inquiries from, and an exit from one of those pages without a call or a form is a lost opportunity, however many visits the page received. Write down the three service pages with the most exits, and open each one as a stranger would.

Read each page for the moment the visitor gave up. Was the phone number visible without scrolling, or buried at the bottom? Did the page answer the question its title promised, or did it talk about the business instead? Was the form short, or did it ask for information the visitor did not have yet? The exit number points at the page; the page itself explains the exit. Do not change the page before reading it, because a change made without reading is a guess, and the exit count will not say whether the guess was right.

Fixing the page, one change at a time

Fix one thing per page, and fix the most likely cause first. If the next step was hidden, make it visible: a tap-to-call button at the top, a booking link beside the service description, a short form that asks only for what the business will use. If the page did not answer the visitor's question, add the answer in plain words: the hours, the towns served, what the first visit involves. If the form asked too much, shorten it and say what happens after it is sent.

Then leave the page alone for a month. One change, one month, and the exit count can be read honestly: if the exits from that page fall and the inquiries from it rise, the fix worked. If nothing changes, the cause was something else, and the next most likely fix gets its turn. Changing three things at once feels productive and teaches nothing, because the business cannot tell which change did the work. A Dayton business with a small site can afford this patience — there are only a handful of pages that matter, and each one gets its turn.

When an exit is fine

Some exits are the site working as intended. A visitor who leaves from the hours page has the hours. A visitor who leaves from the directions page is on the way. A visitor who leaves from the contact page after copying the number is about to call. These pages should be kept accurate and easy to read, and otherwise left alone; trying to keep a visitor on a page that has already answered their question only adds clutter to a page that was doing its job.

The distinction is the next step the visitor took after leaving. A visitor who leaves and then calls, books, or visits is a success the analytics may never see, because the action happened off the site. That is why the exit count should always be read next to the inquiry count: if the inquiries are steady and the exits are high on the informational pages, the site is working. If the inquiries are falling and the exits are high on the service pages, the site is leaking. The exit page is a clue, not a verdict, and the inquiry count is what turns the clue into a decision.

The habit, once a month

Make the exit check part of the monthly routine, not a one-time project. Once a month, sort the pages by exits, note the three service pages at the top, and read each one as a stranger would. Fix one thing on one page, write down what was changed and why, and move on. The record matters more than the fix: after a few months it shows which pages kept losing visitors and which fixes actually worked, and that record is worth more than any single month's numbers.

Over time the pattern will settle. The pages that earn inquiries will stay steady, the pages that leak will get fixed one at a time, and the exits that remain will mostly be the good kind — visitors who found what they needed and left to act on it. That is what a small business website in a town of 7,065 people is for: not to hold visitors, but to send them out the door towards the phone, the booking, or the drive over. An exit page that does that is not a failure. It is the site doing its job.
