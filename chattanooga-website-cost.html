How Much Does a Website Cost in Chattanooga?

Ask what a website costs in Chattanooga and the answers range from a few hundred dollars to several thousand. Both can be honest prices — for different scopes. This guide gives the published prices, what moves the price, and how to compare two quotes on the same terms, so a Chattanooga business can judge a quote instead of guessing at it.

A business comparing quotes should compare the website first: which pages, what design, who writes the words, who owns the files. The price is the easy part to compare.

The scope is the part that decides whether the price is fair.

  • Tabb lists $1,500 to $5,000
  • Merchant’s Web Design lists $1,800 to $4,000. Those are their published prices, not WebCraft’s, and they are starting prices and ranges — not quotes for a specific business

A range is not a quote. The business at the bottom of a range is buying the smallest version of the service, and the business at the top is buying the largest — and a quote depends on the scope: how many pages, who writes the words, what the site has to do.

A Chattanooga business should ask what each published price includes before comparing it with another, because two prices for two different scopes are not two prices for the same thing.

What moves the price up or down

Pages move it first: five pages cost less than twenty, and a page that needs writing, photographs and a form costs more than a page that only needs the words the business already has. Content moves it second — who writes the words, and whether the business supplies photographs or the designer has to take them.

Booking, ordering and payment move it third: a form that takes a message is one job, and a system that takes a booking is another.

The two things that move the price most are the ones no sales page lists: who writes the content, and who owns what afterwards. A cheap build that leaves the business without its files, its domain or its login details is not cheap — it is a bill that arrives later, in the month the business tries to leave.

A quote should say, in writing, who owns the domain, the files and the hosting account, before the business signs.

Compare the three-year total, the coverage, and the exit — what happens to the site if the business stops paying — and the right price usually becomes obvious.

How to compare two Chattanooga quotes

Put the quotes side by side with the scope next to the price: which pages, who writes the words, which photographs, which forms, who owns the domain and the files, and what support comes after launch.

Two quotes at the same price can be entirely different scopes, and two quotes at different prices can be the same work with different labels. The quote that lists the work can be judged. The quote that lists only a number cannot.

The questions that catch a bad quote are short: who owns the domain, what happens if we stop working together, what does the monthly price leave out, and how long until launch. A quote that answers all four in writing is a quote the business can trust.

A quote that answers none of them is a quote that will cost more later — usually in the month the business tries to leave and discovers it does not own its own website. Chattanooga has no shortage of builders; the business should choose the one whose quote it can actually read.
