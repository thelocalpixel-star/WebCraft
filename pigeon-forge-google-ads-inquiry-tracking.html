Pigeon Forge Google Ads: Count Inquiries, Not Clicks

Pigeon Forge welcomes about nine million visitors a year, and most of them arrive with a phone in one hand and a plan that changes by the hour. A Google Ads click in that crowd is cheap to buy and easy to misread: the click happened, the customer may not have. The businesses that make ads pay here count three numbers instead of one, and they let the third number decide.

Clicks are not customers in a visitor town

A visitor searching for a place to eat on the Parkway, a show for tonight, or a cabin for the weekend is deciding fast, and their thumb is faster than their wallet. They tap an ad because the photo looked good, because the name sounded familiar, or because they meant to tap the listing underneath it. None of those taps is a booking, and a campaign report that stops at clicks will call all of them a success.

That is why the first number to count is not clicks at all. It is the tap that had intent behind it: the call that lasted long enough to ask a question, the booking form that was started with real dates, the message that named a party size. A Pigeon Forge attraction, restaurant, or cabin business can get hundreds of clicks a week from people who were never going to buy, and the ad platform will happily sell more of them.

The three numbers that matter

Count clicks, then count inquiries, then count the cost of each inquiry. Clicks tell you the ad was seen and the offer was interesting. Inquiries tell you the ad found someone who wanted to act: a call, a completed booking form, a message with a date in it. Cost per inquiry is the number that decides whether the campaign earns its keep, because it is the only one of the three that connects the ad spend to a person the business can serve.

Keep the three numbers in separate columns and look at them weekly, not daily. A single day on the Parkway can swing wildly with weather, a Dollywood festival weekend, or a holiday crowd, and a daily verdict will chase noise. A week smooths the spikes out and shows whether the inquiries are holding steady while the clicks bounce around. When cost per inquiry climbs for two weeks in a row, something real has changed: the offer, the page, or the audience.

What a visitor-town inquiry looks like

A good inquiry from a visitor carries a date. Tonight, this weekend, next month: the date is what separates a browser from a buyer. The booking or contact form should ask for it early, and the phone greeting should ask for it first. A call that ends with a date and a name is an inquiry worth paying for; a call that ends with a price question and a hang-up is information the ad bought, not a customer it found.

Seasonal peaks change what the inquiry is worth. A July weekend inquiry for a dinner show or a cabin is a booking that fills a seat that cannot be sold tomorrow, so it is worth more than the same inquiry in a quiet January week. That does not mean the budget should follow the crowd blindly. It means the business should know, before the peak, what an inquiry is worth in that week, and set the daily cap so the campaign can spend on the days that pay and rest on the days that do not.

Setting up the count before you spend

The count has to exist before the first dollar is spent, or the first month is guesswork. A dedicated phone number on the ad landing page, a booking form that records where the visitor came from, and a simple weekly sheet with the three numbers are enough to start. The setup is not complicated, and it does not need expensive software: it needs a business owner who writes the numbers down every Monday and looks at them before changing anything.

Test the path the way a visitor would. Search the way a tourist searches, tap the ad on a phone, and try to book. If the booking form asks for an email address before it asks for a date, if the phone number is a small link at the bottom of the page, or if the page loads slowly on a Parkway cell connection, the campaign is paying to send people into a dead end. Fix the path first, then judge the ads, because an ad cannot rescue a page that loses the visitor it just bought.

Let cost per inquiry decide

After a month of honest counting, the decision writes itself. If one ad group brings inquiries at a cost the business can afford, keep it and give it more of the budget. If another brings clicks and almost no inquiries, pause it and look at the page it sends people to before blaming the ad. The platform will always offer more clicks; the business should only buy the ones that turn into names, dates, and bookings.

And retest every season. The ad that worked in the summer, when nine million visitors are moving through town, may not work in the quiet weeks, when the audience is locals and planners booking months ahead. The campaign that is reviewed weekly, counted honestly, and judged on cost per inquiry is the one that keeps working when the crowd changes, because it is measured against the customer, not the click.
