Web Design for Johns Creek, GA Businesses

Your website is your office

Customers in Johns Creek find businesses online, not by driving past. An office-park business has no window to dress and no foot traffic to catch — the visitor arrives from a search, on a phone, and decides in seconds whether this is the right business. The website is the reception, the showroom and the phone call all at once, and a site that treats itself as a brochure — a few pages of claims and a contact form buried at the bottom — is an office with the door closed.

The site therefore has to answer three questions in seconds: what the business does, where it works, and how to reach it. Not in the third paragraph, not on a subpage, but in the first screen. A visitor who finds the answer calls. A visitor who has to hunt for it clicks the next result — and the next result is a competitor whose site answered faster.

The first screen has to do the work

The first screen carries the phone number, tappable, and the service in the customer’s words — the words the customer typed to find it, not the trade’s words for it. The area is named plainly: Johns Creek, north Fulton County, metro Atlanta. A business that serves the whole metro should say so, because a customer in a neighbouring suburb who thinks the business only serves Johns Creek will not call.

There should be one clear next step, not five buttons competing for attention: call, or fill in the form. The form asks for three things — the service, the town, and a way to reply — and promises when the answer comes. A form that asks for everything up front is a form the visitor abandons; a form that asks for the minimum and promises a quick reply is a form the visitor finishes.

Proof without a storefront

A business with no walk-in traffic proves itself with real work: photographs of real jobs, words customers actually used, and reviews in their own words. None of it can be invented, bought, or written by the designer — a review the business wrote itself is not a review, and a photograph from a stock library is not proof of anything. One real proof page beats ten pages of claims, because the visitor is not deciding whether the business sounds good. They are deciding whether to trust it.

The honest way to earn reviews is to ask real customers after completed work, in their own words, and to publish what they say — including the imperfect ones. A page of five-star praise with no detail is less convincing than three reviews that name the job, the town, and what went right. A Johns Creek business whose customers never visit an office has to prove itself on the page, and the proof has to be real or it is worth nothing.

What the project looks like from Johns Creek

The project runs remotely: a brief by phone, a design to review online, a launch, and a handoff. No office visit is needed, because the review happens in the browser — where the customer will see it — and the business can ask for changes in plain words, looking at the real pages. A small site goes from brief to launch in days, not months, and the business reviews real pages, not mockups in a meeting room.

The handoff is the part that matters most for a business with no storefront: who can change the site after launch, and how. The domain should be in the business’s name, the files should belong to the business, and at least two people should be able to reach the hosting account. A website the business cannot change is a website the business does not own, however good it looks on launch day.

What it costs, stated plainly

A WebCraft website costs $500 once, and hosting and care together are $20 a month if the business wants them — the hosting, the updates, the backups and someone to call, in one price. Extra pages are $50 each. Those are the published prices, and the quote for a Johns Creek business should be compared on the same terms as any other: what it includes, what it leaves out, and who owns the domain and the files.

The questions that catch a bad quote are short: who owns the domain, what happens if we stop working together, what does the monthly price leave out, and what happens to the site if the designer disappears. A quote that answers all four in writing is a quote the business can trust. A quote that answers none of them is a quote that will cost more later — usually in the month the business tries to leave and discovers it does not own its own website.
