Serving Decatur and DeKalb County

Web design for Decatur businesses, in the seat of DeKalb County.

Decatur is the county seat of DeKalb County, just east of downtown Atlanta, with a walkable downtown around the Decatur Square. The city is served by MARTA rail, and its Square, shops and restaurants draw visitors from across the east side of the metro area.

Decatur businesses serve customers who arrive on foot, by train and by car — and most of them checked the website first, on a phone, before they came. A site that works on the phone, on the Square, is a site that works for the city it serves.

The phone test on the Decatur Square

Decatur is a walking city, and its customers decide on foot and on phones. A customer standing on the Square, choosing between two businesses, opens both sites on a phone and decides in seconds: which one is open, which one is closer, which one answers the phone.

The site that answers those three questions first wins the visit — and the site that makes the customer hunt for them loses it to the business across the street.

The test takes a minute. Open the site on a phone, as a customer would, and find the hours, the phone number, the address and the directions — all of them, before a minute is up, without hunting. Then tap the phone number and see whether it calls.

A site that passes the test is a site that works where its customers actually are: on the Square, on a phone, deciding now. A site that fails it is a brochure, and a brochure does not bring anyone through the door.

The five pages a Decatur site needs first

A Decatur site starts with five pages, and the first is the homepage: the business, its area, and how to reach it. A customer standing on the Square should find the hours and the phone number there in seconds, without hunting through a menu.

Next comes the service page, written the way a customer would say it, and the about page, which says who the business is in plain words. The contact page lists the phone number, a form, and the hours — and the proof page carries a real job and a real review, in the customer’s words.

Five good pages beat twenty pages of filler. Each one answers a single question. Later it can grow — a second service page, a page for the next neighbourhood, a page for the season — once the five are working.

The mistake is to build the twenty pages first and the five good ones never. A visitor who finds the answer on the first page they open is a visitor who calls; a visitor who has to hunt through filler ends up across the street instead.

The $500 website, and the $20-a-month care

The price is $500 once for the website. Hosting and care together are $20 a month, if the business wants them — someone to call, the hosting, the updates and the backups, in one price — and extra pages are $50 each.

A quote is only comparable when it says the same four things: who owns the domain and the files, what is included, what is left out, and what happens when the business wants to leave. If it cannot say those four things, it cannot be compared at all.

Look at what the quote leaves out. A few hundred dollars can buy a site with no hosting, no updates and no handoff — and by the second year that site costs more than the $500 website with $20 a month of care.

Buy the cheapest quote that covers the work nobody in the business will do. For a Decatur business, that work is the updates, the backups, and the phone that gets answered — the jobs that keep the site right between Square weekends.

After launch: the checks that matter in a walking city

The launch is not finished when the site goes live. In the first month, do the checks a walking customer would do — the ones that decide whether a visitor on the Square becomes a customer.

Three checks, in the order a customer on the Square would make them:

  • call the number on the site from a phone, standing on the Square, and see who answers
  • send a test message through the contact form, with a real email address, and check that the reply arrives. A form that thanks you and sends nothing is the failure every launch should catch on day one
  • and check that the hours on the site match the hours on the door, because a customer who walks over to a closed shop does not come back

Then search for the business name. A new site takes time to appear — a few days to a few weeks to be indexed, months to rank — and nobody can promise otherwise.

Keep a note of what was checked and when, with the login details beside it — which hosting account, which registrar, and which email the account is registered under. A launch checked in the first month is one the business can trust; a launch never checked stays a guess.
