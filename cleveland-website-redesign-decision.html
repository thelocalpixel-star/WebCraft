A Cleveland Website Redesign Decision Guide

A Cleveland business owner looking at an aging website faces a real question: rebuild it, or fix the parts that fail? A redesign is the right answer when the structure itself gets in the way — not when a photo is old or a paragraph is out of date. This guide works through the decision in order, so the money goes to the problem that actually costs customers.

Name the customer task that fails

Start by listing the jobs a customer comes to the website to do: find a service, get a sense of price, send an inquiry, find the hours or the phone number. Then test each one on a phone, the way most customers will. Write down which tasks fail and how they fail — a service nobody can find, a form that never sends, a phone number buried at the bottom of the page.

The failing task decides the scope. If every task works and only the photos look dated, the site needs targeted fixes, not a rebuild. If the core tasks fail — customers cannot find what you do or cannot reach you — the structure is the problem, and a redesign is on the table.

Separate a look problem from a structure problem

Dated colours, an old logo treatment or a typeface that feels tired are look problems. They matter, but they are fixed with a refresh: new styling on the same pages, keeping the structure and the content that works. A structure problem is different: navigation that hides the services, pages in the wrong order, a quote form that asks for too much before a customer is ready.

Ask one question to tell them apart: if the site looked new but kept the same pages in the same order, would the failing task work? If yes, it is a look problem. If no, the structure has to change, and that is what a redesign does. Paying for a rebuild to fix a look problem wastes money a refresh would have saved.

Decide what to keep before you rebuild

A redesign is not a bonfire. Before anything is rebuilt, list what already works and should be kept: the service descriptions customers quote back to you, the photos you have permission to use, the pages that already bring inquiries, and the web addresses customers and searchers already use. Those are assets, and a redesign should carry them forward.

For every page that moves or disappears, agree on where it goes. Keep useful addresses where possible and plan redirects when a redesign changes them, so a bookmark or a search result does not land on an error. The cheapest part of a redesign is the list made before it starts; the most expensive part is rebuilding something that was already working.

Price the redesign against what a new site costs

WebCraft’s standard website is $500 one time for up to five pages, with optional hosting and care at $20 a month and extra pages at $50 each. A redesign is quoted on its scope, not a flat figure, because the work depends on how much is kept and how much is rebuilt. Compare the two on the same basis: what pages, what content, what the customer can do when it launches.

A redesign that keeps most of the content and changes the structure should cost less than starting over; a redesign that replaces everything is a new website by another name and should be priced that way. Get the scope in writing before work starts — pages, content, redirects and the launch check — so the price and the work match.

Test the new site against the old tasks

Before the redesigned site goes live, repeat the tests from the first section. Open it on a phone, find each service, send a test inquiry and confirm it reaches the inbox you monitor. Check that the addresses you kept still work and that the ones you moved redirect. A redesign that fails the same task as the old site is not finished, however good it looks.

Give the test to someone who did not build the site. A fresh pair of eyes finds the confusing step the builder stopped seeing weeks ago. When every task passes, launch — and keep the list of tasks, because it is also the checklist for the next time the site needs attention.
