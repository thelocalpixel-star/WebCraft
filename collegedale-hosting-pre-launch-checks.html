Pre-Launch Checks for a Collegedale Site

A website launch is a quiet event — nobody applauds, and nobody tells the business what is broken. The first visitors find the problems, and they find them by trying the five things every visitor tries first: the phone number, the form, the hours, the links, and the site on a phone. A Collegedale business about to go live should check those five things itself, before the customers do, because a launch-day fix takes ten minutes and a customer-found fix takes a customer.

Check one: the phone number, tapped not read

Open the new site on a phone and tap the number. Not read it — tap it. If the call starts, the check passes. If the number is plain text that has to be copied by hand, the check fails, and the fix is to make the number a link. A visitor comparing two Collegedale businesses will call the one whose number works with a thumb, and will not copy a number by hand when the next result is one tap away.

Check the number on every page, not just the homepage. A visitor who lands on a service page from a search should find the number there too, without going back to the homepage. Every page is a front door for someone who found it through search, and every front door needs a number on it. While checking, call the number yourself: if it rings an unanswered phone, or a voicemail nobody checks, the website is bringing calls the business is not taking.

Check two: the form, filled in and answered

Fill in the contact form as a customer would, with a real email address the business can reply to, and send it. Then watch for the reply — not just the thank-you page, but the message in the inbox. A form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common launch-day failure, and the business only discovers it when a customer mentions a message that never arrived. The test takes two minutes and proves the whole chain works: the form, the sending, and the inbox it lands in.

While testing, read the form the way a customer reads it. Does it ask for the service, the town, and a way to reply — three fields, no more? Does it say when the business will answer? A form that asks for a life story is a form a customer abandons, and a form that says nothing about reply time leaves the customer guessing. Three fields, one promise about when the answer comes, and a test message that actually arrives.

Check three: the hours say what is true today

Read the hours on the new site and ask whether they are true today — not in general, not last summer, but today. A Collegedale business that keeps different hours in different seasons, or closes early on a particular day, needs a website that says which season it is in. “Open daily” is not an answer when the business closes early on Sunday. The hours on the homepage should be the hours for this week, and the business should know how to change them when the week changes.

The same check covers closures. A day closed for a holiday, a private booking, or a storm should be on the website before the visitor drives over, not discovered at a locked door. One honest line — “Closed Monday for a private event” — saves a trip and keeps the customer for the next visit. A launch is the right time to set this habit, because the hours checked once are the hours the business will remember to check again.

Check four: every link goes somewhere real

Click every link on the new site, including the ones in the footer and the ones nobody thinks about — the social links, the service links, the “learn more” links. A link that goes nowhere, or to a page that no longer exists, is a small failure that tells a visitor the site is not looked after. A new site should have no dead links at all, because every link was put there on purpose, days ago, and there has been no time for them to rot.

The check also covers the links that leave the site. A Facebook link that points at the wrong page, a map link that points at the wrong town, a directory link that points at a business that moved — each one is a visitor sent to the wrong place by the business’s own website. Fix them before launch, and put the link check on the quarterly list, because links are the part of a website that breaks quietly, one at a time, without anyone noticing.

Check five: the whole site, on a phone, in sunlight

The final check is the whole site, on a phone, outside, in sunlight, with one hand. Can you read the text without zooming. Can you tap the number and the form button without hitting the wrong thing. Does the site load fast enough that you do not give up waiting. A site that passes at a desk and fails on a phone fails where most visitors actually are — and a Collegedale customer standing in a car park deciding whether to call is not going to wait for a slow page to load.

This check is also the check for the hosting itself. A site that loads slowly on a phone is often a site on hosting that cannot keep up, or a site carrying images far larger than a phone needs. Before launch, the images should be sized for the web, the pages should load in a couple of seconds, and the business should know what its hosting covers if the site slows down later. Launch day is the cheapest day to find out — every day after it, the audience is real.
