Pre-Launch Checks for a Maryville Site

A website should be checked before launch by someone who did not build it. The builder knows what the site is supposed to say, and reads what they meant instead of what is actually there. A fresh pair of eyes finds the wrong number, the broken form, the page that only makes sense to its author, and the link that goes nowhere. A Maryville business about to launch should hand the site to one honest tester, give them five jobs, and fix what they find — because a launch-day fix takes ten minutes and a customer-found fix takes a customer.

Why the builder cannot be the tester

The person who built a website cannot test it, because they know what it is supposed to do. They click the link they meant to put there, read the number they meant to type, and fill in the form the way they designed it to be filled in. A visitor does none of those things. A visitor clicks the link that is actually there, reads the number that is actually typed, and fills in the form the way a customer would — which is often not the way the designer expected.

The tester should therefore be someone who has never seen the site: a friend, an employee who did not work on it, a customer the business trusts. Give them the address, no instructions, and five jobs. Watch what they do, not what they say — where they hesitate, where they tap the wrong thing, where they give up. The hesitation is the finding. A site that needs instructions is a site that is not ready, and the tester’s confusion is the most honest review the site will ever get.

Job one: call the business, as a customer would

The tester’s first job is to find the phone number and call it — on a phone, from the site, without being told where the number is. If they find it in seconds and the call starts, the check passes. If they have to hunt for it, or the number is plain text that has to be copied, the check fails. While they are on the call, the business should answer the way it answers a real customer: who picks up, what do they say, and how long does it take to get an answer to a simple question.

The call is also a test of the business, not just the site. A website that brings calls the business does not answer is a website working for the competitor. The tester should note what happened when they called — answered in person, voicemail, no answer — and the business should decide whether that is the experience it wants its first customers to have. Launch day is the cheapest day to find out; every day after it, the callers are real.

Job two: fill in the form, and wait for the answer

The tester’s second job is to fill in the contact form with their real email address and a real question, and send it. Then the business watches: does the message arrive, how fast, and does the reply answer the question or ask for the information the form already collected. A form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common launch-day failure, and only a real test message finds it — the builder already knows the form is supposed to work, and never thinks to check whether it does.

The tester should also say whether the form was easy to finish. Did it ask for the service, the town, and a way to reply — three fields — or did it ask for a life story. Did it say when the business would answer. A form that asks too much is a form a customer abandons, and a form that says nothing about reply time leaves the customer guessing. Three fields, one promise about when the answer comes, and a test message that actually arrives — that is the whole check.

Job three: read every word out loud

The tester’s third job is to read the site out loud, every page, from the top. Reading out loud finds the mistakes silent reading misses: the sentence that stops halfway, the word repeated twice, the price that contradicts the price on the next page, the town name spelled two ways. It also finds the sentences that only make sense to the business — the jargon, the claim nobody can check, the paragraph that says nothing in fifty words.

Reading out loud also tests the site’s honesty. Does it claim a service the business does not offer, a town it does not serve, a result it cannot promise. A Maryville site should name Maryville and the places it genuinely serves, and stop there. A page that claims everywhere brings enquiries the business cannot take, and the tester reading it out loud is the first person to notice — because they are the first person to read it as a customer would, rather than as its author did.

Job four and five: the phone test, and the stranger test

The fourth job is the phone test: the whole site, on a phone, outside, in sunlight, with one hand. Can the tester read the text without zooming. Can they tap the number and the form button without hitting the wrong thing. Does the site load fast enough that they do not give up waiting. A site that passes at a desk and fails on a phone fails where most visitors actually are — and a customer standing in a car park deciding whether to call is not going to wait for a slow page.

The fifth job is the stranger test: after five minutes, ask the tester what the business does, where it is, and how to get in touch. If they can answer all three without looking, the site has done its job. If they cannot, the site has not — however good it looks. Those three answers are the whole purpose of a small business website, and a launch that cannot pass the stranger test is a launch that should wait a day while the site is fixed.
