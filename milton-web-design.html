The questions to ask before hiring

Three questions matter before any money moves. What is the scope — which pages, and what does the price include. Who owns what — the domain name, the website files, the hosting account. And what happens after launch — who can change the site, and how. The answers should be written down, in plain words, before the work starts, because a scope agreed in a phone call and forgotten by launch day is a scope that never existed.

The warning signs are the same in Milton as anywhere else: no written scope, the designer holding the domain in their own name, no handoff for the business to change its own site, and a price that cannot be explained in one sentence. A designer who cannot answer the three questions plainly is a designer to skip — and a designer who answers them in writing, before being asked, is a designer worth talking to.

The five pages a Milton site needs first

Start a Milton site with five pages and nothing else. The homepage, which says what the business does, where in Milton it works, and how to reach it — a visitor arriving from an Atlanta search should know in seconds that this is a north Fulton business, not a downtown one. A service page, in the customer’s words rather than the trade’s. An about page, saying in plain words who the business is. A contact page, carrying the phone number, the form, and the hours. And one proof page — a real job, a real review in the customer’s own words — because an unproven claim gives the visitor no reason to believe it.

Five strong pages beat twenty thin ones. Every page answers one question, and the site can grow from there — another service page, a page for a nearby town, a page for the season. The mistake is building the twenty first and never getting to the five that matter: a visitor who finds the answer on the first page they open picks up the phone, and a visitor who has to dig through filler clicks back to the results and calls the next business instead.

Judge a quote by what it covers, not by the first number. A quote of a few hundred dollars can leave out the hosting, the updates and the handoff, and by the second year it can cost more than the $500 website with $20 a month of care. The right quote is usually the cheapest one that covers the jobs nobody in the business will do — and a quote that cannot say what it covers cannot be compared at all.

The first week after launch: what to check

The site going live is not the finish. In the first week, ring the number on the site from a phone and see who answers. Send the contact form a real message from a real email address and check the reply arrives — a form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common launch-day failure, and only a genuine test message catches it. Look at the hours, and make sure the map listing points at the right place.

Then look the business up by name and open the first page of results. A new site needs time to show up in search — days to weeks before it is indexed, months before it ranks — and nobody can promise otherwise. Note what was checked and when, and keep the login details beside it: the hosting account, the domain registrar, and the email the account sits under. A launch that was checked in its first week is a launch the business can rely on. One that was never checked is a guess.
