The questions to ask before hiring

Three questions matter before any money moves. What is the scope — which pages, and what does the price include. Who owns what — the domain name, the website files, the hosting account. And what happens after launch — who can change the site, and how. The answers should be written down, in plain words, before the work starts, because a scope agreed in a phone call and forgotten by launch day is a scope that never existed.

The warning signs are the same in Milton as anywhere else: no written scope, the designer holding the domain in their own name, no handoff for the business to change its own site, and a price that cannot be explained in one sentence. A designer who cannot answer the three questions plainly is a designer to skip — and a designer who answers them in writing, before being asked, is a designer worth talking to.

The five pages a Milton site needs first

A small business website needs five pages before anything else. The homepage, which says what the business does, where it works, and how to reach it. A service page, which describes the service in the customer’s words, not the trade’s. An about page, which says who the business is, in plain words. A contact page, with a phone number, a form, and the hours. And one proof page — a real job, a real review in the customer’s own words — because a claim without proof is a claim the visitor has no reason to believe.

Five pages done well beat twenty pages of filler. Each page answers one question, and the site can grow later — a second service page, a page for a new town, a page for the season. The mistake is to build the twenty pages first and the five good ones never: a visitor who finds the answer on the first page they open is a visitor who calls, and a visitor who has to hunt through filler is a visitor who clicks the next result.

The first week after launch: what to check

The launch is not finished when the site goes live. In the first week, call the number on the site from a phone and see who answers. Fill in the contact form with a real email address and confirm the reply arrives — a form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common launch-day failure, and only a real test message finds it. Check the hours, and check that the map listing points at the right place.

Then search for the business name and open the first page of results. A new site takes time to appear in search — a few days to a few weeks to be indexed, and months to rank — and no one can promise otherwise. Write down what was checked and when, and keep the login details with it: the hosting account, the domain registrar, and the email the account is registered under. A launch checked in the first week is a launch the business can trust. A launch never checked is a guess.
