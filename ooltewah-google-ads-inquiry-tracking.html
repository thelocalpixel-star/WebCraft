Tracking Inquiries from Ooltewah Google Ads

An Ooltewah business running Google Ads gets a report full of clicks — and the clicks are not customers. This guide covers tracking the inquiries the ads actually bring: the calls, the forms, and the cost of each one, so the business can see which ads earn their keep and which earn nothing.

Clicks are not customers

The ad platform counts clicks. The business counts calls and form submissions. The gap between the two is where money is wasted: a campaign can bring a hundred clicks and no customers, and the report will still call it a success, because the platform measures what it sells.

A business that judges ads on clicks is judging them on the platform’s measure, not its own — and the platform’s measure is not the one that pays the bills.

The fix is to track the inquiry, not the click. Every ad, every landing page, every month: how many calls came from it, how many forms were submitted, and what each inquiry cost. A campaign that brings ten inquiries at a cost the business can afford is a campaign that works.

A campaign that brings a hundred clicks and no inquiries is a campaign that spent money on nothing — and the business should know that in the first week, not the third month.

The three things to track

Calls, form submissions, and direction requests or bookings. One number per source, every month: how many calls the ads brought, how many forms were submitted from the ad’s landing page, and how many bookings or direction requests followed.

Three numbers, and the business can see which ads earn their keep and which earn nothing — and which landing page converts and which one wastes the clicks it gets.

The numbers should be kept in one place, in plain words, where the business can read them without a dashboard course: the ad, the month, the calls, the forms, the cost. A report the business cannot read is a report the business will not use, and a report it will not use is a report it should not pay for.

The tracking that matters is the tracking the business actually looks at.

Setting it up without a developer

Three pieces, none of them needing a developer. A call tracking number — a number that forwards to the business’s real number, so calls from the ad can be counted separately. A form thank-you page — a page the visitor only reaches after submitting, so submissions can be counted.

And the habit of naming the source: every ad, every landing page, labelled so the business knows which one brought the inquiry.

One tension to state honestly: the tracking number on the ad is not the business’s real number, and the real number should stay on the website, the Business Profile and every listing — because a business with different numbers in different places confuses customers and search engines alike. The tracking number belongs on the ad, where it can be counted.

The real number belongs everywhere else, where it can be found.

Reading the report: what to keep, what to pause

The number that matters is cost per inquiry, not cost per click. An ad that costs $5 a click and brings one inquiry in ten clicks costs $50 an inquiry. An ad that costs $2 a click and brings one inquiry in fifty clicks costs $100 an inquiry — and the cheaper click is the more expensive ad.

The business should ask what each inquiry cost, and keep the ads whose inquiries it can afford, and pause the ones whose inquiries it cannot.

Small numbers need patience: a week of data is not a verdict, and a few inquiries either way can look like a trend and mean nothing. Give a campaign a month, change one thing at a time, and ask whether the change brought more inquiries or fewer.

The ad that earns nothing for a month, at a cost the business can see, gets paused — not because ads do not work, but because that ad, for that service, in that month, did not.

The monthly routine

Thirty minutes, once a month: the inquiries by source, the ad that earned nothing, the page that converted, and one change. Write the numbers down — the ad, the month, the calls, the forms, the cost — and keep them, because a year of monthly numbers is the record that decides next year’s budget.

A business that tracks its inquiries knows which ads to keep, which to pause, and what to spend next month.

The routine also catches the slow drift nobody notices: the ad that stopped bringing calls three weeks ago, the landing page whose form broke after an update, the budget that ran out on the 20th and left the last ten days of the month silent.

Thirty minutes, once a month, is the cheapest insurance a business running ads has — and the businesses that do it never again pay for a month of clicks that brought nothing.
