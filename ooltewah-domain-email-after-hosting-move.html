Ooltewah Domain and Email After a Move

Moving a website to new hosting breaks email more often than it breaks the website. The site moves in an afternoon; the email stops arriving, and nobody notices for a day or two, because the website still looks fine. An Ooltewah business planning a move should treat the domain and the email as the move itself, and the website as the easy part. This guide covers the records to copy, the order to move in, and the checks that catch the break before customers do.

The most common disaster is email going dark

When a business moves hosting, the website is the part everyone watches, and the email is the part everyone forgets. The domain name points to the new host, the site appears, and the move looks done — while the mail records still point at the old host, or were never copied at all, and new messages quietly vanish. Customers write, hear nothing back, and call a competitor. The business finds out days later, when someone mentions an email that never arrived.

The reason is simple: email is controlled by records attached to the domain name, not by the website. Moving the website files does not move those records unless someone moves them deliberately. A business that uses Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 for email has mail records that must be copied exactly to the new settings; a business whose email lives with the old host must decide where the mailboxes go before the move, not after. Either way, the mail records are a line on the checklist, not an afterthought.

Know what you are moving: host or platform

There are two kinds of move, and they carry different risks. A host move keeps the same website on the same platform and only changes where it lives — the pages, the addresses and the content stay exactly the same, and the risk is mostly email and the small things the old server was doing, like sending form messages. A platform move rebuilds the site somewhere else — a site builder to WordPress, for example — and the web addresses usually change, which is where search rankings get lost without redirects.

A business should know which move it is making before it starts, because the checklist is different. A host move needs the mail records copied, the site tested on its new address, and the old hosting kept running for a week. A platform move needs all of that plus a redirect for every old address, and time for Google to re-crawl. Most Ooltewah small businesses are making a host move, and a host move done in the right order is a quiet afternoon, not a crisis.

Copy the mail records before anything else

Before anything is moved, someone should write down the domain’s current records: the mail records that say where email goes, and the text records that prove the domain is allowed to send mail. Those records live with whoever controls the domain’s settings — the registrar, or the old host if it holds the domain — and they must be copied exactly to the new settings. A single mistyped character sends email nowhere, and the error gives no warning.

The records should be copied before the website moves, not during it, and checked by sending a test message to every address the business uses — the main address, the one on the website, the one customers actually write to. A test message that arrives proves the records are right. A test message that does not arrive is a problem found in minutes, while the old setup is still running and the fix is easy. After the switch, the same test is a problem found in days, when the fix is already late.

Build, test, then switch — in that order

The move itself has a safe order, and it is worth following exactly. First, copy the files and the content to the new host and build the site there completely. Second, test the copy on a temporary address: every page loads, the form sends, the phone number is right, and the email still arrives. Third, and only then, change the domain to point at the new host. The switch is the last step, not the first, because everything that can be tested should be tested before customers see it.

Two details make the switch smoother. Lower the domain’s TTL — the setting that controls how long the internet remembers the old address — a day or two before the move, so the change spreads in minutes rather than hours. And do not launch a redesign at the same time as the move: if something drops afterwards, the business will not know which change caused it, and will spend weeks guessing. One change at a time, tested before it goes live, is the whole method.

The week after: what to watch

Keep the old hosting running for at least a week after the switch. Some visitors and some mail servers will still hold the old address in their memory, and the old server quietly catching them is what keeps the move invisible. Cancelling the old hosting on the day of the switch is the one mistake that turns a smooth move into a week of lost messages.

In that week, watch four things: the website loads from a phone on mobile data, not just office wifi; the contact form sends and the reply arrives; email arrives at every address; and the search console shows no sudden errors. A move on the same domain with the same addresses and the same content is invisible to Google — rankings drop when addresses change without redirects, or when a staging copy left behind a setting that blocks search engines. Check those two, and the move is done.
