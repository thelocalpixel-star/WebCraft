A Five-Page Website Plan for Murphy, NC

Five pages are enough for a Murphy business — if each one carries real local detail. A small site does not need twenty pages; it needs five pages that each do a job, written for Murphy and the towns around it, not copied from a template. This guide covers which five pages, and what each one must say.

Five pages are enough when each one works

A five-page website has one job per page, and that is why it works. Home says what the business does and where. Services says what the business sells, one page per service. About says who is behind it. Contact says how to reach it, and when. A visitor can find any of those in one tap, and a business can keep all five current without a full-time job.

The mistake is to add pages that do no work. A blog with two posts from last year, a gallery with stock photos, a testimonials page with no testimonials — those pages do not help a customer decide, and they make the site look abandoned. Five working pages beat fifteen half-finished ones, every time.

Home: say Murphy in the first line

The homepage should say Murphy in the first line, because the visitor needs to know they have found a business that serves Murphy. “Fence repair in Murphy, NC” is a homepage. “Welcome to our website” is not one. The first line should also name the service and the next step: what the business does, where it does it, and how to call.

The rest of the homepage is a short tour: the services, in one line each, with links to their pages; one proof paragraph — what the work includes, how long it takes, what it costs to find out; and the phone number, visible without scrolling. A homepage that does that in one screen has done its job. Everything else is detail that belongs on the other four pages.

Services: one page per service you actually sell

The services section is usually two or three pages, one per service the business actually sells. Each page names the service, says who it is for, says where it is done, and ends with one next step. A page for a service the business rarely does is a page that will bring enquiries it cannot fulfil — better to leave it out and let the phone do the explaining.

Each service page should also carry one detail only a Murphy business would know. The roads, the weather, the distances, the kind of houses — the specifics that show the page was written by someone who works here. Generic copy could belong to any town; a page with one real local detail belongs to Murphy, and a Murphy customer can tell the difference.

About and contact: the proof pages

The about page is not a company history. It is proof: who runs the business, how long they have done the work, and what they do differently. One honest paragraph is enough. A photo of the actual work, or the actual person, is worth more than a paragraph about values. Customers do business with people, and the about page is where they meet them.

The contact page is the most practical page on the site. The phone number, tap-to-call on a phone. The towns served, named honestly. The hours, kept current. And a form with the few fields that matter — name, contact, what the work is, where it is. A contact page that asks for twelve things will be abandoned; a contact page that asks for four will be used.

What to leave out

A five-page plan also means leaving things out. Leave out the blog unless someone will write in it every month. Leave out the gallery unless the photos are the business’s own work. Leave out the extra services, the extra towns, and the extra pages that exist to make the site look bigger than the business. A small business does not need to look big; it needs to look reachable.

The test for every page is the same: would a customer miss it if it were gone. If the answer is no, the page does not belong on the site. Five pages that would each be missed are five pages that earn their keep — and a site that small is a site the business can keep current, which is the real test of a website plan.
