Sevierville Exit Pages: Find Them, Fix Them

Sevierville sits at the western end of the Parkway corridor, in the seat of Sevier County, and its business websites are read by people who are deciding fast — visitors choosing between Pigeon Forge, Gatlinburg, Seymour and Kodak, often on a phone, often on the move. On a corridor like that, an exit is not a slow leak. It is a decision made in seconds, and the visitor who leaves a service page without calling is usually gone for good. This guide is about finding the pages where those decisions go wrong, and repairing the step the visitor could not find.

A decision, not a leak

On the Parkway, a visitor decides in seconds. The page loads, the eye scans, the finger hovers — and the visitor either stays or goes. That is not a leak. It is a decision, made by a person in a car, or in a hotel room, or on a porch, with a question in mind. The job of the exit check is to find the pages where the decision goes the wrong way, and to fix the reason.

Start with the pages that should be earning inquiries. The service pages, the booking page, the page that describes the work. Rank them by how often visitors leave, and read the three worst as a stranger would. Is the next step visible? Does the page answer the question its title promises? Does it say which towns the business serves? A page that fails those three tests is not a mystery. It is a page to repair.

Where the corridor's visitors go

Corridor visitors are not one crowd. Some are passing through on the way to the Smokies. Some are staying the week and looking for a service. Some live in Sevierville and need a local business. The reports cannot tell these apart by themselves — but the pages they leave from can. A visitor who leaves from the hours page has what they need. A visitor who leaves from a service page without calling has not found it.

So read the exits page by page, not as a single number. The pages that answer a question — hours, directions, contact — will always have high exits, and that is fine. The pages that should be starting a conversation — services, booking, the work itself — should not. The split between those two groups is the whole of the exercise. Everything else is detail.

The pitch the page makes

Every service page makes a pitch, whether it means to or not. Three questions, in the visitor's words: what is this, is it for me, and what do I do next? Miss the first, and the visitor never reads the second. Miss the second, and the visitor leaves for a page that speaks to them. Miss the third, and the visitor agrees with everything and does nothing.

Fix the third question first. The next step should be visible without scrolling, and it should say what happens after it is taken: call, and someone answers; book, and a time is confirmed; write, and a reply comes within a day. Then fix the second: the answer the visitor came for — the hours, the towns served, what a first visit involves — in plain words, at the top of the page. One repair per page. Then leave it alone for a month, and read the numbers again.

Exits that are not failures

Three kinds of exit are good news. The visitor who leaves from the hours page now knows the hours. The visitor who leaves from the directions page is on the way. The visitor who leaves from the contact page after copying the number is about to call. Those pages need to stay accurate and easy to read. Nothing more. Trying to hold a visitor on a page that has already answered their question only clutters a page that was working.

The way to tell a good exit from a bad one is the inquiry count. Read it beside the exit share for the same month. Inquiries steady, exits high on the informational pages: the site is working. Inquiries falling, exits high on the service pages: the site is leaking. The exit share on its own is a clue. The inquiry count is what turns the clue into a decision — and the decision is usually one page, repaired, not a redesign.

Checking the corridor monthly

Put the check on the calendar, once a month. Rank the service pages by exit share. Note the three at the top. Read each one as a stranger would, on a phone, in a hurry. Repair one thing. Write down what changed, and why. That is the whole routine — and the writing down matters as much as the repair, because after a few months the notes show which pages kept losing visitors and which repairs actually worked.

Given time, the pattern settles. The pages that earn inquiries hold steady. The pages that leak get repaired one at a time. And the exits that remain are mostly the good kind: visitors who found what they needed and left to act on it. That is what a corridor website is for. Not to hold visitors. To send them towards the phone, the booking, or the drive down the Parkway.
