Tracking Google Ads Inquiries in Crossville

A Crossville business running Google Ads without inquiry tracking is paying for clicks and guessing at customers. Three things to count — calls, forms and bookings — and the cost of each one is the whole report.

By WebCraft · Published November 17, 2026 · Google Ads guide

Clicks are not customers

A click is a visit. An inquiry is a call, a form submission or a booking. The two are not the same, and a campaign can produce many of the first and none of the second. Crossville businesses serve a mixed market — year-round residents, the Fairfield Glade community, and I-40 travelers passing across the Cumberland Plateau — and each group clicks for different reasons. Only the inquiries tell the business which clicks were customers.

The confusion is expensive because clicks feel like progress. A dashboard full of clicks looks like a campaign that works, right up until the phone stays quiet and the month ends. The business that counts clicks is measuring the ads. The business that counts inquiries is measuring its own results, and those are the only results that pay.

Count three things, ignore the rest

Count calls from the ad, form submissions that name the service, and bookings that came through either one. Those three are the report. Impressions, click-through rate, likes and shares are not measures of a campaign that exists to bring customers — they are background noise, and a monthly report built on them is a report about the advertising, not about the business.

Keep the count simple enough to do every month without a specialist. A notebook, a spreadsheet or a note on the calendar is enough: the date, the channel, and whether the person became a customer. The tracking that gets done beats the tracking that was planned and never started.

Separate ad calls from every other call

An ad inquiry is only useful if the business knows it came from the ad. The simplest method is a phone number that appears only on the ad landing page, so every call to it is an ad call. The form should do the same job: a field that records where the visitor came from, or a landing page whose form submissions are counted separately from the rest of the site.

Compare the ad inquiries with the organic ones side by side, in the same month, on the same page. If the ads bring ten inquiries and the organic pages bring two, the ads are doing the work — and the business should know that before it decides whether to keep paying. If the numbers are reversed, the pages are doing the work and the ads are buying what the business already earns for free.

Cost per inquiry is the number that decides

Divide the month’s ad spend by the month’s real inquiries. That number — the cost of one inquiry — is the figure that decides whether a campaign continues, changes or stops. A campaign whose cost per inquiry is higher than the job is worth has answered itself, and no amount of clicks can rescue it. A campaign whose cost per inquiry is low is a campaign worth growing slowly.

There is no published benchmark worth borrowing for this number, because every service, town and margin is different. The comparison that matters is against the business’s own jobs: what a booked job is worth, and what the business can afford to pay to win one. Compare this month with last month, and this service with that one — not with a number from a blog post about another industry.

The monthly routine that keeps it honest

Once a month, on the same day, write down the same three numbers: inquiries from ads, inquiries from the rest of the site, and cost per ad inquiry. Change one thing at a time — the page, the keywords, the geography, the budget — and note what changed, so a good month and a bad month can be compared instead of guessed about.

The routine is the difference between a campaign and a habit. A campaign is measured and adjusted; a habit is paid for and forgotten. Thirty minutes a month is enough, and the month it is skipped is the month the business starts paying for clicks again instead of customers.
