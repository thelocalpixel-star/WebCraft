Citation Building and Cleanup for Local Search

A citation is your business name, address and phone number appearing on a site that is not your own — a directory, a chamber listing, a review site, a trade association page. Google reads those mentions the way a person reads references: several places agreeing about the same business makes the business look real, and several places disagreeing makes it look uncertain. This guide covers which citations are worth building, which ones are worth fixing, and which ones are worth ignoring entirely.

What a citation is, and what it is not

A citation is not a link, and it is not an advertisement. It is a mention: the business name, address and phone number, written down somewhere other than the business’s own website. Google uses those mentions to check that the business is what its website says it is, and that it is where its website says it is. A business with the same details in the same form in several places it controls is easy for Google to trust. A business whose details disagree from place to place is not.

What a citation is not is a ranking trick. A hundred listings on directories nobody has heard of will not move a business up the results, because Google does not count mentions the way it counts recommendations. The value of a citation is accuracy and relevance: the right details, in the right places, where customers actually look. Everything else is noise, and much of the citation industry sells noise by the hundred.

The handful that matter

For most small businesses, the citations that matter fit on one hand. The Google Business Profile, if the business is eligible for one. The business’s Facebook page. The chamber of commerce, if the business is genuinely a member. The trade or professional association the business actually belongs to. And one or two directories that customers in that trade really use — the kind a customer would name if asked where they found the business. That is the list. It is short on purpose.

Each one earns its place by being somewhere a customer might actually look, or somewhere Google actually checks. A chamber listing matters because a chamber is a real organisation with real members, and membership is a fact a customer can verify. A trade directory matters when customers in that trade use it to choose. A general directory nobody visits matters to nobody, however many of them a service offers to create. The test for any citation is simple: would a customer ever find the business through it. If not, it is not worth building.

Finding what is already out there

Before building anything new, a business should find out what already exists. Search for the business name and phone number, and open the first page of results. The listings that appear are the ones customers actually find, and they are the ones that matter — including the ones the business never created. Directories scrape details from each other, so a business often has listings it never asked for, with details it never supplied, including old phone numbers and old addresses.

Make a list of what the search turns up, with the details each listing shows. Do not try to list every directory on the internet; list the ones on the first page of a search for the business name, and the ones that appear when searching the business phone number. Those are the listings with any chance of reaching a customer. A listing on page six of a search nobody runs is a listing that can wait, or be ignored for good.

Fixing what is wrong, in the right order

Fix the listings in the order customers find them, not in alphabetical order. The business’s own website comes first, because it is the source every other listing should match. Then the Business Profile, if there is one, and the Facebook page. Then the chamber and trade listings, and the directories that appear on the first page of a search for the business name. Each fix should make the listing match the website exactly — the same name, the same address, the same phone number, written the same way.

Do not pay a service to fix listings the business never created and nobody visits. The money is better spent on the handful of listings that bring customers, or on the website itself. When the places a business controls all agree, the places it does not control matter far less, because Google and customers both start with the sources the business stands behind. A cleanup that fixes five real listings beats a cleanup that touches a hundred invisible ones.

What never to buy, and the monthly check

Never buy a citation package that promises hundreds of listings, and never claim a membership that does not exist. A chamber listing for a business that is not a chamber member is a false claim, and a trade listing for a business that is not in the trade is worse than no listing at all. Citations built on inventions fall apart the moment anyone checks them, and the business that built them has spent money to look less trustworthy, not more.

The monthly check takes ten minutes. Search for the business name and phone number, open the first five results, and compare the three lines — name, address, phone — against the website. If something has changed, fix the listing, and write the change down. Details change: a phone number moves, a suite number is added, a business changes its name. A citation that was right last year and wrong this year is worse than no citation at all, because it sends customers to a number that no longer answers.
