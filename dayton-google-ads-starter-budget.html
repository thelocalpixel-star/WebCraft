A Dayton Google Ads Starter Budget, Built on Principles

Starter-budget principles for Dayton businesses: the job the ad must do, a small set of searches, a ready landing page, and cost per inquiry as the measure.

By WebCraft · Published November 4, 2026 · Google Ads guide

Start with the job the ad must do

A starter budget should begin with a sentence, not a number. What do you want the ad to produce: a phone call, a completed contact form, or a booking request? An ad that is measured against a vague goal like more traffic will always look successful, because traffic is easy to buy. An ad measured against inquiries is harder to flatter, and that is the point. Write the goal down before you set any budget, and judge the campaign against it afterwards.

The budget then follows from a limit you choose, not from a figure someone else spent. Decide the total amount you are willing to spend in a month on this experiment, and treat that as the ceiling. A starter budget is a test with a defined cost, not a commitment to keep spending. If the test produces inquiries at a cost you can accept, you have something to scale. If it does not, you have learned something at a known price.

Choose a small set of searches, not every keyword

New advertisers often add every keyword that seems related to their business, which spreads a small budget so thin that nothing gets enough exposure to learn from. A starter campaign works better with a short list of searches that match the service you actually want inquiries about, phrased the way a customer would phrase them. If you offer one service in particular, start there rather than advertising everything at once.

Match the ad to the search. An ad for a specific service should lead to a page about that service, not to a homepage where the visitor must hunt for it. This alignment is the cheapest improvement available to a new campaign: it costs nothing in extra spend and it raises the chance that a click becomes an inquiry. Before adding more keywords, check that every keyword already in the campaign leads somewhere relevant.

Build the landing page before you raise the spend

Raising spend on a page that cannot convert is the fastest way to waste a starter budget. Before the campaign launches, the landing page should state the service, show what it involves, answer the caller's first questions, and offer one clear next step. A phone number the business answers, a short form that asks only for what is needed, and a statement of the area served will do more work than a longer page that says everything and prioritizes nothing.

Test the page the way a customer would. Open it on a phone, read it once, and try to make contact. If the form is long, shorten it. If the next step is below several screens of text, move it up. If a test inquiry does not reach the inbox you monitor, fix that before a single ad runs. Every click that reaches a broken form is money spent teaching you nothing.

Read cost per inquiry, not clicks alone

Clicks are the easiest number to report and the least useful one on its own. A campaign can produce many clicks and no inquiries, and a campaign with fewer clicks can produce the inquiries that matter. The number that should guide a starter budget is the cost of one genuine inquiry: total spend divided by the number of real inquiries the campaign produced. Track inquiries separately from clicks, and count only the ones a person actually made about the service.

No article can tell you what your cost per inquiry will be, because it depends on your service, your area, and your page. That is why the starter budget is a test: it exists to measure your number, not to confirm someone else's. After a defined period, compare the cost per inquiry with what a new customer is worth to your business. That comparison, not any published average, is the decision about whether to continue, change, or stop.

Decide in advance when to pause, change, or keep going

Write the decision rules before launch. If the campaign produces no inquiries after a reasonable test period at your chosen budget, pause it and fix the page or the keyword list before spending more. If it produces inquiries but at a cost above what a customer is worth, narrow the searches or improve the page rather than raising the budget. If it produces inquiries at a cost you can accept, then consider increasing the budget gradually, watching whether the cost per inquiry holds.

Resist the temptation to judge a campaign after a day or two. A short test with a small budget produces noisy results, and reacting to noise usually means pausing a campaign that was about to work or scaling one that was about to fail. Set a review date when you launch, review the inquiry record on that date, and make one change at a time so you can tell what helped. A starter budget rewards patience and clear records more than it rewards spending.
