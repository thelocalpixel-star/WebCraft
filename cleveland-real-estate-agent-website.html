A Website for a Cleveland Real Estate Agent

A real estate agent's website has to do two jobs at once: win the listing and win the buyer. The seller wants to know the agent can sell their house; the buyer wants to search, save, and book a showing without friction. A Cleveland agent's site that does one of those jobs well and the other badly is a site that loses half its visitors.

Two visitors, two jobs

The buyer arrives with a budget and a list of must-haves, and wants to search listings, save the ones they like, and book a showing — all from a phone, all without calling first. The seller arrives with a house and a question: what is it worth, and can this agent sell it.

A Cleveland agent's site has to serve both on the homepage, in the first screen, or one of them leaves.

Cleveland is the county seat of Bradley County, and its buyers split the same way its houses do: some want the historic blocks near downtown, some want the newer subdivisions, and some want land outside the city.

The site should say so plainly — the areas served, the kinds of homes handled, and the next step for each visitor — because a buyer who cannot find their kind of house in the first screen will search somewhere else.

Search that actually works

The search is the product for buyers, and it has to work on a phone. Listings should load fast, filter by price, beds, and area, and show real photos, real prices, and a map — not a slideshow that takes a minute to load.

A search that makes the buyer wait is a search the buyer abandons, and the next agent's site is one tap away.

The search should also come from the listing service itself — the IDX feed from the local MLS — so the listings are current, the prices are real, and the agent is not maintaining a second, stale list by hand.

A site with last month's listings is worse than a site with no listings at all, because it sends buyers to houses that are already sold. Check with your broker and your MLS before publishing any feed, and keep the attribution and the disclaimers the MLS requires on the page.

Neighborhood pages that name real places

A neighborhood page should name the places a buyer actually asks about.

In Cleveland, that means the historic blocks near downtown, around Inman Street and the Bradley County Courthouse; the Blythe-Bower blocks and the Lee University corridor; the newer subdivisions along Paul Huff Parkway; the East Cleveland growth along APD-40 and Old Tasso Road; and the county communities beyond the city, like Charleston on the Hiwassee River and McDonald to the south.

Each page should say what the area is actually like — the age of the houses, the lot sizes, the schools and the commute, in plain words — and it should say what it does not know, too. No invented prices, no invented days-on-market, no invented rankings.

A neighborhood page that names real streets and admits what it cannot promise is believed; a page that invents statistics is not, and the buyer who catches one invented number will not trust the rest.

The valuation form and the showing form

The seller's form should ask for less, not more: the address, a phone number, and what the seller wants to know.

A valuation form that asks for the roof's age, the square footage, and the renovation history before the agent has even said hello is a form that will sit empty — and the seller who abandons it will call the next agent instead.

The agent should answer the same day, with a range and a next step, not with a lecture.

The buyer's form is the same shape: the listing, a phone number, and a time to see it. A showing request that gets a reply the same day, with a confirmed time, is a showing that happens; a request that waits two days is a buyer who has already seen the house with someone else.

Both forms need an owner — somebody who reads them the same day and calls back the same day — because a form nobody reads is a customer lost to the agent who answered first.

The compliance lines, in plain sight

A real estate website carries lines that other business websites do not, and they belong in plain sight. The brokerage name, the agent's license number, and the Equal Housing logo should appear where a visitor can find them without hunting — usually the footer and the contact page.

The MLS attribution and disclaimers go on the search pages, exactly as the MLS requires, and the agent should check with their broker before publishing anything the MLS has not approved.

The rule is simple: never publish a number, a claim, or a listing the brokerage has not verified, and never promise a result the market has not delivered.

A Cleveland agent's website is a marketing tool and a regulated one at the same time, and the agent who treats the compliance lines as decoration is the agent who gets a call from the broker. Get the lines right, keep them visible, and let the listings and the neighborhood pages do the selling.
