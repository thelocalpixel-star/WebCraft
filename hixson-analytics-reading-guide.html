Reading Your First Analytics Report in Hixson

A Hixson business that opens its first analytics report usually finds a wall of numbers: visits, page views, bounce rates, session times, charts that rise and fall for reasons nobody explains. Most of it can be ignored. A small business on Hixson Pike or Highway 153 needs exactly three answers from the report: how many people asked for something, where they came from, and which page they were reading when they decided to call. This guide is about reading the report in that order, and skipping the rest until it earns its place.

The report is not the business

An analytics report describes the website, not the business. It counts visits, not customers, and a visit is a much cheaper thing than a customer: a person who glanced at the hours, a searcher who opened the wrong page, a regular who checks the phone number every week. The report will happily add all of those together and call the total a success. A Hixson shopkeeper would never count foot traffic past the door as sales, and the website should be counted the same way. The first skill in reading analytics is remembering what the numbers leave out.

That is why the report should never be opened before the inquiry log. The log is the business's own count: the calls that asked about a service, the forms that named a job, the bookings with a date in them. Write that number down first, then open the report and ask what explains it. A month with forty inquiries and four hundred visits is a better month than a month with twenty inquiries and a thousand visits, and the report will not say so on its own. The report explains the inquiries; the inquiries judge the report.

The three numbers to read first

Read three numbers, in this order, and stop there the first time. First, the inquiries: how many people contacted the business, split into calls, forms and bookings. Second, the channels: for the inquiries, not the visits, where did they come from — search, direct visits, a referral, or a paid campaign. Third, the pages: which pages the inquiries came through, and which pages had visits and no inquiries at all. Everything else in the report is either detail for one of those three numbers, or decoration.

The split matters because the channels behave differently in a community like Hixson, a Chattanooga neighbourhood on the north side where most customers arrive by car along Hixson Pike. Search may bring the most visits while direct visits bring the most bookings, because the regulars type the address straight in. A campaign may bring clicks that never call. Count the inquiries per channel, not the visits, and the business will see which channel is earning its keep and which one is only earning visits.

Where Hixson visitors come from

The geography in the report deserves one look, and only one. A Hixson business serves Hixson and Hamilton County, with customers coming up from downtown Chattanooga and across from Red Bank and Soddy-Daisy. If the report shows visitors from towns the business does not serve, that is not a triumph; it is a sign the pages are being found by the wrong people, or that the service area is not stated plainly enough on the page. A visitor from far away who never calls is not a lead. It is a visit that cost the business nothing and taught it nothing.

The useful geographic question is narrower: do the visitors come from the towns the business actually serves, and do they reach the pages for the work it actually does? A service page that draws visitors from the right towns and produces no inquiries is a page problem, and it can be fixed. A service page that draws visitors from everywhere and produces no inquiries is a targeting problem, and fixing the page will not solve it. The report can tell the two apart, but only if the business knows which towns it serves before it opens the report.

The numbers that flatter and mislead

Some numbers exist to make the report look good. Total page views rise whenever one curious visitor reads ten pages. The real-time visitor count is a snapshot, not a trend. The bounce rate cannot tell a satisfied visitor who found the phone number from a visitor who left in disgust, and on a small site it will swing wildly from month to month on the strength of a handful of visits. None of these numbers is useless, but none of them answers the three questions, and a business that reads them first will spend its month chasing them.

The most misleading number of all is the visit total on its own. It rises for reasons that have nothing to do with the business: a local event, a mention somewhere, a slow news week, a page that answers a question people ask once and never act on. A Hixson business that judges itself by visits will feel rich in a month when nothing was sold, and poor in a month when the phone rang steadily. Judge the month by the inquiries, and let the visits explain them — never the other way round.

A first report, read once a month

Read the report once a month, on the same day, in the same order: inquiries first, then channels, then pages. Write the three numbers down next to last month's, in one place, so the comparison is honest. The first month's numbers are a baseline, not a verdict — a small site in a community of Hixson's size will have small numbers, and small numbers move in jumps. A single good week can double a month. Judge the direction over three months, not the size of any one jump.

Then pick one thing to fix, and only one. The page with the most visits and no inquiries is usually the right choice: make the next step visible, answer the question the title promises, or say plainly which towns the business serves. Leave the page alone for a month after the fix, and read the report again. A first report read this way teaches the business something every month, and after a year the business knows its own market — which pages earn inquiries, which channels pay, and which numbers were only ever noise.
