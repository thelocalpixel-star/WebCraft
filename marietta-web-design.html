What a Marietta customer checks before driving over

Marietta has a real downtown — the Square, with its shops, restaurants and the Strand Theatre — and many of its businesses depend on customers deciding to drive over. That decision is made on a phone, before the drive: is the business open now, where exactly is it, can I park, can I call. A website that answers those four questions in seconds is a website that brings the visit. A website that buries them is a website the customer leaves for the next result.

The pre-visit check is the test every Marietta site should pass. Open the site on a phone, as a customer would, and find the hours, the address, the phone number and the directions — all of them, in under a minute, without hunting. If any one of them is missing, stale or hard to find, the site is losing visits it never sees: customers who checked, could not find what they needed, and went somewhere that answered.

The five pages a Marietta site needs first

A small business website needs five pages before anything else. The homepage, which says what the business does, where it works, and how to reach it. A service page, which describes the service in the customer’s words, not the trade’s. An about page, which says who the business is, in plain words. A contact page, with a phone number, a form, and the hours. And one proof page — a real job, a real review in the customer’s own words — because a claim without proof is a claim the visitor has no reason to believe.

Five pages done well beat twenty pages of filler. Each page answers one question, and the site can grow later — a second service page, a page for a new town, a page for the season. The mistake is to build the twenty pages first and the five good ones never: a visitor who finds the answer on the first page they open is a visitor who calls, and a visitor who has to hunt through filler is a visitor who clicks the next result.

The first month after launch: the three checks

The launch is not finished when the site goes live. In the first month, do three checks. Call the number on the site from a phone and see who answers. Fill in the contact form with a real email address and confirm the reply arrives — a form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common launch-day failure, and only a real test message finds it. And check that the map listing points at the right place, with the right hours.

Then search for the business name and open the first page of results. A new site takes time to appear in search — a few days to a few weeks to be indexed, and months to rank — and no one can promise otherwise. Write down what was checked and when, and keep the login details with it: the hosting account, the domain registrar, and the email the account is registered under. A launch checked in the first month is a launch the business can trust. A launch never checked is a guess.
