Sorting Quote Requests for a Cookeville Website

A Cookeville website that works will fill an inbox. The problem is not getting enquiries — it is telling the real ones from the noise, and answering the real ones before a competitor does. This guide covers how to triage an inquiry inbox: real enquiries first, noise second, and a same-day reply that starts a conversation.

By WebCraft · Published November 11, 2026 · Quote guide

The inbox is a triage desk

Every enquiry that arrives should be sorted, not just answered in the order it came in. A customer who needs work this week and gave a phone number is a different priority from a vague message with no contact details. Treating them the same means the urgent one waits while the business chases the one that was never going to become work.

Triage takes five minutes a day and saves hours a week. Read each enquiry once and put it in one of three piles: reply now, reply today, or no reply needed. The reply-now pile is the customer with a real job, a real location, and a way to reach them. The reply-today pile is the enquiry that needs one clarifying question. The no-reply pile is the spam, the wrong business, and the message with no way to answer it.

Real inquiries first, noise second

A real inquiry names the work, the place, and a way to reach the person. It does not have to be long — “We need a new fence at our house in Cookeville, can you call me?” is a complete enquiry. Noise is the opposite: a single word, a link, a message asking about a service the business does not offer, or an enquiry from a town the business cannot serve.

The noise pile is not a failure of the website. Every working website gets it. The failure is letting the noise slow down the real enquiries. Answer the real ones first, in the order of urgency, and deal with the noise when the real work is done. A business that answers its best enquiries last is a business that loses them.

The two-question follow-up

Most enquiries need one or two clarifying questions before a price can be given. The mistake is to send back a list of ten. Two questions are enough to start: the size or scope of the job, and when it is needed. Those two answers decide the price range, the schedule, and whether the job is worth a visit.

Ask the two questions in one message, and make them easy to answer. “Roughly how big is the area, and when would you like it done?” is a question a customer can answer from their phone in thirty seconds. A long questionnaire is a question the customer answers never. The follow-up should feel like a conversation, not an application form.

When to call instead of email

Some enquiries should be answered by phone, not by email. A customer who gave a phone number and asked for a call expects a call. A job that is hard to price from a message — a repair, a custom build, anything with unknowns — is faster to scope in a two-minute call than in five emails. And a customer who has already asked two businesses is more likely to choose the one whose voice they have heard.

The call does not need to be a sales pitch. It needs to confirm the work, confirm the location, and give a next step with a time. “I can come by Thursday morning and give you a price on the spot” is a complete call. The customer knows what happens next and when. An email that says the same thing takes longer to write and longer to read.

The same-day reply habit

The habit that matters most is the simplest: reply the same day, every day. A customer who asks for a quote is usually asking more than one business, and the first useful reply usually wins. Same-day does not mean instant — it means the enquiry is answered before the working day ends, with something the customer can act on.

The habit needs a system, not willpower. One person owns the inbox, checks it twice a day, and answers the reply-now pile first. An automatic acknowledgement helps — “Thanks, we will reply today” — but it is not a reply. The real reply is a human message with a next step and a time. A Cookeville business that builds that habit will win work that slower competitors never see.
