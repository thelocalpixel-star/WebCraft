An Athens Hosting Renewal Checklist

A renewal checklist for Athens businesses: who owns the domain, what the renewal covers, backups, access, and a calendar that prevents an expired site.

Find out who actually owns the domain

The most expensive hosting problem is not a renewal fee. It is discovering, at renewal time, that the domain is registered under someone else's account, an old employee's email, or an agency that no longer answers. Before anything expires, log in to the registrar account and confirm three things: the domain is in an account you control, the administrative email is one you still monitor, and the account's payment method is current. If any of those fail, fix them now, while there is no deadline pressure.

Ownership matters separately from hosting. A business can change hosting providers and keep its domain, but only if it controls the domain registration. Keep the registrar login, the domain's expiry date, and the name of the person responsible for renewals in one place that more than one trusted person can reach. A business that loses access to its domain can lose its website address, its email, and its search presence at the same time.

Read what the renewal actually renews

A renewal notice may cover several different things: the domain registration, the hosting plan, an email service, or an added certificate. These often renew on different dates and sometimes with different providers. Read the notice line by line and match each charge to a service you recognize. If a line item is unfamiliar, ask the provider what it is before paying it, and check whether the same service is already covered elsewhere.

WebCraft's published pricing is a useful reference point here: a website is $500 one-time, with optional hosting and care at $20 per month. Whatever provider you use, the question is the same. What does the plan include, what is billed separately, and what happens if a payment fails? A provider that answers those questions plainly is easier to plan around than one whose renewal notice is the first you hear about a charge.

Make a backup and confirm access before anything expires

A renewal is the wrong time to discover that nobody can log in to the hosting account. Confirm that you can sign in, that you know where the site's files and database live, and that a recent backup exists somewhere other than the hosting account itself. A backup stored only on the same server it protects is no backup at all when that server is suspended for non-payment.

If you are changing providers, move the site and test it before cancelling the old plan. A move that happens after cancellation becomes a rebuild under pressure. Keep the old plan running until the new site loads correctly, the contact form reaches the inbox you monitor, and the email addresses you rely on still work. A week of overlap between two plans is cheaper than a week offline.

Check the small things that break a live site

Renewals break sites in quiet ways. A security certificate that lapses turns a working site into a warning page. A payment method that expired with an old card suspends the account without warning. An administrative email at a former employee's address means the renewal reminders went to someone who no longer reads them. Work through each of these before the expiry date, not after the site goes down.

After any renewal or change, open the site on a phone, submit a test inquiry, and confirm it arrives. Check the homepage, one service page, and the contact page. This takes five minutes and catches most renewal problems while they are still easy to fix. A site that looks fine to its owner on a desktop computer may still be showing a warning to a first-time visitor on a phone.

Keep a renewal calendar you will actually use

Put every expiry date on one calendar: domain, hosting, email, and certificate. Set a reminder thirty days before each date and another seven days before. The thirty-day reminder gives you time to compare providers or correct an account problem; the seven-day reminder catches the renewal you meant to handle and forgot. Assign one person to own the calendar, and one backup person who can act if the first is away.

When you switch providers, update the calendar the same day, not later. Most renewal disasters are not technical failures; they are a date that lived in one person's inbox and nowhere else. A shared calendar, a controlled domain account, a current payment method, and a tested backup are the whole system. None of it is complicated, and all of it is easier than recovering a site that expired.
