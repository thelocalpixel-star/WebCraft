Can Your Business Get a Google Business Profile?

A Google Business Profile is what puts a business on the map when someone searches nearby. Not every business can have one, and the ones that can must follow rules about what they claim. This guide explains who qualifies, what a service-area business is, what never to claim, and what to do instead if your business is not eligible.

What a service-area business is

Google draws a line between two kinds of business. One has a place customers visit: a shop, a salon, a restaurant. The other goes to the customer: a plumber, a cleaner, a landscaper, a mobile repair service. Google calls the second kind a service-area business. It can have a Business Profile, but the profile works differently: it hides the business address and shows the area served instead.

The distinction matters because it decides what the profile is allowed to show. A service-area business names the towns and neighbourhoods it serves, and customers contact it by phone or message. It does not show a storefront, because there is no storefront to show. A business that serves customers at its own address and at theirs should say so honestly, and list both — the address where customers can come, and the area where the business travels.

Who can have a profile — and who cannot

A business qualifies when it serves customers in person, at a real location or at the customer's location. A plumber who visits homes qualifies. A shop with a door customers walk through qualifies. A business that only sells online, with no in-person service at all, generally does not qualify — an online-only shop has no local presence for a map to show.

Eligibility is decided by how the business actually works, not by how it describes itself. A business cannot become eligible by renting a virtual office, using a post office box as an address, or borrowing someone else's premises. Google checks, and a profile built on a claim that is not true is a profile that can be suspended. If you are unsure whether your business qualifies, the safe move is to read Google's own eligibility rules before you apply, not after.

What never to claim

Four claims will get a profile into trouble. A storefront that does not exist: if customers cannot walk in, do not show an address as if they can. Hours when nobody is there: a business that works by appointment should say so, not invent opening hours. An address that is not yours: a home address used without care, a virtual office, or a friend's shop are all the wrong answer. And a service area you cannot reach: listing towns you do not serve misleads customers and earns the kind of review no business wants.

The rule behind all four is the same: the profile must describe the real business, not the business you wish you had. A service-area profile that hides its address and names the towns it genuinely serves is honest and useful. A profile that invents a shop on a street where none exists is a listing that will not survive a check — by Google, or by a customer who drives there.

How verification works

Google verifies a profile before it shows it properly. The usual methods are a postcard sent to the business address, a phone call, or a video check in which the business shows its premises or its work. The business name on the profile must be the real name customers know — not a string of keywords, and not a different name invented for search.

Verification is not a one-time event. Google can ask again if the details change, or if something about the profile looks wrong. Keep the name, the phone number, the service area and the hours accurate, and keep them the same everywhere the business is listed. A profile whose details match the website and the directories is a profile that stays up.

If you cannot have one, do this instead

Some businesses genuinely cannot have a profile, and that is not the end of being found. A website with clear service pages, honest service-area wording and a phone number that works is the first substitute. Accurate listings in the directories that matter — the ones customers actually use — are the second. Real reviews, in the customer's own words, gathered after real work, are the third. None of these needs a map pin to work.
