Why your ad should land on a service page, not your homepage

An ad promises one thing — a service, in a place, for a person with a specific problem. A homepage answers a different question: what does this business do, in general.

Sending the click to the homepage makes the visitor do the work the ad already did: find the service, check the area, and work out the next step. Most will not.

They will go back and click the next ad instead.

A service page keeps the promise. It names the service and the area in the first screen, explains what happens next, and offers one clear action — a phone call or a quote request.

That is the whole job of a landing page. It does not need to be long, and it does not need to sell the whole business.

It needs to match the search that triggered the ad, and to make the next step obvious on a phone.

Before a campaign launches, open the landing page on your phone and follow it as a customer would.

If you cannot reach the service, the area and the contact action in under a minute, fix the page before you pay for the clicks.

What Google Ads management actually includes

Google Ads management is not setting the ads up once and leaving them alone.

It is monthly work, and the work is mostly checking: which searches triggered the ads this month, and which of those searches should never trigger them again; whether the landing pages still match what the ads promise;

whether the budget is pacing to last the month or running out in the second week; and what the spend bought in real inquiries — calls and form submissions — against what it cost.

A manager who cannot say what they checked this month is not managing the ads, whatever the invoice says.

The monthly work also includes the decisions a business should not have to make alone:

  • Pausing a search term that brings clicks and no inquiries.
  • Raising the budget on the service that does bring them.
  • Telling the business honestly when an ad is not working instead of spending its budget anyway.

What management is not is a guarantee: no one can guarantee a cost per lead, a fixed position, or a set number of calls, because the auction changes daily and competitors change their bids.

Before hiring a manager, a business should ask four questions:

  • What do you check each month?
  • How do you report it?
  • What happens to the account if we stop working together?
  • What does your fee cover that the ad spend does not?

The account itself should belong to the business, not the manager — because an account the business does not own is an account it cannot take with it.

Google Ads or Facebook Ads: which one first?

Google Ads and Facebook Ads reach people in different moments. Google Ads reach the person who just searched for the service — a plumber, a roofer, a website — and is ready to call.

Facebook Ads reach the person scrolling, who was not looking for the service and has to be interrupted into interest.

For a local business, that difference is the whole decision: search captures demand that already exists, and social has to create it.

The costs differ the same way. Search clicks cost more because the person is closer to buying — published 2025 benchmarks put Google search at roughly $5.26 a click and about $70 a lead

against Meta at roughly $1.72 a click and $25 to $30 a lead. But a cheaper click that rarely becomes a customer is not a bargain.

The number to compare is the cost per inquiry, not the cost per click, and the business should run the same test on both: one service, one page, one month, and count the calls.

As a rule, search comes first for a business that needs calls now — the plumber with an open week, the contractor with a slow month, the shop with a new service.

Social comes first for a business that needs to be known before it is needed — a new name in town, a seasonal offer, a service people do not search for until they see it.

Many businesses end up running both, with search for the calls and social for the awareness — but the budget should go to search first, because search is where the customer already raised a hand.

Google Ads: questions before you start

Is Google Ads spend included in website pricing?

No. Advertising spend, campaign management and the website package are separate costs. We agree on the campaign scope and budget before launch.

Can you target Cleveland, Chattanooga or smaller communities?

Campaigns can be planned around your real service area. Location options and available reach need to be reviewed in the account; targeting is not proof that every person seeing an ad is physically inside a boundary.

Should I choose SEO or Google Ads first?

It depends on the offer, budget, website readiness and how quickly you need to test demand. SEO develops organic visibility over time. Ads buy exposure while a campaign runs. Both need an effective inquiry path.

Will you guarantee a cost per lead?

No. Costs and lead quality depend on search demand, competition, the offer and follow-up. We agree on measurement and review criteria instead of guaranteeing the same cost for every business.

Should my Google ad send people to my homepage?

Usually not. An ad promises one service, so the click should land on the page for that service, with the area and the next step clear in the first screen. A homepage asks the visitor to find the service themselves, and most will leave instead. Use the homepage only when the ad itself is about the business as a whole.

What does a Google Ads manager actually do each month?

They check which searches triggered your ads, add the searches that should never trigger them again, check the landing pages still match the ads, watch the budget pace so it lasts the month, and report what the spend bought in real inquiries. The account stays yours, and the monthly report should show inquiries and spend side by side.

Should a local business run Google Ads or Facebook Ads?

Google Ads first, in most cases — it reaches people who just searched for the service and are ready to call. Facebook Ads reach people who were not looking, so the clicks cost less but convert less often. Compare the cost per inquiry, not the cost per click, and test one service on one page for a month before choosing.
