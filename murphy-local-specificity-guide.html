A Murphy Local-Specificity Guide

Useful local website content explains the services a business offers, the area it serves and the details customers need. Apply those principles to a Murphy service-area page so local SEO copy reflects the actual business.

A templated page says nothing only you could say

A templated city page is easy to spot. It lists generic services, uses stock photos, and says it serves the Murphy area without saying anything about Murphy at all. Swap the town name for another one and nothing on the page breaks, because nothing on the page was ever specific to the place. A customer reading it learns that a business exists, and almost nothing else — not what it does best, not where it actually goes, and not why a local customer should choose it over a business an hour away.

The test is simple. Read your page and cross out every sentence that would still be true if the business were in another town. What is left is the local part of the page, and for many small businesses it is very little. That is not a writing problem. It is a specificity problem: the page was written from a template, not from what the business actually knows about its customers and its area. The rest of this guide is about putting that knowledge back in.

Name the work you actually do

Start with the services you genuinely want inquiries about, and describe them the way you would describe them to a customer standing in front of you. A repair business can say which kinds of jobs it takes on and which ones it turns away. A service business can say what a first visit usually involves and what it needs from the customer before it starts. Specifics like these do more than inform — they qualify. A customer who recognises their own problem in your description is a customer who already trusts you a little.

Be just as specific about what you do not do. If a job needs a site visit before you can quote, say so. If you do not cover a particular type of work, say that too, and say what you do instead. Honest limits save both sides time, and they make the services you do list more believable. A page that claims to do everything convinces nobody; a page that describes a few things precisely convinces the right people.

Say where you actually go, in words customers recognise

Service-area wording is where templated pages fail most visibly. “Serving Murphy and surrounding areas” tells a customer nothing about whether you will come to them. Name the communities you can genuinely support, in the words customers use for them, and explain any boundaries that matter — how far you travel, whether distance affects timing, and what happens when a job sits outside your usual area. A customer who can see themselves in your service area will call; a customer who has to guess will call someone else.

Do not claim coverage you do not have, and do not imply an office in a place where you have none. A business can serve a community well without being based there, and customers understand that — but only if the page says so plainly. State where you are based, where you travel, and how a customer in each place reaches you. Accuracy here is not just good manners; it is the difference between an inquiry you can serve and one you have to turn away.

Put in details only a local business would know

Every trade has local knowledge that a template cannot invent. Demand may peak at a particular time of year. Access to some properties may depend on the season. Customers in some places may need to know what to have ready before you arrive, or how long a job usually takes once you are there. These are small details, and they are exactly what make a page feel written by someone who works in the area rather than someone who filled in a form.

Write them down as you encounter them. When a customer asks a question twice, the answer belongs on the page. When a job surprises you — access, timing, materials, a preparation step — note what a customer should have known beforehand. Over a few months, those notes become the most useful part of your site, because they answer the questions real customers ask, in the order they ask them.

Check the page the way a Murphy customer would

Before you publish, read the page as a stranger would. Can you tell what the business does, where it goes, and how to start — without already knowing the answers? If you have to guess at any of those, a customer will guess too, and most will guess wrong or give up. Ask someone who has never seen the business to read the page and describe it back to you. The gaps they find are the gaps your customers are finding.

Then keep the page honest over time. Services change, areas change, and the questions customers ask change with them. Revisit the page after a few real inquiries and check whether it still describes the work you want and the area you serve. A genuinely local page is never finished — it is maintained, the same way the business itself is, one real detail at a time.
