The five pages a Lilburn business needs

WebCraft's standard website covers up to five pages for $500, one time, and five pages are enough for most small businesses here — if each page has a job. Home says what the business does and where, in the first screen. Services says what it sells, one page per service it actually sells. About says who is behind it.

Contact says how to reach a person. The fifth page earns its place by answering the question customers actually ask.

For a Lilburn business, the area page matters most. Name the places served — Lilburn, Old Town, the Highway 29 corridor, and the nearby towns in Gwinnett County — and say plainly whether the work happens on site, at the customer's place, or remotely.

A customer searching from Lilburn wants to know the business serves Lilburn, and the page should say so in the first line, not the footer.

A small city between two bigger ones

Lilburn sits between Atlanta, nineteen miles down Highway 29, and Lawrenceville, the Gwinnett County seat, eleven miles the other way — and that position shapes the search.

A Lilburn business competes for Lilburn searches, not for Atlanta searches, and the website should be built for the smaller fight it can win: the service, the town, and the proof that the business is here.

That is also why the local detail matters more than the design. A page that names Old Town, the Greenway Trail, and the city hall and library in the revitalized downtown tells a Lilburn customer the business knows the town. A page that could belong to any suburb in America tells them nothing, and they will tap the next result.

In a city of about 14,500 people, being specific is not a limitation — it is the advantage.

What the first draft should prove

The first draft arrives as a link, not a meeting. Open it on your phone, on Highway 29, and check four things: the service is in the first screen, the area is named, the phone number works when tapped, and the words sound like the business.

If any of those fail, the draft is not ready — and that is what the revision rounds are for.

The revisions are usually two rounds, and they are for the words, the photos, and the details — not for redesigning the site from scratch each time. A provider who shows a draft early, takes the corrections, and relaunches quickly is a provider who has done this before.

One who disappears for a month and returns with a finished site nobody reviewed is the warning sign.

The price, the ownership, and the exit

WebCraft's price is $500, one time, for up to five pages, with hosting and care at $20 a month only if the business wants it. The number matters less than the terms beside it: the domain in the owner's name, the site the owner keeps, and the freedom to leave with both.

A Lilburn business that has spent years building a name on Highway 29 should not lose its website to a provider it stops paying.

Compare any other quote on the same three lines — price, ownership, exit — and get the answers in writing. A cheap build that rents the site by the month and switches it off when the payments stop is not a $200 website; it is a $200 deposit on a website you will never own.

The right choice leaves the business holding its own website, its own name, and its own customers.
