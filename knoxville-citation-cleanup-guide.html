What a citation is, and why consistency matters

A citation is any place on the internet that lists your business name, address, and phone number — a directory, a map listing, a social profile, a chamber page, a review site. Customers use citations to find you and to check that you are real. When the details match everywhere, the picture is clear. When they do not — an old phone number here, a different spelling of the name there, an outdated address somewhere else — customers hesitate, and some of them call the wrong number or drive to the wrong place.

Consistency is not about being listed everywhere. It is about being listed accurately wherever you are listed. A small business with ten correct listings is in better shape than one with fifty listings, half of them wrong. This guide is about finding the listings that exist, fixing the ones you control, and keeping them fixed.

Find every listing that mentions your business

Start by searching for your own business. Search the business name, the phone number, and the business name together with Knoxville. Note every listing that appears, including ones you did not create — directories often build listings from public records, old data, or aggregators, and they can contain details you never supplied. Make a simple list: where the listing is, what name it shows, what address and phone number it shows, and whether it looks current.

Check the places customers actually look, not just the ones you remember. Map listings, the major directories, review sites, and your own social profiles are the obvious ones. But also check the less obvious: old directory entries from a previous address, listings under a slightly different business name, and duplicate listings for the same business. Duplicates are common, and they are worth finding, because a customer may land on the wrong copy.

Fix the details you control first

Work through your list starting with the listings you control: your website, your map listing, your social profiles, and any directory where you have an account. Decide on one canonical version of your name, address, and phone number, and use exactly that version everywhere. Small differences count — “St.” versus “Street”, a suite number included in one place and missing in another, a phone number with or without an area code. Pick one form and stick to it.

Do not try to fix everything in one sitting. Fix the most visible listings first — the ones customers actually find — and work down the list over a few weeks. Keep notes on what you changed and when, so you can check later whether the change actually took effect. Some directories update immediately; others take days or weeks, and some require a verification step before they publish a correction.

Only claim listings that are yours

Claiming a listing means taking responsibility for the details it shows, and that is only appropriate for listings that belong to your business. Never claim a listing for a business you do not own, and never create a listing for a location you do not operate from. If you find a listing for your business that you did not create, claiming it is usually the right move — it gives you control over the details — but check first that the details it shows are actually yours.

Duplicates and closed listings need a different response: report them rather than claiming them. Most directories have a way to report a duplicate or a business that has closed or moved, and cleaning those up prevents customers from landing on stale information. This is slow work, and it is not glamorous, but it is the work that stops a customer from calling a number you stopped using three years ago.

Re-check on a schedule

Citations drift. Directories refresh their data from aggregators, a well-meaning employee updates one profile and not the others, a phone number changes and one listing keeps the old one. A cleanup done once is a cleanup that slowly undoes itself. Put a reminder on the calendar — once a quarter is enough for most small businesses — to search for your business again and compare the top listings against your canonical details.

The quarterly check takes less than an hour once the first cleanup is done, and it catches problems while they are small. Note what changed, fix what you control, and report what you do not. Accuracy is not a project with a finish line; it is a routine, like checking that the phone number on your website still rings. Customers will never thank you for it, which is exactly the point — they will simply find you, call you, and arrive where they expected to.
