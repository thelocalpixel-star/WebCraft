The monthly number that follows it decides what the website really costs over three years, and that number ranges from nothing at all to $179 a month. The short answer: $200 to $2,500 to build

WebCraft builds a website for $500, one time, with up to five pages and hosting and care available at $20 a month if the business wants it. That is the number to hold the others against.

At the low end, East Tennessee Web Creator publishes a Starter site at $200 one time, with no monthly fees, and a Small Business site starting at $400. At the high end for a small build, Trueline Web Co., based in Athens, publishes most builds at $750 to $2,500.

Between those ends sit two more Athens names. Aston Cook Design, a one-person studio in Athens, publishes $500 to $1,800, one time, with no monthly fee ever — you pay once, and the site is yours.

What the Athens quotes actually include

The price only means something beside the scope. Aston Cook builds the site start to finish as a one-person studio, for artists and independent professionals, with no monthly fee and no subscription to cancel.

Trueline quotes one flat number for the build and one flat number for monthly care, with most builds taking two to four weeks from kickoff to live, and its care plan covers hosting, security updates, backups, and small content changes.

Athena's packages are scoped the same way: a set number of pages, an inquiry form, and revision rounds, with domain, hosting, and paid tools additional. WebCraft's $500 covers up to five pages, one time, with the $20-a-month hosting and care optional rather than required.

The question to ask any of them is the same: what is included, what is extra, and who owns the site when the work is done.

What to ask before you sign

Before signing anything, ask who owns the domain name, who holds the hosting account, and what happens to the site if you leave. A website registered in the provider's name, on the provider's hosting, with no handover, is a website you rent — and the rent can rise.

The answers should be written down, with the ownership and the exit terms beside the price.

Ask what the quote leaves out, too: the domain name, the business email, the photos, the copy, and the pages beyond the agreed number are the usual extras, and they are where a low quote grows. Ask how long the build takes, how many revision rounds are included, and what a change costs after launch.

A quote that answers all of that in plain words is a quote you can compare; a quote that does not is a quote you cannot.

The cheapest quote is not the cheapest website

The cheapest build in Athens is $199 or $200, and for a business that needs a simple site and nothing else, it may be the right answer.

But a low quote can also mean a template with your name on it, a site you cannot change yourself, or a build that leaves out the pages a customer actually needs — and fixing those later costs more than the difference between the quotes.

Compare the three-year total, not the first number, and compare what the site has to do: answer the customer's questions, take the call or the form, and look like the business it represents.

A $500 site that does all three, with no monthly charge unless you choose it, is cheaper than a $200 site that does one of them and needs replacing in a year. The cheapest website is the one you do not have to buy twice.
