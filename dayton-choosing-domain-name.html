Choosing a Domain Name in Dayton

A domain name is the address customers remember, the name they type, and the name they say to someone else. A Dayton business that chooses well will use the same address for years; a business that chooses badly will pay for the mistake every time someone misspells it, forgets it, or lands on someone else's site. This guide is about choosing well: business name first, .com first, short and easy to say, no hyphens or numbers, a trademark check, and registered in your own name.

Business name first

Start with the business name, exactly as customers know it. The domain should be the name, or as close to it as the registry allows — not a clever phrase, not a keyword stuffed with the town and the service, not a name the business will outgrow. A customer who hears the business name should be able to guess the domain, and a customer who sees the domain should be able to say the business name. If the two do not match, one of them is wrong, and it is usually the domain.

If the exact name is taken, the honest options are few: a shorter version of the name, the name with a plain word added — the town, or the word the business is known by — or a different ending, in that order. What should not happen is a rewrite of the business to fit a domain: a name changed because the address was taken is a business renamed for a registrar. The name comes first. The domain follows it. And the domain should be written down, with the business name, in the one document that records what the business owns.

.com first, short and easy to say

Choose .com first, because it is the ending customers expect. A customer who hears a domain will type .com unless told otherwise, and a business on any other ending will spend its life correcting people. The other endings have their uses — a local ending, a trade ending — but for a small business in Dayton, .com is the default, and any other choice needs a reason, not a preference. If the .com is taken and the business still wants the name, the answer is a different name, not a different ending.

Keep it short, and keep it easy to say. A domain that has to be spelled out over the phone, letter by letter, is a domain that will be mistyped. Say it out loud: can a customer hear it once and type it correctly? Can it be said on the radio, printed on a van, and read from a business card without confusion? Short is not a style choice. It is a practical one — every extra word is another chance for the customer to land somewhere else.

No hyphens, no numbers

Do not put hyphens in a domain. A hyphen is invisible when spoken — nobody says hyphen — and a customer who hears the name will type it without one, and land on someone else's site. A hyphen is also easy to forget and easy to mistype, and it makes the address look like the dozens of lookalike domains that exist for every good name. The same goes for numbers: a customer who hears five will type 5, and a customer who hears two will wonder whether it is 2, to, or too. Words, not symbols, and not digits.

The test is the phone test: say the domain to someone who has never seen it, once, and ask them to type it. If they type it correctly, the domain passes. If they ask how it is spelled, or whether that was a hyphen, or whether the number is a word, the domain fails — and a domain that fails the phone test will fail every day, in every conversation, for as long as the business uses it. Choose the name that passes the first time.

Trademark check, registered in your own name

Before registering, check that the name does not belong to someone else. A quick search of the trademark register and of the web will show whether another business already uses the name, or something close enough to confuse customers. A domain that infringes a trademark can be taken away, and a business that builds its reputation on a name it does not own will lose the name and the reputation together. The check takes an hour. The loss takes years.

Register the domain in the business's own name, with the business's own account, and keep the login. A domain registered by a designer, a friend, or an agency is a domain the business does not control — and a business that does not control its own address can lose it when the relationship ends. The registration should list the business as the owner, the business's email as the contact, and the renewal on a calendar the business actually reads. A domain that lapses because nobody remembered the renewal is a website that disappears overnight.

The address customers remember

A good domain is an address customers remember without trying: the business name, .com, short, spoken once and typed correctly. It will be printed on the van, spoken on the phone, and typed by customers who have never seen it written down. It will outlast the website design, the hosting, and the marketing — the address stays while everything else changes. Choose it as if the business will use it for a decade, because it probably will.

And once it is chosen, use it everywhere, the same way, every time: the website, the email, the business cards, the van, the search listing. A domain that appears in two forms — with and without the www, with and without the town — is two addresses, and the business will spend its life explaining which one is right. One address, used consistently, is an address customers remember. In Dayton, as anywhere, the businesses that are easy to find are the ones whose address is easy to remember.
