An Asheville Google Ads Starter Budget, Built as a Test

Asheville is a mountain town of about ninety-five thousand people, built around the Biltmore Estate, the Blue Ridge Parkway, and a downtown of breweries, galleries, and farm-to-table restaurants. It is also a visitor town, with a fall foliage season that fills the Parkway in October and a quieter winter behind it. A starter Google Ads budget here should be built like a test in that town: small, focused, and honest about what it can prove.

A starter budget is a test

The first mistake with a starter budget is treating it like a commitment. A business that puts a thousand dollars into a campaign and expects customers on day one has bought a hope, not a test. A test has a question, a limit, and a decision at the end: does this service, in this town, bring inquiries at a price we can afford? If the answer is yes, the budget grows. If the answer is no, the business learns it for the price of the test instead of the price of a year.

Set the cap at an amount the business can afford to lose completely. That is the only honest way to set a first budget, because a test can fail, and a budget the business needed for rent will be stopped the moment it wobbles, which is exactly when the test is producing its most useful information. A small business in Asheville should pick a monthly number it can forget about for thirty days, write it down, and then let the campaign run long enough to answer the question.

One service, one page, one town

The second mistake is spreading the test too thin. A starter campaign for five services across three towns is five tests running at once, and none of them will have enough clicks to prove anything. Pick one service — the one with the best margin, the clearest offer, and the shortest path from inquiry to booked job — and build the campaign around it alone. One service, one landing page, and the town the business actually serves.

The landing page is half the test. A visitor who clicks an ad for a service should land on a page about that service, with the price signal, the proof, and the next step all visible without scrolling. Asheville searchers are often visitors planning from a hotel room or a car on the Parkway, and they will not dig through a homepage to find the service they just clicked on. If the page is the homepage, the test is testing the homepage, not the ads, and the result will say more about the page than the campaign.

What the test costs in a visitor town

Published 2025 benchmarks put Google search ads near $5.26 per click and about $70 per lead on average, across many industries and markets. Those are averages, not promises for Asheville, and the real number for one service in one town will be different. But the shape of the numbers is useful for sizing the test: if a click costs around five dollars, a hundred dollars buys about twenty clicks, and twenty clicks may bring one or two inquiries, or none at all.

That arithmetic is why the test needs enough budget to buy a meaningful number of clicks. A budget so small that it buys ten clicks and stops has not tested anything; it has bought a coin flip. A month of a few hundred dollars, spent on one service and one page, buys enough clicks to see whether inquiries appear at all, and what they cost. The business should decide the number before the campaign starts, write down what would count as success, and resist the urge to move the goalposts halfway through.

Reading the result honestly

Judge the test on inquiries and cost per inquiry, not on clicks and not on impressions. A campaign that brought two hundred clicks and no calls has failed, however good the click rate looked. A campaign that brought eight clicks and two booked jobs has succeeded, however small the numbers look. The size of the test is not the point; the price of a customer is.

Give the test a full month, and read it in weekly slices. Asheville's seasons move fast — a fall weekend on the Parkway is a different market from a January weekday — and a test judged on its first week will often be judged on the wrong week. If the month ends with inquiries the business can afford, the budget can grow in steps, one service at a time. If it ends with clicks and no inquiries, the first thing to check is the page, then the service chosen, and only then the idea of advertising at all.

After the test: grow, change, or stop

Three endings are all honest. Grow: the test brought inquiries at a price the business can afford, so the budget rises and a second service is tested the same way, one at a time. Change: the clicks came but the inquiries did not, so the page or the offer changes and the test runs once more with the fix in place. Stop: the service does not bring inquiries at a price that works, and the money moves to the channel that does — a better page, local search, or word of mouth earned from real jobs.

None of the three is a failure except the fourth option: spending without ever deciding. A starter budget that rolls on for a year, never reviewed, never counted, is not a test anymore. It is a habit, and habits are what a starter budget is supposed to prevent. Test small, count honestly, and let the month decide.
