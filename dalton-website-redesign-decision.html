A Dalton Website Redesign Decision Guide

Refresh or rebuild? A Dalton business asking that question usually has a third option it has not considered: keep what works and rebuild only what does not. This guide covers the three paths — refresh, rebuild, and the middle path — what each one fixes, what it costs, and how to choose between them with numbers the business already has.

Three paths, not two

Most redesign conversations start with two options: refresh the old site or rebuild it from scratch. The third option — keep the pages that work and rebuild the ones that do not — is the one most businesses actually need, and the one nobody offers, because it is harder to sell than a fresh start. But it is usually the cheapest path to a site that works.

The three paths answer three different problems. A refresh answers stale content. A rebuild answers a broken structure. The middle path answers a site that is half working: some pages bring enquiries and rank well, and some pages are dead weight. Knowing which problem the site has is the whole decision — and the numbers the business already has will say which one it is.

What a refresh fixes

A refresh fixes the visible problems without touching the structure: new photos, rewritten headlines, accurate hours and prices, a clearer call to action, and a faster page. A refresh is the right answer when the site’s structure is sound and the problem is that the content has gone stale. It is cheaper, faster, and it keeps the pages that already rank and already bring enquiries.

A refresh is also the right answer when the business itself has not changed. The services are the same, the towns are the same, the customers are the same — only the website has aged. In that case, new content on the old structure is all the site needs. A business that rebuilds a site that only needed a refresh has paid for a new house when the old one needed paint.

What only a rebuild fixes

A rebuild is the answer when the structure itself is the problem. The site was built on a platform nobody can edit, the pages cannot be found by search engines, the mobile version is unusable, or the business has changed so much that the old pages describe a business that no longer exists. No amount of new photos fixes a site that cannot be edited or found.

A rebuild is also the answer when the business has outgrown the site — new services, new towns, new ways of taking bookings. But a rebuild should keep what the old site earned: the domain, the pages that rank, the content that works, and the logins. A rebuild that throws those away is not a fresh start; it is a step backwards, and the business will pay for it in lost enquiries.

The middle path: keep what works

The middle path starts with a page-by-page audit: which pages bring enquiries, which pages rank, which pages nobody reads. The pages that work stay — same URLs, same content, maybe a fresh headline. The pages that do not work are rebuilt or removed. The homepage and the contact page are usually rebuilt; the service pages that bring calls are usually kept.

The middle path is cheaper than a rebuild and more thorough than a refresh, and it keeps the search visibility the old site earned. It also forces the decision the other two paths avoid: which pages actually earn their keep. A business that does the audit discovers that half its pages were doing nothing — and that the other half were doing all the work.

How to choose, with numbers you already have

The choice does not need a consultant. It needs three numbers the business already has: how many enquiries the site brings each month, which pages bring them, and how old the content is. If the site brings enquiries and the content is stale, refresh. If the site brings nothing and cannot be edited, rebuild. If some pages work and some do not, take the middle path.

Write the decision down before spending anything: what is wrong, which path fixes it, what it will cost, and how you will know it worked. “Our service pages bring calls but the homepage is five years old. We will rebuild the homepage and refresh the service pages, and judge it by the calls next month.” That paragraph is the brief, the budget, and the test — and it is worth more than any redesign proposal.
