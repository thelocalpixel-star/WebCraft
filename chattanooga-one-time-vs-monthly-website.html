Start with what you keep

Chattanooga has more web designers competing for the same job than any other city in this region, and most of them sell a monthly plan. Before comparing prices, compare ownership: who owns the domain, the files and the content when the payments stop? A site you cannot take with you is rent, whatever the sales page calls it. A site you own is an asset, even if you pay someone to look after it.

The ownership questions are short. Is the domain registered in my name? Can I log into the hosting account? If I cancel the monthly plan, does the website stay mine, and can I move it to another host? Get the answers in writing. Everything else in this guide — the prices, the five-year totals, the decision rule — only matters once those answers are clear.

The Chattanooga price picture

A bigger market means more choice and more monthly plans. The published prices in this market cluster in the same range: monthly website plans around $99 to $130 a month, a $4,000 one-time option from one firm, and hosting-only plans from $9.99 a month that cover hosting and nothing else. Against those, a WebCraft website is $500 once, with $20-a-month hosting and care that is optional.

Chattanooga businesses also face a choice Cleveland businesses see less often: agencies selling bundled growth packages at far higher monthly prices. The comparison method does not change with the size of the number. Put every quote on the same basis — year one, year two, and what you own — and the expensive option has to justify itself on work delivered, not on confidence.

The five-year maths

Monthly prices look small until they are multiplied. A $99-a-month plan costs $1,188 a year, or $5,940 over five years. A $130-a-month plan costs $7,800 over five years. A $4,000 one-time purchase is expensive in year one and then done. A WebCraft site costs $500 once, plus optional care at $20 a month: $1,700 over five years, and $500 if you arrange hosting yourself and skip the care.

Hosting-only at $9.99 a month is about $600 over five years, but it buys no design, no pages and no care — it is a shelf, not a shop. These are published prices and they change; check the source before you decide. The maths is the point, not the brand names: a monthly fee that never ends is the most expensive way to own a website, unless it buys work you genuinely need every month.

What to ask before choosing a monthly plan

If a monthly plan still appeals, ask five questions before you sign. Is the website mine if I cancel? What does the monthly fee include — edits, hosting, support, or all three? Is there a setup fee, and what happens to my email if I leave? How many changes a month are included, and what counts as extra? And who do I call when the site breaks on a Saturday?

The answers that matter are the specific ones: a number of included changes, a named handoff list, a straight yes or no on ownership. “We handle everything” is not an answer to any of these questions. If the provider cannot say what you own, the plan is rent, and the five-year total is the price of that rent.

A simple decision rule

The decision is simpler than the sales pages make it. If you want to own the site, pay for help when you need it, and keep the option of moving on with the site in your hands, buy it once. If you want one bill, no involvement, and someone else handling every change, rent it monthly — but only after the ownership answers are in writing, and only with the five-year total in front of you.

A Chattanooga business does not need the most expensive website in the market. It needs a site its customers can find, a phone number they can tap, and an owner who knows what they bought.
