A Soddy-Daisy Analytics Monthly Routine

A Soddy-Daisy business does not need to watch its analytics every day. It needs one routine, once a month, in the same order every time: the inquiries first, then where they came from, then which pages earned them, then one page fixed. The routine takes less than an hour, and it works because it is the same every month — the numbers can be compared, the fixes can be judged, and the business learns what actually moves the phone. This guide is that routine, written for a city on Chickamauga Lake in northern Hamilton County, where the seasons move the numbers and the routine has to see past them.

Same day, same order, every month

Pick a day — the first Monday, the fifteenth, any day that will be kept — and do the routine on that day every month. The day matters less than the sameness: a routine done on the same day each month produces numbers that can be compared, and a routine done whenever someone remembers produces numbers that cannot. Put the day on the calendar, and treat it the way the business treats any other recurring job. Analytics rewards the business that reads it on a schedule and acts on what it finds.

Do the routine in the same order every time, and write the numbers down in the same place. The order is the discipline: inquiries first, then channels, then pages, then the fix. A routine that starts with the visit total will end up judging the month by visits, however good the intentions were. A routine that starts with the inquiries will judge the month by what the business actually sold, and the rest of the numbers will fall into their proper place — as explanations, not verdicts.

Start with the inquiry log

Before opening the analytics at all, count the month's inquiries from the business's own records: the calls logged, the forms received, the bookings taken. Count them in three columns — calls, forms, bookings — because they are three different things. A call is immediate and often urgent. A form is considered: the writer has read the page and has a question it did not answer. A booking carries a date and is closest to a sale. Adding the three together loses the information in the split.

The split also says something about the website. Many visits and few calls may mean the phone number is hard to find on a mobile screen. Many calls and few forms may mean the form asks for too much. Forms that never become bookings may mean the site promises something the business cannot schedule. Count the three kinds separately for a month, and the website's weak spot usually names itself. Write the three numbers at the top of the page before reading anything else in the report.

Channels, counted by inquiry

For each inquiry, note where it came from: search, a direct visit, a referral from another site, or a paid campaign. The analytics will show the same split for visits, but the split that matters is the one for inquiries, because the channels behave differently. Search may bring the most visits while direct visits bring the most bookings. A campaign may bring clicks that never call. Count the inquiries per channel, not the visits per channel, and the business will see which channel is earning its keep.

Watch for a channel that brings visits and no inquiries for two months running. That channel is either reaching the wrong people or sending them to the wrong page, and the fix is different in each case: the wrong people means the search terms or the targeting need narrowing, while the wrong page means the landing page needs a clearer next step. Do not judge a channel on one month — a single month in Soddy-Daisy can be moved by the lake season, a holiday, or a storm. Two months of the same pattern is a signal.

Reading past the lake season

Soddy-Daisy sits on Chickamauga Lake, and the lake moves the numbers. Warm months bring more visitors, more searches, more activity of every kind; cold months bring fewer. A business that compares a July to a January will conclude that something broke in the winter, when nothing broke at all — the season changed. The honest comparison is with the same month last year, or with the month before after allowing for the season. The routine should note the season next to the numbers, so next year's reading is not fooled the same way.

The season also changes what the numbers mean. A summer spike in visits from people looking for lake information is not the same as a summer spike in inquiries from people who need the business's service. The routine's job is to separate the two: the visits went up, but did the inquiries go up with them, and from the same pages? If the visits rose and the inquiries did not, the season brought lookers, not buyers, and the pages that brought them are not the pages to fix. If the inquiries rose too, the season brought customers, and the pages that earned them deserve to be studied.

One page fixed before next month

End the routine by fixing one page. Not a redesign, not a rewrite of the whole site — one improvement, finished in the time left over from the checks. The weakest page is usually easy to spot: the service page with visits and no inquiries, the page that still describes a service the business changed, or the page a customer recently said confused them. One page, one fix, and the site is better at the end of the month than it was at the start.

The fix should be the thing the page's visitors were missing. Often it is the next step: a phone number that is easier to tap, a booking link that is visible without scrolling, or a plain answer to the question the page keeps getting asked. Sometimes it is a stale line — a service the business no longer offers, a town it no longer serves. Write down which page was changed and what was changed, so that twelve months from now the business can see twelve pages improved, one at a time, without ever having run a project.
