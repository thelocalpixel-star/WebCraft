A Phone-First Website Plan for Maryville

For a Maryville business, the call is the conversion. A visitor who calls is a visitor who is ready to book, and a website that makes the call easy wins the work. This guide covers the phone-first plan: the ten-second call test, the number on every screen, what the person answering should say, and what happens when nobody is free.

The ten-second call test

Open the website on a phone and count the seconds until a visitor can call. If it takes more than ten, the site is losing work. The test is simple because the customer’s patience is simple: a person with a problem wants to talk to someone who can fix it, and every extra tap is a reason to try the next business on the list instead.

Run the test from every page, not just the homepage. A visitor who lands on a service page from a search should be able to call from that page, without going back to find the number. A visitor who reads the whole page and decides to call should find the number at the end of it, not have to scroll back to the top. Ten seconds, from any page, is the standard.

The number on every screen

The phone number belongs in three places: the top of every page, the bottom of every page, and next to every call to action. It should be a tap-to-call link on a phone, not plain text the visitor has to copy or remember. A number that can be tapped is a call; a number that has to be typed is a chore.

The number should also be the same everywhere — on the website, on the Business Profile, in directories, and on the van. A customer who finds two different numbers will call the wrong one or neither. One number, everywhere, kept current, is the whole system. If the number changes, it changes everywhere on the same day.

What the person answering should say

A phone-first website is only half the plan. The other half is the person who answers. The first twenty seconds decide whether the caller books or hangs up: a greeting with the business name, a question about what they need, and a next step with a time. “Thanks for calling — what can we help with today?” is enough to start, as long as it is followed by a real answer.

The person answering should also know the basics without putting the caller on hold: which services the business offers, which towns it serves, and what the next step is — a visit, an estimate, or a booking. A caller who is put on hold twice will call the next business. A caller who gets an answer and a time will book.

After hours and missed calls

Calls come in after hours, and every missed call is a customer who may not call back. The plan should say what happens then: a voicemail that names the business, says when calls are returned, and asks for the name, the work needed, and a number. A voicemail that says “leave a message” and nothing else is a dead end.

Missed calls should be returned the next working morning, in the order they came in, before anything else. A customer who left a message last night and hears nothing by noon has already called someone else. The return call should open with the reason they called — “You left a message about a fence repair” — so the customer knows they were heard, not just dialled.

Proof next to the button

A call button works better with proof next to it. A line under the number — the towns served, the hours, or a sentence about how quickly calls are returned — tells the visitor the call will be answered by someone who can help. “Call now — we answer until 6pm and return missed calls the next morning” is proof. A bare number is a guess.

Proof can also be the simplest kind: the business name, the town, and the service, right next to the button. A visitor in Maryville who sees a Maryville number next to the words “fence repair in Maryville” knows they have the right business. The button and the proof belong together, on every page, because the visitor decides in seconds whether to tap.
