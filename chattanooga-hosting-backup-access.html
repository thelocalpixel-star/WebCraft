Chattanooga Hosting Backups and Access

A small business website usually has one person who touches it. A Chattanooga business often has three: an employee who updates the hours, a designer who changes the pages, and an owner who approves both. More hands mean more ways to lose the site — an update that breaks the form, a change nobody remembers making, a login that belongs to someone who left. This guide covers backups and access for a website more than one person can change.

More hands, more ways to lose the site

A website with one editor breaks in one way at a time. A website with three editors breaks in three ways at once, and nobody knows which change caused it. An employee updates the hours and the form stops sending. A designer changes a page and the phone number disappears. The owner approves a new offer and the old one is still on the homepage. Each change is small, each person meant well, and the site is wrong in three places by the end of the week — and nobody can say when any of it happened.

The problem is not the people. It is that nobody wrote anything down. A business with more than one person touching its website needs a record of who changed what and when, or it will spend its time undoing changes it cannot trace. The record does not need to be fancy. A shared note, one line per change — the date, the person, and what changed — is enough to answer the only question that matters when something breaks: what changed last.

The change log nobody keeps

The change log is a short document, kept where everyone who touches the site can see it. Every change gets one line: the date, who made it, and what they changed. “Nov 4 — Maria — updated holiday hours.” “Nov 9 — designer — replaced homepage photo.” The log takes thirty seconds per change, and it turns a mystery into a list. When the form stops working, the business looks at the last three lines instead of guessing.

The log also settles the arguments that eat a business’s time. Did the hours change before or after the customer called. Was the price updated before the quote went out. Without a log, those questions are arguments. With a log, they are answers. A Chattanooga business with three people on its website should treat the change log the way it treats the till: counted, written down, and checked when something does not add up.

Backups before changes, not after

The rule for a site with more than one editor is simple: back up before a change, not after. Whoever is about to change the site — the employee, the designer, the owner — takes a fresh copy first, makes the change, and checks the site immediately after. If the change breaks something, the restore takes minutes, because the backup is from an hour ago. A business that changes first and backs up later is gambling that nothing will break, and with three people making changes, the odds of that bet get worse every week.

The backup itself has two parts, and both matter: the files, which are the design and the pages, and the content, which is the words, the form entries and the settings. A backup that holds only the files can rebuild the look of the site, but not the words. A backup that holds only the content can rebuild the words, but not the look. The business needs both, from the same date, stored somewhere the host does not control — a folder on the office computer, updated once a month, is a second copy.

Access levels: who can edit, who can break, who can restore

Not everyone who touches a website should be able to do everything to it. The employee who updates the hours needs to edit text, not to install plugins or change the hosting. The designer needs to change the design, not to hold the only copy of the domain password. The owner needs to be able to reach everything, including the hosting account and the domain registrar, even if they never log in. Access should match the job, and the person with the most access should be the person the business trusts with the keys, not the person who happened to set the site up.

The practical version is a short document, kept where the owner can find it: the hosting provider’s name, the account email, the domain registrar, and who holds each password — in a password manager the business controls, with a second person able to reach it. Two people should be able to reach the account, not one. When a person leaves, the business changes one password in the manager and the handover is done, instead of spending a week locked out of its own website.

The monthly restore test, with a second person

A backup that has never been restored is a promise, not a plan. Once a month, someone should test it — and the test should be done by a second person, not the one who took the backup. Restore the copy to a test address and check that the pages load, the form sends, and the phone number is right. If the restore works, the business has a backup. If it does not, the business has learned something cheaply instead of expensively, on a quiet day instead of the morning the site is down.

The monthly test also catches the slow decay nobody notices day to day: the backup that stopped running when the card expired, the password that changed and was never written down, the domain renewal date creeping closer. Ten minutes, once a month, with a second person watching, is the cheapest insurance a shared website has. The businesses that do it never need it urgently. The businesses that do not, need it all at once.
