A remote project is normal — the shape is what matters

An Athens business hiring a web designer will usually work with someone they never meet in person. WebCraft itself works this way, based near Cleveland and serving businesses remotely across the region. That is normal now, and it works well — as long as the project has a clear shape. The shape is a written brief, a draft you can open on your phone, a review round with written changes, and a handoff that leaves you owning the site.

What a remote project must not be is a black box: money goes in, a website comes out weeks later, and nobody can say what happened in between. If a designer cannot describe the steps — brief, draft, review, launch, handoff — the distance is not the problem. The missing process is.

The first call: what to have ready

Have four things ready before the first call. The services you want inquiries about, in your own words. Photos of real work, if you have them, and the names of the towns you serve around Athens. The one action you want a visitor to take — usually a phone call or a quote request. And one or two websites you like, with a sentence on what you like about each. A designer who hears those four things can quote a real job instead of guessing.

Be ready for the designer to ask about content. Most small-business projects wait on words and photos, not on design. If you can send the service list and a few photos in the first week, the project moves. If the content waits, the launch waits with it, whatever the quoted timeline said.

Reviewing a draft without a meeting

The draft usually arrives as a link. Open it on your phone first, not your desktop — most of your customers will meet the site on a phone. Check the service list against the brief. Check the area wording: does it name Athens and the towns you actually serve, or does it promise places you cannot reach? Tap the phone number and send a test message through the form, and confirm it arrives.

Send changes back as a written list, page by page, not as a phone call from memory. “On the services page, change the second paragraph to…” beats a twenty-minute call nobody wrote down. Two rounds of changes are a normal part of a fixed-price project; ask how many are included before you agree. A five-page plan for Athens gives the draft a shape worth reviewing.

Ownership and handoff, in writing

Remote or not, the ownership questions are the same. The domain should be registered in your name. The hosting account should be one you can log into. The website files and the written content should be yours to keep and move. At launch you should receive a handoff: logins, files, and a short note on what runs where and who edits it.

Ask what happens if you stop the monthly care. With WebCraft the $20-a-month care is optional; if you stop it, the site stays yours and you arrange hosting. Other providers differ — some keep the site if you cancel the plan. The answer matters more than the price, because it decides whether you bought a website or rented one.

The questions that protect a small budget

A small budget needs a fixed price, not an hourly meter. Ask what the fixed price includes: how many pages, how many rounds of changes, a contact form, inquiry tracking, and the handoff. Ask what costs extra, and get both lists in writing. Then ask what the timeline depends on — usually your content and photos — and agree on who sends what by when.

The cheapest remote quote is rarely the cheapest website. A $500 one-time site you own, with optional $20-a-month care, costs less over two years than most monthly plans, and you can walk away with the site in your hands. The brief, the draft review and the handoff are what make a remote project safe. Insist on all three, and the miles between you and the designer stop mattering.
