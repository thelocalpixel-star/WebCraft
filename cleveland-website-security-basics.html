Cleveland Website Security Basics

A small business website in Cleveland holds more than most owners realise: customer names, inquiry details, appointment requests, and the reputation the business spent years building. Security for a site like that is not a product to buy once. It is four habits, kept up month after month: a secure connection, software that is updated, logins that are strong, and backups that have actually been tested.

HTTPS: the padlock is the front door

A website without HTTPS sends everything between the visitor and the site in plain text, where anyone on the same network can read it. A website with HTTPS encrypts that connection, and the browser shows a padlock to say so.

Chrome has marked sites without HTTPS as Not secure since 2018, and a visitor who sees that warning on a business website has a reason to leave before reading a word.

The certificate that makes HTTPS work is free. Let's Encrypt issues certificates at no cost, and most hosting providers install and renew them automatically. The business should still check: open the site, look for the padlock, and click it to see the certificate is current.

Certificates do not last forever — lifetimes are shortening, to 200 days from March 15, 2026 — so a site whose certificate is renewed automatically is a site that will not quietly go insecure one weekend. A certificate is not a guarantee the site is safe.

It is the front door being locked, and a locked front door is where security starts.

Updates: the habit that prevents most break-ins

Most small business websites are not hacked by a genius. They are hacked through software that was not updated: a content system, a plugin, or a theme with a known flaw that was fixed months ago and never installed.

The attacker does not pick the business; the attacker scans the internet for the flaw, and the un-updated site is the one that answers.

The habit is simple: update the site's software when updates appear, and remove what the site does not use. A plugin installed three years ago for a feature the site no longer has is not harmless clutter; it is a door nobody is watching.

If the business pays for hosting and care, the updates should be part of that care, and the owner should be able to ask when they were last done and get a date, not a shrug. A site that is updated is a site that has closed the doors the attackers already know about.

Strong logins, and who holds the keys

A website login should be long, unique, and held by as few people as possible. The admin password should not be the business name, the town, or a password reused from an email account.

Each person who can change the site should have their own login, so that when someone leaves — an employee, a contractor, a former designer — their access can be removed without changing everyone else's password.

Two-factor authentication, where the login needs a code from the owner's phone as well as the password, is the single cheapest upgrade a small business can make. A stolen password alone is no longer enough to get in.

The business should also know who holds the keys: the domain account, the hosting account, and the website login are three different doors, and the owner should hold all three, or know exactly who does. A business that cannot log in to its own domain does not own its website in any way that matters.

Backups that have actually been tested

A backup the business has never restored is a hope, not a backup. The site should be backed up regularly — the files and the content together — and the backup should live somewhere other than the same server as the site, so that a failure of the server does not take the backup with it.

A backup on the same machine that hosts the site protects against a deleted page. It does not protect against a dead server.

Once or twice a year, the business should test the restore: take the backup, put it on a test copy of the site, and check that the pages, the forms, and the images all come back.

The test will find the problems while there is still time to fix them — a backup that stopped running months ago, a backup that is missing the images, a backup that nobody knows how to restore. A tested backup is the difference between a bad day and a lost website.

The security layer, distinct from the rest

Security is one layer of looking after a website, and it is not the same as the others. Backups are about recovery after something goes wrong; access control is about who can change the site; security is about stopping the wrong person getting in at all.

A business that confuses the layers will spend money on the wrong one: a backup plan is not a substitute for updates, and strong passwords are not a substitute for a backup.

Keep a short, written record: when the certificate was last checked, when the software was last updated, who holds each login, and when the backup was last tested. Four lines, reviewed once a month, will catch most of what goes wrong with a small business website before a customer does. Security is not a project with an end date.

It is a set of habits, and a Cleveland business that keeps them will have a website that stays up, stays trusted, and stays theirs.
