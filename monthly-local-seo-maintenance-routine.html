A Monthly Local SEO Maintenance Routine

Local search does not stay fixed once it is set up. Hours change, services change, photos go stale, and a review left unanswered sits where every searcher can see it. None of that needs a project. It needs thirty minutes, once a month, in the same order every time. This is that routine: the details, the hours, the photos, the reviews, and one page made better — then stop.

Start with the details

Open the website and read the business name, address, and phone number exactly as they appear, then check the same details everywhere else the business appears: the search listing, the social profiles, the directories that matter.

The details must match word for word, because a search engine that sees two different phone numbers or two spellings of a street has to guess which one is right, and a customer who sees two different numbers has to guess too. A mismatch found in this check is the most valuable thing the thirty minutes can produce.

Fix what is wrong at the source, not just on the website. If the listing has an old number, change the listing; if a directory has an old address, correct the directory or ask for the correction.

Then check the website footer and the contact page, where stale details hide longest, because nobody reads the footer until a customer complains that the number does not work. Write down every correction made, with the date, so next month's check starts from a known state instead of from memory.

Hours, services, and photos

Read the hours on the website and compare them with the hours on the listing and the hours on the door. Hours change more often than any other detail — a seasonal change, a Saturday added, a holiday closure — and they are the detail a customer checks right before driving over.

A customer who arrives to a closed door because the website said open will not check the website again. If the hours are about to change, put the change on the website before it takes effect, not after.

Then read the services list the way a stranger would: does it still describe the work the business actually wants? Remove the service that was dropped, add the one that was taken on, and fix any service page that describes work the business no longer does.

Photos get the same treatment: remove the photo of a job from years ago if it no longer represents the work, and add one recent photo the business has permission to show. A photo of real, recent work is worth more than a gallery that has not changed in two years.

Reviews: ask, answer, and learn

Reviews are the part of local search the business does not control and cannot ignore. Each month, ask one recent customer for a review — one, not ten, because one honest request a month builds a steady stream without turning the business into a nuisance.

Ask the customer who was pleased, soon after the job, and make the request easy: a short message with a direct link, and no pressure to say anything in particular. A review that is earned this way reads like it was earned, because it was.

Answer every new review, good or bad, and answer it promptly. A thank-you for a good review takes a minute and shows the next reader that the business is paying attention.

A bad review needs a calm, short reply that acknowledges the problem and offers a way to make it right, without arguing and without discussing the customer's private details in public. Then read the reviews together: if three customers mention the same thing — slow replies, unclear pricing, a confusing booking step — that is not three complaints.

It is one fix, named three times, and it belongs on next month's list.

Improve one page

Every month, pick one page and make it better in exactly one way. Not a redesign, not a rewrite of the whole site — one improvement, finished in the time left over from the checks.

The weakest page is usually easy to spot: the service page with visits and no inquiries, the page that still describes a service the business changed, or the page a customer recently said confused them. One page, one fix, and the site is better at the end of the month than it was at the start.

The fix should be the thing the page's visitors were missing. Often it is the next step: a phone number that is easier to tap, a booking link that is visible without scrolling, or a plain answer to the question the page keeps getting asked.

Sometimes it is a stale line — an old price signal, a service the business no longer offers, a town it no longer serves. Write down which page was changed and what was changed, so that twelve months from now the business can see twelve pages improved, one at a time, without ever having run a project.

Write it down, then stop

Keep the routine on one page: the date, the details checked, the corrections made, the review asked for, and the page improved. Five lines, once a month, and the record becomes the business's own history of local search — what was fixed, when, and whether the inquiries moved afterwards.

Without the record, the routine is a feeling; with it, the routine is evidence, and the business can see which months helped and which months were just busy.

Then stop. The routine is thirty minutes because local search rewards consistency, not obsession. Checking the rankings every day will not move them, and changing the website every week will not help the business learn what worked. Do the routine, write it down, and go back to the work the business actually sells.

The site will be checked again next month, in the same order, and the small improvements will keep adding up while the business gets on with serving customers.
