A Website for a Cleveland Salon

A salon website has one job above all others: get the client into the right chair at the right time. Everything else — the photos, the service menu, the stylist pages — exists to make that booking easier and to make sure the client who books is booking the right thing. A salon site that puts booking first, and answers the price and time questions honestly, will book more chairs than a prettier site that hides both.

Booking first: service, then stylist, then time

The booking path should follow the order a client thinks in. First the service: a cut, a colour, a bridal trial, a manicure. Then the stylist, if the client has a preference, or anyone available, if they do not. Then the time.

A booking flow that asks for the time before the service has it backwards, and a client who has to start over because the stylist they wanted is not free on the day they picked will often not start over at all.

Each step should show what the client needs to decide. The service step should show the time the service takes and the price it starts from, so the client is not surprised at the chair.

The stylist step should show who does what — not every stylist does every service, and a client booking a colour specialist for a cut they do not do is a booking that will have to be moved. The time step should show real availability, not a request form that promises a callback.

A client who books online expects the booking to be real.

A service menu with from prices and times

A salon service menu should list every service the salon actually offers, with the time it takes and the price it starts from. The word from matters: a colour service that starts at one price and rises with length, thickness, or extra work should say so, so the client who books a long colour is not surprised at the till.

A menu that hides prices entirely does not create mystery. It creates a client who books somewhere that does show them.

Keep the menu honest about what is included. If a cut includes a wash and a finish, say so. If a colour service includes a toner and a blow-dry, say so. If a treatment is an add-on, say so, and say what it adds.

The clients who read the menu carefully are the ones who arrive with the right expectations, and the right expectations are what turn a first visit into a regular booking.

Stylist pages and the colour consultation

A stylist page should show the stylist's work, their specialties, and the services they actually perform. A client choosing between stylists wants to see real photos of real work — cuts, colours, and finishes the stylist has done — not a stock photo of a model.

The page should also say how long the stylist has been doing the work, and whether they take new clients, because a page that sends a new client to a stylist with a closed book wastes the client's time.

Colour deserves its own path. A client booking a colour for the first time should be offered a consultation, and the website should explain what that is: a short visit where the stylist sees the hair, talks through the result, and checks whether a patch test is needed.

A patch test — a small skin test before a colour service — is a safety step some clients need, and the website should explain it plainly, without alarming anyone: it is a routine check, it takes a few minutes, and it protects the client. A salon that explains this clearly is a salon a careful client trusts.

Deposits, cancellations, and real photos

A deposit and cancellation policy should be on the website, in plain words, before the client books — not discovered at the chair. If a colour appointment needs a deposit, say how much, when it is taken, and when it is refunded or moved. If a late cancellation carries a charge, say so, and say how much notice avoids it.

Clients accept fair policies they can see; they resent policies they discover after the fact.

Photos are the salon's proof, and they should be real. A gallery of the salon's own work, on real clients who have agreed to be shown, will book more appointments than a grid of stock images ever will.

The photos should be labelled honestly — a stock photo, if one is used for a style the salon has not done yet, should be labelled as an example, not presented as the salon's work. A client who arrives expecting the photo and gets something different will not come back, and will tell people why.

Hours that match the listing, and a next step

The hours on the website must match the hours everywhere else the salon appears — the search listing, the social profile, the door. A client who drives across Cleveland for a Saturday appointment the website said was available, and finds a closed door, is a client lost for good.

Check the hours on the site every time they change, and check them against the listing at the same time.

End every page with the next step. The service menu should end with the booking button. The stylist page should end with that stylist's availability. The home page should end with the phone number and the hours. A salon website is not a brochure; it is the front desk, and the front desk's job is to take the booking.

Make it easy, make it honest, and the chairs will fill.
