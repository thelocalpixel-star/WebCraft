Sweetwater Hosting Backups That Work

A business can have ten backups and still lose its website, if all ten sit in the same place and nobody has ever tried to restore one. A Sweetwater business needs one backup that works: the files and the words, from this month, held somewhere the host does not control — and a restore that has been tried once, on a quiet day, before it is ever needed. This guide covers that one backup, how to make it, and the two mistakes that make all the others useless.

The backup that matters is the one you can restore

A host that says it keeps backups is making a claim the business cannot verify without trying. The only backup that counts is one that has been restored — to a test address, on a quiet day — and shown to work: the pages load, the form sends, the phone number is right. A backup that has never been restored is a promise, and promises are cheap. The test takes half an hour, once, and it turns a promise into a fact the business can rely on.

The test should also answer two questions the sales page never does: how far back does the backup go, and how long does a restore take. A backup from yesterday is useful when today’s update breaks the site. A backup from a month ago is useful when the break is discovered a month late. A restore that takes a day is useful when the business can wait a day; a restore that takes a week is a week without a website. Ask both questions, write the answers down, and test the restore before the answers matter.

Files and words: back up both, from the same day

A website has two parts, and a backup that holds only one of them rebuilds only half a site. The files are the design, the pages and the images — what the visitor sees. The words are the content, the form entries and the settings — what the business has written and collected. A backup of the files alone can rebuild the look of the site, but not the words; a backup of the words alone can rebuild the words, but not the look. The business needs both, from the same date, or the restored site is a stranger to its own customers.

For a small Sweetwater site, the practical rule is one automatic backup from the host, and one copy the business holds itself — a download of the files and a copy of the content, taken on the same day, stored on the office computer or in a folder the business controls. Two copies, two places, same week. That is enough. Ten copies in one place is not, because one place can fail all at once, and a host that loses the site can lose its backups with it.

Somewhere the host does not control

The second copy must live somewhere the host does not control, because the most common reason a business needs a backup is a problem with the host itself — a failed server, a billing dispute, an account locked by mistake. A backup held on the same server as the website is a copy of the thing that just broke, in the place that just broke it. It is the equivalent of keeping the spare key inside the locked house.

The somewhere else does not need to be fancy. A folder on the office computer, updated once a month, is a second copy. A USB drive in a drawer is a second copy. What matters is that the business can reach it without the host’s help, on a day the host is not answering. The copy should also include the login details — the hosting account, the domain registrar, and the email the account is registered under — because a backup without the keys to the account is a file the business cannot use.

The update that breaks the site — and the backup that fixes it

The most common day a Sweetwater business needs its backup is the day of an update. A plugin is updated, a theme is updated, the platform itself is updated — and a page breaks, a form stops sending, or the whole site shows an error. The fix is a restore, and the restore is only as good as the last backup. A backup from yesterday fixes an update from today. A backup from three months ago fixes it by losing three months of changes.

The habit that prevents the problem is small: back up before updating, not after. Whoever runs the update — the owner, an employee, or the person on the care plan — should take a fresh copy first, run the update, and check the site immediately after. If the update breaks something, the restore takes minutes, because the backup is from an hour ago. A business that updates without a fresh backup is gambling that nothing will break, and the odds of that bet get worse every year the site gets older.

Once a month, ten minutes, same day

Backups decay, because the website changes and the backup does not. A backup from January is a backup of a website the business no longer has, once the business has changed a price, added a page, or taken on a new service. The routine is therefore monthly, and it takes ten minutes: download the files and the content, check that the download is from this month, and store it where the host does not control it. Same day each month, same ten minutes, same folder.

The monthly ten minutes also catches the slow problems no one notices day to day — the backup that stopped running when the card expired, the hosting plan that renewed at a higher price, the domain renewal date creeping closer. A business that spends ten minutes a month on its backup never needs it urgently. A business that never spends it needs it all at once, on the worst possible day.
