Reading Your First Analytics Report in Murphy

A Murphy business that opens its first analytics report will see small numbers. That is not a problem with the report; it is the honest shape of a small town in North Carolina's far west, where the customer base is Cherokee County and the nearby towns of Andrews, Hayesville and Brasstown, not a metro area. Small numbers still speak, if they are read the right way. This guide is about reading a first report when the numbers are small, the seasons move them, and every inquiry matters enough to count by hand.

Small numbers are still numbers

The first mistake a small-town business makes with analytics is comparing its numbers with a big city's. A Murphy site will not have thousands of visits a month, and it does not need them: a handful of inquiries a month, from the right towns, is a working website in a town this size. The report's job is not to impress. It is to answer three questions: how many people asked for something, where did they come from, and which page were they reading when they decided to call. Those questions have answers at any size.

The second mistake is reading the small numbers as if they were precise. On a small site, one visitor can move a percentage by ten points, and one good week can double a month. A bounce rate of eighty percent might be four visitors out of five, and a visit total that doubles might be six people instead of three. Read the counts, not the percentages, and read them over three months before drawing a conclusion. A small report read patiently teaches more than a big report read in a hurry.

Count the inquiries by hand first

Before opening the report, count the month's inquiries from the business's own records: the calls, the forms, the bookings. In a town the size of Murphy, that count can be done by hand, from memory and the phone log, and it should be — the hand count is the truth, and the report is only an explanation of it. Write the number down, split into calls, forms and bookings, because they are three different things: a call is immediate, a form is considered, a booking carries a date.

Then open the report and ask what explains the count. If the inquiries rose, which pages earned them? If they fell, did the business stop being found, or is it still being found and the page stopped converting? The report can answer both questions, but only if the business knows the inquiry count before it opens the report. A report read without the count will be read as a scoreboard, and a small-town scoreboard always looks like losing.

The seasons move a mountain town's numbers

Murphy's numbers move with the seasons, and the routine has to see past that. Warm months bring visitors to the mountains; cold months bring fewer. A business that compares a July to a January will conclude that something broke in the winter, when nothing broke at all — the season changed. The honest comparison is with the same month last year, or with the month before after allowing for the season. Note the season next to the numbers, so next year's reading is not fooled the same way.

The season also changes who the visitors are. Summer brings people from outside Cherokee County — travellers, second-home owners, people passing through — and their visits are not the same as a neighbour's visit. A summer spike in visits from far away that brings no inquiries is not growth; it is lookers. A summer spike in inquiries from the towns the business serves is growth. The report can tell the two apart by geography, but only if the business checks where the visitors came from instead of only how many there were.

The pages that earn, and the pages that only collect

Look at the pages the inquiries came through, and the pages that had visits and no inquiries. The analytics will show which pages visitors landed on and which pages they visited before contacting the business, and the two lists are not the same. The page that earned the inquiry is the one that carried the next step the visitor used, and that page deserves to be studied and copied. The pages with visits and no inquiries are the ones to fix.

Open each no-inquiry page and read it as a stranger would: is the next step visible without scrolling, does the page answer the question its title promises, does it say which towns the business serves? A Murphy service page that hides the phone number, or that talks about the business instead of the job the searcher typed, will keep collecting visits and losing callers. Fix one page, one change, and leave it alone for a month. On a small site there are only a handful of pages that matter, and each one gets its turn.

What to ignore, and what to keep

Ignore the totals that flatter: page views that rise when one visitor reads ten pages, the real-time count that is a snapshot, the bounce rate on a handful of visits. Ignore the visit total on its own, which rises for reasons that have nothing to do with the business. Keep the three numbers that answer the questions: the inquiries, the channels that brought them, and the pages that earned them. Keep them in one place, in the same order, on the same day each month.

Keep the record, too. Five lines a month — the date, the inquiry count by kind, the channels, the pages that earned inquiries, the one page to fix — and after a year the business has its own history: which months the inquiries rose, what changed in those months, and whether the change was the page, the season, or the channel. A Murphy business that keeps that record knows its own market, however small the numbers look. A business that does not is guessing, however good the charts look.
