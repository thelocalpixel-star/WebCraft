A Name, Address and Phone Consistency Audit

A business has one name, one address and one phone number, but Google may hold five versions of each, collected from directories, old websites and customer mentions. This guide covers how to audit the listings you actually control, find the mismatches, and fix them in the order that matters, without paying for a service that promises to fix listings you never created.

What NAP means, and why three lines matter

NAP is short for name, address and phone number, and it is the way Google decides whether the business it shows is the business the customer is looking for. When the name, address or phone number differs between the website, the Business Profile and a directory, Google has to guess which version is right. A business that makes Google guess is a business Google shows less often, because showing the wrong number to a customer is worse than showing nothing at all.

The same confusion hits customers directly. A person who finds one phone number on Google and a different one on the website does not know which to call, and many will call neither. A customer who drives to an old address listed on a directory has wasted a trip, and will not come back to check whether the new address is right. Three lines of text, kept consistent, prevent both problems at once.

Start with the listings you control

The first step is to list the places where your business details appear and that you can actually change. That list is shorter than most people expect: your own website, your Google Business Profile if you have one, your Facebook page, and any directories where you created the listing yourself. Those are the listings that matter, because those are the ones a customer is most likely to find and the ones you can fix today.

Do not start with the directories you never created. There are hundreds of listing sites that scrape business details from each other, and chasing all of them is a job with no end. A customer does not find a business through a directory nobody has heard of; they find it through Google, through the website, and through the one or two directories that matter in their trade. Fix what you control first, and the scraped copies usually follow the corrected source over time.

The audit: one spreadsheet, three columns

Open a spreadsheet with three columns — name, address, phone — and one row for each listing you control. Write down exactly what each listing says, character for character, including abbreviations and suite numbers. Do not write down what you think it says, and do not write down what it should say. The audit is a record of reality, not a wish list, and it only works when the entries are exact.

The mismatches will usually fall into a few patterns. The business name appears with and without a legal suffix, or with a town name added in one place and not another. The address uses “Street” in one listing and “St” in another, or includes a suite number that the others omit. The phone number is the old landline on one listing and the mobile on another. Each pattern is a decision to make once, then apply everywhere — one name, one address, one phone, written the same way every time.

Fix in order: your site, your profile, then the rest

Fix your own website first, because it is the source Google trusts most for a business that has one. The name, address and phone number should appear the same way in the footer of every page and on the contact page, and the phone number should be a link a visitor can tap. A website that contradicts itself — one number in the header and another in the footer — fails the audit before Google ever looks at a directory.

Fix the Business Profile second, because it is what searchers see first. Then fix Facebook and the directories you created, one at a time, working from the ones customers actually use. Do not pay a service to fix listings you never created; the money is better spent on the listings that bring customers. When the places you control all agree, the places you do not control matter far less.

The monthly check that keeps it fixed

Consistency is not a one-time job, because details change: a phone number moves, a suite number is added, a business changes its name. The monthly check takes about ten minutes. Search for the business name and phone number, open the first five results, and compare the three lines against the spreadsheet. If something has changed, update the spreadsheet first, then the listing — the spreadsheet is the single source of truth, and every listing should match it.

The check also catches a problem that no audit can prevent: a customer or a competitor suggesting an edit to a public listing. Google accepts suggested edits, and a wrong one can sit unnoticed for months. A monthly look at the top results catches it early, when the fix is one correction instead of a month of lost calls. Ten minutes a month is a small price for a phone number that always works.
