A Murphy Hosting Renewal Checklist

A Murphy business that pays for a website has two renewals, not one: the hosting, which keeps the site online, and the domain, which is the address customers type to find it. They renew on different dates, at different prices, and losing track of either one causes a different kind of trouble. This guide is the once-a-year check for both of them, written down so the business does it in twenty minutes and then forgets about it for another year.

Two renewals, not one — and different consequences

Hosting and the domain name are two different purchases, and they renew separately. The hosting renews with the company that stores the website files; the domain renews with the registrar that holds the name. A business can lose the hosting and still own the name, or lose the name and still have the hosting — and losing the name is worse, because the name is the address every customer, every listing and every search result points to. A lapsed domain can be bought by someone else while the business is still paying for hosting it cannot reach.

The first step of the yearly check is therefore to write down both dates, with both prices, in the same place: the hosting renewal date and price, and the domain renewal date and price, with the name of the company that holds each one. If the business does not know one of those dates, that is the first thing to find out — by logging in to the account, or by asking the person who set it up. A date nobody knows is a date that will arrive as a surprise.

The auto-renew trap for a business that looks once a year

Both renewals usually renew themselves, which is convenient until the price changes. A hosting plan bought at an introductory price renews at a higher one, and the renewal notice often arrives as an email the business never opens, because it looks like the same email as last year. A Murphy business that looks at its hosting once a year can spend years paying a renewal price it never agreed to, simply because the plan renewed itself while nobody was watching.

The check is to read the renewal notice properly, once a year, and compare it with the first year’s price. What did the first term cost, what does this renewal cost, and when does the money leave the account. If the renewal is a surprise, the business has learned something about its host — and about its own bookkeeping. A plan that renews automatically at a price that was never stated plainly deserves a second look before the next renewal, not after it.

What the business actually used this year

Before deciding whether the renewal price is fair, the business should ask what it actually used this year. How many email boxes does it have, and does anyone read them all. How much storage does the site use, and has it grown. Did the business call support this year, and did support answer. A plan that covers email, storage and support the business actually used is a plan earning its price. A plan that charges for boxes nobody reads and storage the site will never fill is not.

The usage check also catches the slow drift nobody notices: the email box for an employee who left two years ago, the storage taken up by images from a redesign, the backup that stopped running when the card expired. The renewal is the one day a year the business is supposed to look, and the host is supposed to answer. Twenty minutes, once a year, is the whole job — and a business that does it will never again pay for a service it stopped using.

The price check: first year against this year

Put the first year’s price next to this year’s renewal price and ask what changed. If the price rose and the coverage rose with it — more storage the business needed, support that answered faster — the renewal may be fair. If the price rose and nothing changed, the business should ask the host why, or look at what else is available. WebCraft’s own arrangement is stated plainly: a website costs $500 once, and hosting and care together are $20 a month if the business wants them. That $20 covers the hosting and someone keeping the site running, and a business comparing plans should ask each one the same question: what does the monthly price leave out.

The comparison should be about coverage, not price alone. One plan at a few dollars a month covers a place to store the files and nothing else; another at a higher price covers the files, the updates, the backups and someone to call. The cheapest plan that covers the jobs nobody in the business will do is usually the right one. The most expensive plan is usually a waste of money the business could spend on the site itself.

The renewal-day routine, written down

The routine, once a year, in order: read the renewal notices for the hosting and the domain, and write down both dates and both prices. Check the domain is registered in the business’s name, with a business email address, at a registrar the business can reach directly. Check what the business used this year — email boxes, storage, support — and cross out what it did not use. Decide whether to renew, renegotiate or move, and write the decision down with the date.

Then set the same check for next year, on the same day, and store the answers with the login details, in the same place. A year from now, when the renewal letter arrives with a higher price, those answers are what decide whether to renew or move — not the renewal price alone, and not the sales page the business read a year ago. Hosting is a small monthly decision that becomes a large one only when nobody looks at it for years.
