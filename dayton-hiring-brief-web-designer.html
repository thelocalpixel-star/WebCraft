A Dayton Hiring Brief: Warning Signs in a Web Design Quote

Most bad website projects do not start with a bad designer. They start with a quote that never answered the important questions — who owns the site, what the monthly fee covers, and what happens when the work stops. This guide names the five warning signs to spot in a Dayton web design quote before you sign it.

By WebCraft · Published November 10, 2026 · Hiring guide

The quote that hides the monthly cost

The first warning sign is a quote with two prices: a small number in large type and a monthly fee in the fine print. A website sold for $99 a month costs $1,188 a year, every year — and a $500 one-time site with optional $20-a-month care costs at most $740 in year one. Neither number is dishonest on its own. The problem is a quote that shows you one and hides the other. Ask for the year-one and year-two totals in writing before anything else.

The same test applies to setup fees, “free” first months and discounted introductions. A first month free is a real saving only if the following eleven months are priced in the open. Ask for the full twelve-month cost, and the cost of leaving, in the same document as the headline price.

No ownership in writing

The second warning sign is a quote that never says who owns the site. The domain, the hosting account, the website files and the written content should all be yours — in writing, before you pay. If the quote does not mention ownership, assume the worst and ask directly: if I cancel the monthly plan, does the website stay mine, and can I move it to another host?

A designer who answers clearly — “you own the domain, the files and the content, and here is the handoff list” — has nothing to hide. A designer who changes the subject to design trends has answered the question anyway. Ownership is not a detail to sort out after launch; after launch it is too late.

A deadline with no draft to review

The third warning sign is a launch date with no review step. A project that goes from payment to launch without you seeing the site is a project you cannot steer. You should see a draft on your own phone before it goes live, and you should have at least one round of written changes included in the price. Ask when the draft arrives and how changes are handled — and be suspicious of a timeline that has no answer.

Review the draft the way a customer would: find the phone number, read the service list, check the towns named, and send a test inquiry through the form. A Dayton business serves real places; the draft should name the ones you can reach and none you cannot.

A price that changes when you ask what it includes

The fourth warning sign is a price that moves every time you ask a question. How many pages? How many rounds of changes? Photos, a contact form, inquiry tracking, the handoff — each answer should already be in the quote. A fixed price that grows with every clarification was never a fixed price; it was an opening number.

Get the inclusions in writing: pages, revisions, photos, forms, tracking, handoff, and what costs extra. Then compare quotes on the same list. A guide to planning a Dayton advertising budget makes the same point for ads: agree the scope before the spending starts.

The check before you sign

Before you sign, run the five-question check. What do I own, in writing? What does year one cost, and year two? When do I see a draft, and how many changes are included? What happens if I stop the monthly payment? And what do I receive at handoff? Five clear answers, in writing, and the quote is worth signing. Vague answers are not a negotiation — they are a reason to walk away.

Dayton is a small market, and a bad website project costs a small business more than a big one: the money, the months, and the customers who found a broken site first. A quote that survives these five questions is a quote from a designer who has thought about your business, not just their price. Choose that one.
