A Pigeon Forge Website Redesign Decision

A Pigeon Forge website built years ago was built for a visitor sitting at a desktop, planning a trip weeks ahead. Today the visitor is on a phone, already in town, trying to book for tonight. The redesign question is not whether the site looks old — it is whether visitors who start a booking finish one. This guide covers the booking-flow test, what a refresh can fix, and what only a rebuild can fix.

The booking-flow test

Open the website on a phone and try to book. Count the steps: find the date, pick the time, choose the number of people, enter details, pay. Each step is a place a visitor can leave, and on a phone each step costs more patience than it does on a desktop. A booking flow with seven steps on a phone is a booking flow that loses visitors at step three — and the business never hears from them.

The test is not about how the site looks. A site can look old and book well, if the flow is short and the buttons are big enough to tap. A site can look new and book badly, if the flow asks for an account before it shows a price. The redesign decision starts with the flow, because the flow is where the money is lost.

Measure starts against completions

The number that decides the redesign is the gap between booking starts and booking completions. If a hundred visitors start a booking and ninety finish, the flow works and the site does not need a rebuild. If a hundred start and thirty finish, the flow is broken, and no amount of new photos will fix it. Analytics can show this gap, and a month of data is usually enough to see it clearly.

The gap also says where the problem is. If visitors leave at the date picker, the calendar is the problem. If they leave at the payment step, the payment options are the problem. If they leave at the account-creation step, the account is the problem — and removing it is a repair, not a redesign. Measure first, and the fix usually names itself.

What a refresh can fix on a booking site

A refresh fixes the flow without rebuilding the site. Shorten the steps: show the price before asking for details, make the date picker work with a thumb, let the visitor book without creating an account. Add a call button on every step, so a visitor who gets stuck can call instead of leaving. Those changes are a refresh, and they are usually the cheapest way to close the gap between starts and completions.

A refresh also keeps what the old site earned: the pages that rank, the links that point to them, and the booking history. A business that refreshes instead of rebuilding keeps its search visibility and its customers’ trust. A rebuild throws both away and has to earn them back, which takes months a seasonal business does not have.

What only a rebuild can fix

A rebuild is the answer when the structure itself is the problem. The site was built on a platform that cannot take mobile bookings at all, the pages cannot be found by search engines, or the booking system is a separate tool that the website cannot talk to. No amount of new photos fixes a site that cannot take a booking on a phone.

A rebuild is also the answer when the business has changed so much that the old site describes a business that no longer exists — new attractions, new prices, new ways of taking bookings. But a rebuild should keep what the old site earned: the domain, the pages that rank, the content that works, and the logins. A rebuild that throws those away is not a fresh start; it is a step backwards.

The decision, written down before you spend

Before spending anything, write the decision down in one paragraph: what is wrong, what the fix is, what it will cost, and how you will know it worked. “Visitors start bookings on phones and leave at the payment step. A refresh will shorten the flow and add a call button, and we will judge it by the share of bookings completed next month.” That paragraph is the brief, the budget, and the test all at once.

A written decision also protects the business from the most common redesign mistake: paying for a new design when the problem was never the design. If the paragraph cannot name the problem, the problem is not clear enough to spend on yet. Measure again, fix the small things first, and let the decision wait until it can be written down.
