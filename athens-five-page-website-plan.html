A Five-Page Website Plan for Athens

WebCraft’s standard website covers up to five pages for $500, one time. Five pages is enough for most small businesses in Athens — if each page has a job. This plan works through the five pages in order, what belongs on each, and what to leave out so the site stays useful instead of sprawling.

Start with five jobs, not five pages

Before choosing page names, list the questions a customer brings to the website: what do you do, do you serve Athens, what does it cost to start, how do I reach you, and can I trust you with the work? Each question becomes a page’s job. A page without a job is a page to cut, however nice it sounds in a meeting.

Write the five jobs down and keep the list beside you while you plan. If two jobs belong to the same question — say, the services and the prices — they may share a page. If one question needs room to breathe, it earns its own. The list is the test every later decision has to pass.

Home: one offer and one next step

The home page has one job: state what the business does, for whom, in Athens, and point the visitor to the next step. A visitor should understand the offer in the first screen, without scrolling, and the next step should be one clear action: call, or send a short inquiry. Everything else on the home page supports those two things.

Resist listing every service on the home page. A long list makes the visitor do the sorting the site should do for them. Name the main services in a line or two, link to the services page for the detail, and keep the home page short enough to read on a phone in under a minute.

Services: say what you do, in the customer’s words

The services page is where the customer checks whether you do the work they need. Write each service as the customer would describe it, not as the trade describes it: the problem it solves, what is included, and what happens next. A short explanation per service, with a representative photo where you have permission to use it, beats a long list of service names.

If the business has two genuinely different lines of work — say, repairs and installations — they may each earn a page, and that uses two of the five. If the services are variations on one trade, one honest services page does the job, and the spare page goes to something customers ask about more.

About and contact: proof and the way to reach you

Two pages do the quiet work of trust. The about page says who runs the business, how long it has served Athens and the nearby communities, and what it stands for — in plain words, without claims you cannot support. The contact page gives the phone number, email, inquiry form, hours and the area served, all true and all current.

Keep the contact details identical to the ones on your listings. A phone number that differs between the website and a map listing makes a customer guess, and guessing customers leave. Test the form yourself before launch: send an inquiry and confirm it reaches the inbox you actually monitor.

Decide what the fifth page earns — and what to leave out

With home, services, about and contact settled, one page remains. The candidates are the pages customers ask about most: a gallery of past work, a short FAQ, a quote or pricing page, or a service-area page naming the communities served. Pick the one your customers actually ask for, not the one that sounds impressive.

Everything else waits. A blog, a second gallery or a page for every service can come later — extra pages are $50 each, agreed separately — but a five-page site that does five jobs well will earn more inquiries than a twelve-page site that does none of them clearly. Launch with five good pages, and add the sixth only when a customer question proves it is needed.
