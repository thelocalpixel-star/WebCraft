The Ooltewah starting point

An Ooltewah business looking at an aging website faces a smaller question than the redesign industry suggests. The site is usually three to five pages, built years ago by a relative, a friend, or a cheap template, and the owner wonders whether to rebuild it. The business is small, the budget is small, and a wrong rebuild costs more than a bad-looking site — it costs the pages Google already found, and the customers who call the number on them.

Most Ooltewah sites do not need a rebuild. They need repairs. The design is rarely the problem; the problem is that the phone number is hard to find, the hours are out of date, the photos show work from five years ago, or the site names services the business no longer offers. A redesign fixes the look. The repairs fix the customers. Start with the repairs.

Repair first: the four fixes that cost nothing

Four fixes cost nothing but an afternoon. First, the phone number: put it at the top of every page, make it tappable on a phone, and test it yourself. Second, the hours and services: delete anything out of date, and write the services in the words customers use. Third, the photos: replace the old ones with real photos of real work, taken this year, with permission where needed. Fourth, the dead weight: remove pages nobody visits and towns you do not serve.

Test the result on your own phone, as a customer would. Find the phone number, read the service list, check the area wording, and send a test message through the form. If all four work, the site is doing its job, whatever it looks like. If the phone number is still hard to find after the fixes, the problem is the structure — and that is when a rebuild starts to make sense.

When repair is not enough

A rebuild is the right answer in four situations. The site cannot be edited at all — no login, the builder is gone, and nobody knows where the files are. The site does not work on a phone: text runs off the screen, buttons cannot be tapped, or the phone number is an image. The site names services you no longer offer and the pages cannot be changed. Or the structure itself hides the phone number behind clicks, so customers give up before they reach it.

Notice what is not on that list: an old-looking design, a competitor with a prettier site, or a sales call that says the site is out of date. A site that works on a phone, shows the number, and lists the right services does not need a rebuild because it looks old. It needs a rebuild when the structure stops customers reaching you — and not before.

What a rebuild must keep

A rebuild that throws away the old site throws away what the old site earned. Keep the domain — never let a rebuild move you to a new one. Keep the page addresses Google already found, or redirect each old address to the matching new page. Keep the phone number exactly as customers know it. And keep the pages that bring calls: if the services page is the page people find, it stays, with the same address and the same words, even if the design changes around it.

Ask the designer to list, in writing, what will be kept: domain, page addresses, phone number, and the pages that already bring enquiries. Then ask what happens to the old pages that are removed — each one needs a redirect to a real page, not a dead end. A rebuild without that list is a new site that starts from zero, and a small business cannot afford to start from zero.

Price it against doing nothing

A rebuild has a price, and so does doing nothing. A WebCraft website costs $500 once, with $20-a-month care that is optional, and extra pages at $50 each. Doing nothing costs the calls that never arrive — a customer who cannot tap the number, a visitor who leaves after ten seconds, a search result that points to a page with last year's hours. Neither number is visible on a bank statement, which is why the comparison has to be made deliberately.

The right way to decide is the repair list, not the look of the site. Do the four free fixes, test the site on your phone, and count what still fails. If nothing fails, keep the site and spend the money elsewhere. If the structure fails, rebuild — and pay for a rebuild that keeps what the old site earned.
