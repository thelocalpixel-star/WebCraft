A Landing Page for Your Athens Google Ads

An Athens business paying for Google Ads sends every click somewhere — and too often that somewhere is the homepage. The homepage answers everyone and converts no one. This guide covers the landing page: one page, one job, built for the visitor the ad promised something to.

The homepage is the wrong landing page

The homepage has to serve every visitor: the new customer, the returning customer, the supplier, the job seeker. It answers everyone a little, and no one completely. An ad, by contrast, promises one thing — one service, one offer, one town — and the visitor who clicks it expects to land on that thing.

When they land on the homepage instead, they have to hunt for it, and a visitor who has to hunt is a visitor who leaves.

The mismatch costs the click twice: the business paid for it, and the visitor left without acting. The fix is not a better homepage. It is a page built for the ad: the service the ad named, the offer the ad made, and one clear next step.

The homepage stays where it is, for the visitors who arrive without an ad. The ad gets its own page, for the visitors who arrived because of it.

One page, one job

A landing page has one job: the call, the form, or the booking. Everything on the page serves that job, and anything that does not serve it comes off — the company history, the list of every service, the gallery, the newsletter box. A visitor who came from an ad for one service does not need the whole business.

They need the service, the price or the promise, and the way to act.

There should be one clear next step, not five buttons competing for attention: call, or fill in the form. The form asks for three things — the service, the town, and a way to reply — and promises when the answer comes.

A form that asks for everything up front is a form the visitor abandons; a form that asks for the minimum and promises a quick reply is a form the visitor finishes.

What an Athens landing page must show

The service, in the customer’s words — the words the customer typed to find it, not the trade’s words for it. The phone number, tappable, at the top of the page and again beside the form. The hours, and the area: Athens, the county seat of McMinn County, between Knoxville and Chattanooga on Interstate 75, with U.S.

Route 11 and State Route 30 crossing in town. A business that serves the whole county should say so, because a customer in a neighbouring town who thinks the business only serves Athens will not call.

And proof: a real job, a real review in the customer’s own words, a photograph of real work. None of it can be invented, bought, or written by the designer — a review the business wrote itself is not a review, and a photograph from a stock library is not proof of anything.

One real proof page beats ten pages of claims, because the visitor is not deciding whether the business sounds good. They are deciding whether to trust it.

The brief to give whoever builds it

The brief is short, and it should be written down before anyone builds anything: the service, the offer, the one job the page does, the form fields, and what happens after the visitor submits — who replies, and how soon. The brief also names the measure: inquiries per spend, not clicks.

A builder who gets that brief can build the page. A builder who gets “make a landing page” will build a prettier homepage, and the business will pay for the same clicks with the same result.

The brief should also say what the page is not: not the homepage, not the whole business, not a place for every service. One service, one offer, one job.

A business that writes the brief in plain words, before the work starts, gets a page it can judge — and a page it can judge is a page it can fix, when the numbers say it needs fixing.

How to know the page is working

The measure is inquiries per spend: how many calls and form submissions the ad brought, and what each one cost. Not clicks — a click is a visit, and a visit that does nothing is a visit the business paid for and got nothing from.

The form should be tested with a real submission, not assumed: a form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common landing-page failure, and only a real test message finds it.

Small numbers need patience. A week of data is not a verdict — a few clicks either way can look like a trend and mean nothing. Give the page a month, change one thing at a time, and ask whether the change brought more inquiries or fewer.

When the page brings inquiries at a cost the business can afford, it is working. When it brings clicks and no calls, change the page first and the ad second — because the ad did its job, and the page did not.
