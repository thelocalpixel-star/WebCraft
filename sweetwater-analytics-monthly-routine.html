A Sweetwater Analytics Monthly Routine

The point of a monthly analytics routine is not to find something new every month. It is to ask the same questions the same way, month after month, until the answers form a pattern the business can trust. A Sweetwater business — the largest city in Monroe County, serving Madisonville, Tellico Plains, Vonore and Athens as well — does not need a dashboard it watches daily. It needs thirty honest minutes a month, the same three numbers, and one page made better. This guide is that routine.

The routine is a habit, not a project

A routine is something done the same way at the same time, and its value comes from the repetition. The first month produces a baseline. The third month produces a comparison. The sixth month produces a pattern: which pages earn inquiries, which channels pay, which months are slow and why. A business that treats analytics as a project — a burst of attention, then nothing for months — will never see the pattern, because the pattern only appears when the questions are asked the same way every time.

Keep the routine short on purpose. Thirty minutes is enough, because the routine asks only three questions: how many inquiries came in, where did they come from, and which pages earned them. A routine that tries to answer everything will answer nothing, and a routine that takes a whole afternoon will not be done next month. The business's time is better spent serving customers; the analytics exist to make that serving more effective, not to become a second job.

The inquiry count comes first

Line one of the form is the inquiry count, and it comes before anything else. Count the month's inquiries from the business's own records: the calls that asked about a service, the forms that named a job, the bookings with a date. Write the count next to last month's, and next to the work that was done — which pages were added, which details were fixed. The comparison that matters is with the business's own past, not with a competitor's numbers, because no two businesses count inquiries the same way and a competitor's count is not available anyway.

Two ratios sit on the same line, and they turn the count into a decision. The first is inquiries per visit: of the people who found the site, how many asked for something? A rising ratio means the pages are doing their job better. The second is the cost of each inquiry, when money is being spent on ads or on the site itself: the spend divided by the inquiries it brought. A campaign that brings cheap clicks and expensive inquiries is not working, whatever the click report says. Both ratios need the honest count; without it, they are arithmetic on noise.

Which pages did the work

Line two of the form names the pages. The analytics show which pages visitors landed on and which pages they visited before contacting the business, and the two lists are not the same: a visitor may land on the front page and call from a service page, or land on a service page and call from the contact page. The page that earned the inquiry is the one that carried the step the visitor used. Write it down, and study it — it is the model for the pages that have not earned one yet.

The pages to fix are the ones with visits and no inquiries, and the form should name them plainly. Open each one and read it as a stranger would: is the next step visible without scrolling, does the page answer the question its title promises, does it say which towns the business serves? A Sweetwater service page that hides the number, or talks about the business instead of the job the searcher typed, will keep collecting visits and losing callers. One repair per page, one page per month, and the list of pages that earn inquiries grows while the list that only earns visits shrinks.

Comparing months honestly

Line three of the form is the comparison, and it is only honest if the counting stayed the same. If the business changed what counts as an inquiry — started counting misdials, or stopped counting forms — the numbers will move without anything real changing. Write the definition once and keep it fixed: a call counts when the caller asks about a service or a booking; a form counts when it names the work and leaves a way to reply; a booking counts when it carries a date. Everything else goes in a separate column, marked as noise.

Compare the month with the one before, and with the same month last year when the record is long enough. A single quiet week, a holiday, or a storm will move the numbers, so judge the direction over three months, not the size of any single jump. And remember that a small site's numbers are small: a Sweetwater business may see a handful of inquiries a month, and a change of two or three is not a trend. It is a Tuesday. The form's job is to notice the slow drift, not to panic at the wobble.

Write it down, then stop

Lines four and five of the form are the repair and the date: which page was changed, what was changed, and when. Five lines, once a month, and the form becomes the business's own history — what was fixed, when, and whether the inquiries moved afterwards. Without the form, the routine is a feeling; with it, the routine is evidence, and the business can see which months helped and which months were just busy.

Then stop. The routine is thirty minutes because local search rewards consistency, not obsession. Checking the rankings every day will not move them, and changing the website every week will not help the business learn what worked. Fill in the form, file it, and go back to the work the business actually sells. The site will be checked again next month, in the same order, and the small improvements will keep adding up while the business gets on with serving customers.
