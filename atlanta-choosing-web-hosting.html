Choosing Web Hosting in Atlanta

Every hosting company advertises in Atlanta, and every sales page looks alike: fast, unlimited, best. A business choosing hosting in a city this size is not choosing between sales pages — it is choosing what happens after signing. This guide covers the four things that separate a good host from a good sales page: support, renewal price, ownership, and the smallest plan that covers the real jobs.

Every host advertises in Atlanta

Every sales page promises the same words: unlimited visitors, unmetered bandwidth, 99.9% uptime. None of those words means what it sounds like — unlimited visitors usually means the host has not set a number, not that the server can handle any number.

The words on the sales page describe the plan, not the performance — and the performance is what a visitor experiences. A busy neighbour on the same shared server can slow the site down, and the host’s fair-use rules can throttle a site that gets busy.

The choice should therefore be made on what happens after signing: who answers when the site breaks, what the renewal costs, and who owns the domain, the files and the email. A host that answers those three questions clearly, before the business signs, is a host worth paying.

A host that only talks about the sales page is selling a plan for a website the business does not have.

Support that answers when the site breaks

A hosting plan works until the day it does not — and that day is the day the business finds out what support means.

When it breaks, a form stops sending, a page shows an error, or the site will not load at all. The business finds out whether support means a person who answers, a ticket queue that answers on Monday, or a help page that answers nothing.

Before signing, a business should know how support is reached, how fast it answers, and whether the person answering can actually fix the site or only restart the server.

The test is simple: ask the host what happens when the contact form stops working on a Saturday. If the answer is a ticket that will be looked at on Monday, the business has learned something important about its weekends. If the answer is a phone number and a person, the business has learned something else.

An Atlanta business that takes inquiries seven days a week needs hosting that answers seven days a week, and that fact is worth more than a few dollars a month either way.

The renewal price, read before you sign

Almost every hosting plan is sold at an introductory price for the first term, and renews at a higher one. The increase is rarely explained — it is simply the price now.

A business that signed up three years ago and never looked again can be paying two or three times the introductory price, while the same host offers the introductory price to every new customer who signs up today. Loyalty, in hosting, is often rewarded with a higher bill.

The check is to write down three numbers before signing: the first year’s price, the renewal price, and the new-customer price for the same plan today.

If the renewal is much higher than the new-customer price, the business should ask the host to match its own introductory price — and ask with a copy of the site in hand, files and content downloaded and stored somewhere the host does not control, because a customer who can walk away is a customer who can negotiate.

Who owns what: the domain, the files, the email

The domain should be registered in the business’s name, with a business email address, at a registrar the business can reach directly. The files should belong to the business, not the host or the designer.

The email should live where the business says it lives — with the host, or with a provider like Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 — and the business should know which one, because the move that breaks email is the move nobody planned.

The exit test is the question to ask before signing: can the business leave with its domain, its files and its email, or does leaving mean starting over. A host that makes leaving easy is a host that has to earn the renewal every year, and that is a host worth staying with.

A host that makes leaving hard — the domain in the host’s name, the files held back, the email locked in — is a host the business is already stuck with, and the time to find out is before signing, not when it wants to leave.

The smallest plan that covers the real jobs

Five pages, some images, a contact form and a phone number — that is the whole site, and it does not need the most powerful plan on the page. It needs a plan that keeps it online and quick, keeps it updated, backs it up, and answers the phone when something breaks.

The cheapest plan that covers those four things is usually the right one. The most expensive plan is usually a waste — money the business could spend on the site itself.

WebCraft states its own arrangement plainly: $500 once for the website, and $20 a month for hosting and care together, if the business wants them — the hosting, the updates, the backups and someone to call, in one price, with the files belonging to the business.

Ask each plan the same four questions: is it quick on a busy day, is it updated, is it backed up, and is it answered when it breaks? Buy the cheapest plan that answers all four. In a city where every host advertises, the answers are the choice.
