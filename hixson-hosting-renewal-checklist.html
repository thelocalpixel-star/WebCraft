A Hixson Hosting Renewal Checklist

A hosting renewal usually arrives as a letter or an email with a higher price than the first year, and most businesses pay it without reading it. The renewal is not a bill — it is a decision, made once a year, about whether the hosting still earns its price. A Hixson business should spend twenty minutes on the renewal, once a year, checking four things: the price, the coverage, the domain, and the exit terms. This guide is that twenty minutes, written down.

The first-year price was a promotion

Almost every hosting plan is sold at an introductory price for the first term, and renews at a higher one. The renewal letter is where the real price appears, often two or three times the price the business signed up for, and the increase is rarely explained — it is simply the price now. A business that budgeted for the first year’s price and never looked again can spend years paying a renewal it never agreed to, because the plan renewed itself.

The first check is therefore the simplest: what did the first term cost, what does the renewal cost, and when does it renew. Write both numbers down, with the date. If the renewal is a surprise, the business has learned something about its host — and about its own bookkeeping. A hosting plan that renews automatically, at a price that was never stated plainly, is a plan that deserves a second look before the next renewal, not after it.

What the renewal price actually covers

A higher renewal price is not automatically a bad deal — but it must be judged on what it covers, not on what it cost last year. Does the price include the files, the updates, the backups and someone to call, or only a place to store the files? Has the business used the support in the last year, and did it answer? Is the site faster or slower than it was a year ago? A renewal that covers real work the business would otherwise have to do itself is worth its price. A renewal that covers nothing the business uses is not.

The coverage check should also ask what has changed since the business signed up. Hosting plans change: backup policies change, support hours change, the server the site sits on gets busier. A plan that was right for a new site a year ago may be wrong for a site that now takes bookings, or right for a site that still rarely changes. The renewal is the one day a year the business is supposed to ask — and the host is supposed to answer.

The domain: yours, or theirs

The renewal letter is also the moment to check the domain name — the business’s address on the internet. Who owns it: the business, or the host? Who is the registrar, and when does the domain itself renew? A business that registered its domain through its host may find the host listed as the owner, and a host listed as the owner can make moving very difficult. The domain is the most valuable thing the business owns online, and it should be registered in the business’s name, with the business’s email, at a registrar the business can reach directly.

If the domain is not in the business’s name, the renewal is the time to fix it — while the hosting is still paid and the host is still answering. Transferring a domain to the business’s own registrar account takes days when the host cooperates, and weeks when it does not. A business that waits until it wants to leave to discover it does not own its own address has waited too long.

The exit terms, read before you need them

Before paying another year, a business should know how it leaves: who holds the website files, whether the host will hand them over, and whether the domain moves with them. The time to read the exit terms is while the business is still a paying customer, not after a dispute, when the host has no reason to help. A host that answers these questions clearly — in writing, before the renewal — is a host worth renewing with. A host that will not answer them is a host the business is already stuck with, and the renewal is the price of the lesson.

The exit check has one practical part: download a copy of the website files and the content now, while everything works, and store it somewhere the host does not control. A business that holds its own copy can move in a day, whatever the host says. A business that does not hold a copy is asking permission to leave, and permission is not always given. The copy takes half an hour. The renewal is the reminder to take it.

Renew, renegotiate, or move — the decision in order

The decision at renewal has three options, in order. Renew, if the price covers real work and the exit terms are clean. Renegotiate, if the price has risen without the coverage rising with it — hosts will often match their own introductory price for a customer who asks, and a customer who asks with a copy of the site in hand is a customer who can walk away. Move, if the price is high, the coverage is thin, and the exit terms are unclear — and move before the renewal is paid, not after, because a paid year is a year the business cannot get back.

Whichever option the business chooses, write it down with the date, and set the same twenty-minute check for next year. Hosting is a small monthly cost that becomes a large one only when nobody looks at it for years. Twenty minutes, once a year, is the whole job — and a Hixson business that does it will never again pay a renewal it did not choose.
