A Five-Page Website Plan for Atlanta

An Atlanta small business competes with big agencies across the whole metro, and a single broad page cannot win that comparison. Five narrow pages can: each one about a single service, each with one proof, each ending in one next step. A five-page website built for $500 covers exactly that — five pages that do five jobs, instead of one page that tries to do everything and does none of it well.

Five narrow pages beat one broad page

A broad page tries to say everything: every service, every neighbourhood, every reason to choose the business. The result is a page that says nothing clearly, and a visitor who leaves after ten seconds. Five narrow pages do the opposite. Each page says one thing well, and a visitor who lands on the right page finds exactly what they searched for.

Narrow pages also match how people search in a big city. A customer does not search for “Atlanta business services”. They search for one service, in one part of town, with one problem to solve. A page about that service, for that part of town, is a page that can be found. A page about everything is a page that is found for nothing.

Page one: the service, and who it is for

The first page is the main service, named the way a customer would say it, with one line saying who it is for. “Bookkeeping for Atlanta restaurants” is a page. “Business services in Atlanta” is a category, and categories do not bring customers. The page should say what the service includes, how long it usually takes, and what the customer gets for calling — the three things a visitor wants before they read further.

The first page should also say what the service is not. A bookkeeper who does not do tax returns should say so, because the customers who need tax returns will leave anyway — better that they leave in ten seconds than after a phone call that wastes both sides’ time. A narrow page is honest about its edges, and that honesty is what makes it trustworthy.

Page two: the proof a small business can give

A small business cannot outbid an agency on portfolio size, and it should not try. Its proof is different: a named neighbourhood it serves, a problem it solved that an agency would have missed, a customer who came back for a second job. One specific story is worth more than a page of claims, because a story can be checked and a claim cannot.

Proof can also be a number the business actually knows: how many jobs it did last month, how long the average job takes, how quickly it answers the phone. Those numbers are small, and that is the point — a small business that tells the truth about its size is more believable than a small business pretending to be big.

Page three: the neighbourhoods you actually serve

The third page names the neighbourhoods and suburbs the business genuinely serves — Midtown, Decatur, East Cobb, or whichever are true — and stops there. Atlanta is a big metro, and a small business that claims all of it is a business that will disappoint most of it. A page that names three neighbourhoods honestly brings customers from those three neighbourhoods; a page that claims twenty brings enquiries it cannot take.

The neighbourhood page should also say what distance changes. If a job in the suburbs costs more, or takes longer to reach, say so before the customer calls. An honest limit brings the right customers; a claimed-everywhere page brings the wrong ones, and the business spends its time saying no.

Pages four and five: the price question and the call

The fourth page answers the price question, because in Atlanta the price question is the first question. A page that hides its prices behind a contact form sends the customer to a competitor who answers. The answer does not have to be a number — “most jobs like yours cost between this and that, and here is what changes it” is an answer, and it is more honest than a single figure that fits nobody.

The fifth page is the call: one phone number, one form, one next step. A visitor who has read four pages should not have to decide how to get in touch; the site should decide for them. The number should be tappable on a phone, the form should ask for the service, the neighbourhood and a way to reply, and the page should say when the business will answer. Five pages, five jobs, one call at the end.
