Email history is the business’s memory

A website can be rebuilt from a backup. An email history cannot be rebuilt from anything, once it is gone. The old quotes, the customer conversations, the invoices, the thread that proves what was agreed — all of it lives in the mailboxes, and a hosting move that leaves the mailboxes behind loses it all. The business discovers the loss months later, when it needs an old message and finds an empty inbox. By then the old hosting is cancelled, and there is nothing to recover from.

The first step of any move is therefore an inventory of the mailboxes: every address the business uses, who reads it, and where its messages live. The main address, the one on the website, the one customers actually write to, the one the owner uses for invoices — each one is a mailbox, and each one must be accounted for before anything moves. A move planned around the website and not the mailboxes is a move planned around the wrong thing.

Move the mailboxes first, not last

The safe order is mailboxes first, website second. Set up the mailboxes on the new host — or with the email provider the business uses, such as Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 — and move the old messages into them before the domain points anywhere new. Moving the messages takes time, and it should happen while the old hosting is still running and the old mailboxes are still reachable. A mailbox moved in a hurry, after the switch, is a mailbox moved with messages missing.

The move of the messages should be checked the way the move of the website is checked: by opening the new mailbox and looking for the oldest messages, not just the newest. If the messages from last year are there, the move worked. If only this week’s messages are there, the move is incomplete, and the old hosting is still running to finish it. The check takes ten minutes and saves a history that took years to build.

Copy the records exactly — one character matters

Email is controlled by records attached to the domain name — the mail records that say where email goes, and the text records that prove the domain is allowed to send it. Those records must be copied exactly to the new settings, character for character. A single mistyped character sends email nowhere, and the error gives no warning: messages simply stop arriving, and the business hears about it from a customer who was ignored.

The records should be written down before the move, from the current settings, and copied by the person who can check them — not from memory, and not from a screenshot taken months ago. After copying, the test is to send a message to every address the business uses and confirm it arrives, and to reply to it and confirm the reply arrives too. A test message that arrives proves the records are right. A test message that does not arrive is a problem found in minutes, while the old setup is still running.

The forwarding trap: a copy is not a move

A common shortcut is to set up forwarding from the old mailbox to a new address, instead of moving the mailbox itself. Forwarding looks like it works — messages arrive — but it is a copy, not a move. The old messages stay on the old host, the old mailbox still has to be paid for, and the day the old hosting is cancelled, the forwarding stops and the history is gone. Forwarding is a bridge, not a destination.

The honest rule is: forward while moving, then stop forwarding once the move is checked. Forwarding during the move catches the messages that arrive while the mailboxes are being copied. Once the new mailbox holds the history and the records point at it, the forwarding should be switched off and the old hosting cancelled — after a week, not on the day of the switch, because some mail servers will still hold the old address in their memory. A move is finished when the forwarding is off and nothing is lost, not when the forwarding is on and everything looks fine.

Test every address, then switch, then watch

The switch itself comes last, after the mailboxes are moved and the records are copied and tested. Lower the domain’s TTL — the setting that controls how long the internet remembers the old address — a day or two before the switch, so the change spreads in minutes rather than hours. Then change the domain to point at the new host, and test again: every address receives, every address can send, and the website loads.

Keep the old hosting running for at least a week after the switch. Some visitors and some mail servers will still use the old address, and the old server quietly catching them is what keeps the move invisible. In that week, watch for the quiet failures: a form message that never arrives, a customer who says they emailed and heard nothing, an invoice that went unanswered. A move on the same domain with the same addresses is invisible to Google — but only the business can see whether its email survived, and only by checking.
