Choosing a New Host in Rome

Choosing hosting for the first time is one decision. Choosing a new host when a website already exists somewhere else is a different one, and it is the decision most Rome businesses actually face: the site is already built, the email already works, and the question is whether the new host is better than the old one — and whether the move will break anything. This guide covers choosing a new host, in the order that keeps the move quiet.

Choosing a new host is not choosing a first host

A first host is chosen on price and promises, because there is nothing to compare against. A new host is chosen against the old one, and the comparison should be about what the old host does wrong, not about what the new host promises. Is the site slow. Does support answer. Has the price risen every year. Does the business own its files and its domain. A new host that fixes the one thing the old host does wrong is a better host, even if its sales page is less impressive.

The choice should also be made before the renewal date, not after it, because a paid year is a year the business cannot get back. The best time to choose a new host is a month before the old one renews, with the old hosting still paid and still answering, and the new host chosen on the answers to three questions: what does the monthly price cover, who answers when it breaks, and who owns the domain and the files. A host that answers all three clearly is a host worth moving to.

Will they move the site for you — and what that leaves out

Many hosts offer a free move: they will copy the website from the old host to the new one. The offer is worth taking, but it is worth asking what it leaves out. Does the move include the email, or only the website files. Does it include the content — the words, the form entries and the settings — or only the design. Does it include the domain, or does the domain stay where it is. A move that copies the files and leaves the email behind is a move that breaks the business’s inbox on the day it looks finished.

The business should also ask what happens if the move goes wrong. Who fixes it, how fast, and what happens to the old site while it is being fixed. The honest answer is that the old hosting should stay running until the new copy is tested and working, because a move done in a hurry, with the old hosting cancelled first, is a move with no way back. A host that insists on cancelling the old hosting before the new copy is tested is a host to walk away from.

The email question, asked first

Email is the part of a move that breaks most often, and it should be asked about first, not last. Where do the business’s mailboxes live now — with the old host, or with a provider like Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. Where will they live after the move. Who copies the mail records — the entries attached to the domain name that say where email goes — and who checks that they are copied exactly. A single mistyped character sends email nowhere, and the error gives no warning: messages simply stop arriving, and the business hears about it from a customer who was ignored.

The mailboxes should be moved before the website, and the old messages moved with them, while the old hosting is still running. The test is to send a message to every address the business uses and confirm it arrives, and to reply to it and confirm the reply arrives too. A test message that arrives proves the records are right. A test message that does not arrive is a problem found in minutes, while the old setup is still running and the fix is easy.

The parallel test: new host running, old host still live

The move itself has a safe order, and it is worth following exactly. First, copy the files and the content to the new host and build the site there completely. Second, test the copy on a temporary address: every page loads, the form sends, the phone number is right, and the email still arrives. Third, and only then, change the domain to point at the new host. The switch is the last step, not the first, because everything that can be tested should be tested before customers see it.

Two details make the switch smoother. Lower the domain’s TTL — the setting that controls how long the internet remembers the old address — a day or two before the move, so the change spreads in minutes rather than hours. And do not launch a redesign at the same time as the move: if something drops afterwards, the business will not know which change caused it, and will spend weeks guessing. One change at a time, tested before it goes live, is the whole method.

The move-in checklist, in one paragraph

Before moving, write the move down in one paragraph: what the site is, where the email lives, who moves what, and what the test will prove. “Five pages, email with the old host, new host moves the files and the content, mailboxes moved first, tested on a temporary address before the switch, old hosting kept for a week after.” That paragraph is the brief, the test and the fallback all at once — and a month later, at the old host’s renewal date, it is the paragraph that proves the move was the right decision.

WebCraft’s own arrangement is stated plainly: a website costs $500 once, and hosting and care together are $20 a month if the business wants them — the hosting, the updates, the backups and someone to call, in one price, with the files belonging to the business. A business comparing hosts should ask each one the same three questions, and buy the cheapest plan that answers all three. A move to a new host should be a quiet afternoon, not a crisis — and the paragraph is what makes it quiet.
