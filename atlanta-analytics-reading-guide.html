Reading Your First Analytics Report in Atlanta

Atlanta is the biggest market WebCraft serves, and its analytics reports are the noisiest: the city page alone brings more search impressions than any other page on the site, and the visitors come from across the whole metro — Milton, Johns Creek, Decatur, Roswell, Cumming, and a dozen more suburbs. A first report for an Atlanta business is mostly noise until it is filtered down to the visitors who might actually become customers. This guide is about doing that filtering, and reading what is left.

Filter the metro before reading anything

Atlanta is not one market. It is a dozen counties and a hundred suburbs, and a report that treats them as one place will lie to you. The visitors from Milton are not the visitors from Decatur. The visitors from Johns Creek are not the visitors from Cumming. Before reading a single number, narrow the report to the market the business actually serves — the suburbs, the counties, the radius around the work. Everything after that is a reading of the real market. Everything before it is a reading of a map.

The filter has two parts. The first is geography: which suburbs, which counties, which towns. The second is time: the same month, read the same way, so the comparison is honest. A report filtered this way will be smaller than the unfiltered one — and smaller is the point. A thousand visits from across the metro mean nothing to a business that serves five suburbs. A hundred visits from those five suburbs mean everything.

The three numbers, after the filter

Three numbers, in a fixed order. How many people got in touch — calls, forms, bookings, counted separately. Where those people came from — search, direct visits, referrals, paid campaigns, counted by inquiry rather than by visit. And which pages they came through — the pages that carried the step, and the pages that drew visits and nothing else. Three numbers. Nothing else on the first reading.

Why this order? Because each number corrects the one before it. The inquiry count says whether the month was good. The channels say which kind of effort produced it. The pages say what to fix next. Read in any other order, the numbers will mislead: the visit total will look like success, the campaign clicks will look like customers, and the pages that collect visits will look like pages that work. The order is the method.

Suburb visitors are the real market

Look at the geography next, suburb by suburb. Milton, Johns Creek, Decatur, Roswell, Cumming, Alpharetta — each one is a market of its own, with its own searches and its own visitors. A suburb that brings inquiries is a market the business is winning. A suburb that brings visits and no inquiries is a question: wrong audience, or wrong page? The answer matters, because the two have different cures.

The cure for a wrong audience is a page that names the suburb plainly, and says what the business does there. The cure for a wrong page is a next step the visitor can see, and an answer to the question they typed. Either way, the suburb split is what makes the question visible. Without it, the business sees a metro total and guesses. With it, the business sees five or six small markets, and knows which one to work on.

The numbers that flatter in a big city

Four numbers will try to flatter you. Page views: one curious visitor, ten pages, ten views. Real-time visitors: a snapshot, gone in a minute. Bounce rate: it cannot tell the visitor who found the number from the visitor who left in disgust. And the visit total — the number every big-city report leads with — which rises for a local event, a mention somewhere, a slow news week, and none of those are the business doing better.

In Atlanta these numbers are always large enough to feel like success. That is what makes them dangerous. A business that judges itself by them will feel rich in a month when nothing was sold, and poor in a month when the phone rang steadily. So judge the month by the inquiries, and let the visits explain them. A hundred visits that bring ten inquiries are worth more than a thousand visits that bring two — and the report should be read that way, every month, without exception.

A first report, read once a month

Once a month, same day, same order. Filter to the market. Read the three numbers. Split them by suburb. Ignore the flattering ones. Write the three numbers down next to last month's, in one place. The first month is a baseline, not a verdict — and in a market this size, the baseline will jump around. A single event can double a month. That is why the direction is judged over three months, not one.

Then fix one thing. The page with the most visits and no inquiries is usually the right choice: a next step made visible, a question answered, a suburb named. Leave the page alone for a month after the fix. Read the report again. A report read this way teaches the business something every month — which suburbs bring inquiries, which channels pay, which numbers were only ever noise — and after a year, the business knows its own market, because it read the report properly twelve times.
