Asheville Exit Pages: Find Them, Fix Them

Asheville sits in the Blue Ridge Mountains, in Buncombe County, and its business websites are read by two very different groups: visitors planning a trip to the mountains, and locals who live and work in the city year-round. The two groups leave from different pages, for different reasons, and a single exit count that mixes them will mislead. This guide is about splitting the two apart, finding the exits that lose callers in each group, and repairing the step that matters to the people who left.

Visitors decide; locals act

A visitor's exit and a local's exit are different events, and they should never be counted together. The visitor arrived from far away, read one page, and left because the page did not answer the question they came with — or because it did, and they are now on their way. The local arrived knowing the business, checked the hours or the number, and left because they found it. One of those is a loss. The other is a success. A report that adds them together produces a number that cannot be read.

So split the exits before reading them: visitors from outside Buncombe County on one side, locals from inside it on the other. The service pages should be judged on the visitors — did the people who came to decide actually decide? The hours and contact pages should be judged on the locals — did the people who came to act actually act? Two audiences, two judgements, one report read twice. A page that looks bad in the blended number may be fine for one audience and failing for the other, and the repair is different in each case.

The visitor's exit

The visitor's exit is the one that costs money. They searched for one thing, opened one page, and chose from that page alone — call, book, or move on to the next business in the results. Write down the three service pages with the highest exit shares among visitors from outside the county, and open each one as a stranger would: on a phone, in a hurry, with the question the page's title promises to answer.

In Asheville the question is often about place: where is the business, what are the hours, what does a first visit involve? A visitor who cannot find those answers will not call to ask; they will call the next business in the results. A visitor who finds the answers and still leaves has usually been given no reason to act now — no next step, no sense of what happens after the call. The page had one job, and it did not do it. Repair the step, and the visitor's exit share will fall.

The local's exit

The local's exit is usually a success, and it should be read that way. A local who checks the hours and leaves has the hours. A local who copies the number and leaves is about to call. The pages the locals use — hours, contact, directions — should be kept accurate and easy to read, and otherwise left alone. Trying to hold a local on a page that has already answered their question only adds clutter to a page that was doing its job.

The local's exit becomes a failure only when the local cannot find what they came for: the hours have moved, the number is buried, the page they need has changed. If the locals' exit share on a service page is high and the inquiries from locals are falling, something they rely on has shifted — and the repair is to put it back where they expect it, in the same place every visit. Read the locals' exits beside the locals' inquiries, and the difference between a served customer and a lost one will be plain.

Reading the page that lost them

Whether the exit came from a visitor or a local, the page itself explains it. Open the page and read it for the moment the person gave up: was the number visible without scrolling, or buried at the bottom? Did the page answer the question its title promised, or did it talk about the business instead? Was the form short, or did it ask for information the visitor did not have yet? Was the address clear, or did the visitor have to guess where the business is?

Do not change the page before reading it, because a change made without reading is a guess, and the exit share will not say whether the guess was right. The usual cause is the missing next step — a page that explains a service beautifully and never says what to do next. The repair is small: a tap-to-call button at the top, a booking link beside the description, a short form that asks only for what the business will use. Repair one thing per page, and repair the most likely cause first.

The monthly check, twice

Make the exit check part of the monthly routine, and do it twice: once for visitors, once for locals. Sort the service pages by exit share for each audience separately, note the three at the top of each list, and read each one as a stranger would. Repair one thing on one page, write down what changed and why, and move on. The record matters more than the repair: after a few months it shows which pages kept losing which audience, and which repairs actually worked.

Over time the pattern will settle. The pages that earn inquiries will stay steady, the pages that leak will be repaired one at a time, and the exits that remain will mostly be the good kind — visitors who found what they needed and left to act on it, and locals who found the hours and left to come in. That is what a small business website in the mountains is for: not to hold visitors, but to send them out the door towards the phone, the booking, or the drive over. An exit that does that is not a failure. It is the site doing its job.
