Writing Service Pages in a Customer's Words

A Blue Ridge service page should sound like the customer, not the business. Customers search in their own words, describe their problems in their own words, and choose the business that sounds like it understood them. This guide covers the say-it-like-they-say-it test: writing every service page in the words a customer would use.

The customer-language test

Read a service page out loud and ask one question: would a customer say this? “We deliver comprehensive solutions tailored to your needs” is not something any customer has ever said. “Our deck is rotting and we need it fixed before summer” is something a customer says every week. The page that uses the second sentence is the page the customer recognises as theirs.

The test works because search works the same way. A customer types the problem into Google in their own words — “deck repair Blue Ridge”, “leaking roof Blue Ridge GA” — and the page that uses those words is the page that matches. A page written in business words cannot match a search written in customer words, however good the business is.

Start with the search, not the service

Start every service page with the search that would find it. What would a customer type when they need this work? Write that phrase down, then write the page around it. The headline should carry it, the first sentence should answer it, and the rest of the page should prove the business can do the work.

Starting with the search also stops the most common service-page mistake: writing about the business instead of the problem. A page that starts with the company’s history is a page that has forgotten who it is for. A page that starts with the customer’s problem is a page that has the customer’s attention from the first line.

Replace the business words with customer words

Every trade has business words that customers never use. “Solutions”, “comprehensive”, “turnkey”, “state-of-the-art”, “full-service” — these words fill service pages and mean nothing to the person reading them. Replace each one with the plain word a customer would use: repair, replace, build, fix, clean, install. The page gets shorter, clearer, and more honest in the same edit.

The replacement rule is simple: if a neighbour would not understand the word, do not use it. A neighbour understands “we fix leaking roofs”. A neighbour does not understand “we provide comprehensive roofing solutions”. The business that writes like a neighbour talks is the business a neighbour trusts with the work.

One example, rewritten

Here is a sentence from a typical service page, written as an illustrative example rather than a quote from any real business: “We are committed to delivering high-quality, customer-focused exterior solutions to the Blue Ridge area.” It names no service, no customer, no place beyond Blue Ridge, and no next step. A visitor who reads it knows less than they did before.

The rewrite is: “We repair and replace decks in Blue Ridge and Fannin County. Call for a free estimate, and we will tell you the price before we start.” That sentence has the service, the area, the next step, and the pricing. It is shorter than the original and it does five times the work. Every vague sentence on a service page can be rewritten this way — name the work, the place, and the next step.

Keep the area honest

Customer language also applies to the area. A Blue Ridge service page should name Blue Ridge and the towns the business genuinely serves — and stop there. Customers in Blue Ridge know which towns are near Blue Ridge and which are not. A page that lists towns the business cannot reach does not bring customers from those towns; it brings visitors who cannot be served.

The area line should also use the customer’s geography, not the map’s. Customers think in drives and landmarks, not county lines: Blue Ridge, the towns along the highway, the communities the business actually visits. Write the area the way a customer would describe it, and the page will be found by the people who live in it.
