A Dalton Hosting Renewal Checklist

The first year is a promotion; the second year is the real price; by the third year, the business is paying more than a new customer would pay for the same plan, and nobody has mentioned it. A Dalton business that has had the same host for years should spend twenty minutes on the renewal, once a year, asking three questions: what am I paying, what does it cover, and what would a new customer pay.

The loyal-customer penalty

Most hosting plans start at a low introductory price for the first term, then renew at a higher one. The renewal letter is where the real price appears, often double or triple the price the business signed up for, and the increase is rarely explained — it is simply the price now.

A business that signed up three years ago and never looked again can be paying two or three times the introductory price, while the same host offers the introductory price to every new customer who signs up today. Loyalty, in hosting, is often rewarded with a higher bill.

The first check is therefore the simplest: what did the first term cost, what does the renewal cost, and what would a new customer pay for the same plan today. Write all three numbers down, with the dates.

If the renewal is much higher than the new-customer price for the same plan, the business has learned something about its host — and the next section is the phone call that fixes it.

The phone call that saves money

Hosts will often match their own introductory price for a customer who asks, and the ask is a phone call, not a negotiation. The script is short: “I have been a customer for three years, my renewal is this price, and your new-customer price is that price. Can you match it.”

A customer who asks with a copy of the site in hand — files and content downloaded, stored somewhere the host does not control — is a customer who can walk away, and hosts know it.

The call should also ask what the price covers. A renewal that does jobs nobody in the business will do is worth its price; a renewal that does none of them is not.

Does the price cover the files, the backups, the updates and someone to call, or only a place to store the files? Has the business used the support in the last year, and did it answer? A host that answers those questions clearly is a host worth renewing with.

A host that will not answer is a host the business is already stuck with.

The three-quote afternoon

If the phone call does not fix the price, the next step is an afternoon of comparison: three quotes, judged on coverage, not on price alone.

WebCraft’s own arrangement is stated plainly: a website costs $500 once, and hosting and care together are $20 a month if the business wants them — the hosting, the updates, the backups and someone to call, in one price.

A business comparing plans should ask each one the same questions: what does the monthly price cover, who answers when it breaks, and who owns the domain and the files.

The three quotes should be written down side by side, with the coverage next to the price, so the decision is visible. One plan at a few dollars a month covers a place to store the files and nothing else; another at a higher price covers the files, the backups, the updates and someone to call.

The cheapest plan that covers the jobs nobody in the business will do is usually the right one. The afternoon is also the time to check the exit terms of the current host, while the business is still a paying customer and the host is still answering.

The domain’s own renewal date

While arguing about the hosting, do not let the domain lapse. The domain name renews separately, on its own date, with its own price, and losing it is worse than overpaying for hosting — because the name is the address every customer, every listing and every search result points to.

The renewal check should confirm the domain is registered in the business’s name, with a business email address, at a registrar the business can reach directly, and that the renewal date is written down next to the hosting renewal date.

A domain that is not in the business’s name should be fixed at the renewal, while the hosting is still paid and the host is still answering. Moving it to the business’s own registrar account takes days if the host cooperates, and weeks if it does not.

A business that waits until it wants to leave to find it does not own its own address has waited too long.

Stay, renegotiate, or move — with a deadline

The decision at renewal has three options, in order, with a deadline: stay, if the price pays for real work and the exit terms are clean. Renegotiate, if the price has risen without the coverage rising with it — and the phone call in this guide is the renegotiation.

Leave, if the price is high and the coverage is thin and the exit terms are unclear — and leave before paying the renewal, not after, because a paid year is a year the business cannot get back.

Whichever option the business chooses, write it down with the date, and set the same twenty-minute check for next year. The deadline matters: a decision made before the renewal date is a decision; a decision made after it is a complaint.

A Dalton business that does this check once a year will never again pay a renewal it did not choose, and will never again pay more than a new customer for the privilege of staying.
