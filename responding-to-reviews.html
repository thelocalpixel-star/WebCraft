Responding to Reviews: Positive and Negative

A reply to a review is read by the next customer, not just the one who wrote it. The best replies are short, honest and specific — and the worst ones are the ones that were never written, or were copied from a template.

Reply to every review you can

A review with no reply is a conversation the business walked away from. The customer who wrote it notices, and so does the next customer reading the page. A reply says the business is listening, that real people run it, and that what customers say after the job matters as much as what they said before it. That is true for a glowing review and for an angry one.

The reply does not have to be long. Two or three sentences, in the business’s own words, are enough — and they are better than a polished paragraph that could belong to any business in any town. The customer can tell the difference between a reply written by the person who did the work and one pasted from a template, and so can everyone reading it.

The positive review: thank them for the detail

A good review usually names the detail that mattered: the job finished on time, the crew cleaned up, the phone was answered. Thank the customer for that detail, not just for the praise. One sentence of thanks and one sentence about the work is the whole reply — and it tells the next reader exactly what this business does well, in the customer’s own words.

Never offer a reward for a review, and never ask for one in exchange for a discount or a freebie. An incentivised review is not an honest one, and a business caught trading for reviews loses the trust the reviews were meant to build. The honest reviews a business earns are worth more than any number it could buy.

The negative review: slow down before you type

A bad review is the one most likely to be answered in anger, and the one where anger costs the most. Read it twice before you type anything. Check the facts: the date, the job, what actually happened. Then reply once, briefly: acknowledge the experience, explain what happened without blaming the customer, and offer the next step — a phone call, a visit, a fix — away from the public page.

Never argue with a customer in public, and never post a reply that says the customer is lying, even if you believe it. The readers are not judges; they are future customers deciding whether this business handles problems like an adult. A calm reply to a bad review convinces more people than a perfect record with no replies at all.

The unfair review: answer the readers, not the writer

Some reviews are wrong: the wrong business, the wrong town, a job the business never did, or a complaint about something outside its control. Answer the readers, not the writer. State the facts calmly — what the business does, what it does not do, and how to reach it — invite the person to call, and move on. One reply is enough; a public argument with someone who will not be persuaded helps no one.

Never fake a reply, and never ask friends, family or staff to post reviews that are not real. A fake review is easy to spot — the language gives it away, the pattern gives it away, and the day it is found out, every real review on the page becomes suspect. The honest record, including the occasional bad review with a good reply, is the record worth having.

The monthly routine

Check for new reviews once a week and reply within a few days, while the job is still fresh in everyone’s mind. Keep a note of patterns: the same complaint twice is not two unhappy customers, it is one problem the business should fix. The same praise twice is not a coincidence, it is the thing to say first on the website.

Reviews are a report, not a scoreboard. The number matters less than what the words say, and the words are the cheapest market research a small business will ever get. A business that reads its reviews, replies to them, and fixes what they point at is doing local SEO the way it is meant to be done — one real customer at a time.
