Campaign vs Organic Traffic in Maryville

Maryville is the one city in WebCraft's service area where the organic numbers already move: the Maryville pages are the closest to the first page of the results, and the city brings a steady stream of visitors who found the site without any payment. That makes Maryville the right place to learn the discipline that matters once a business starts paying for clicks: keeping campaign traffic and organic traffic separate, because the two kinds of visitor answer different questions, and a number that mixes them answers neither.

Bought visits and earned visits

A campaign visit is bought: the business paid for the click, and the visitor arrived because the ad matched the search. An organic visit is earned: the visitor arrived because the page was found without payment, through the business's own content, details, and reputation. The bought visit proves the ad worked; the earned visit proves the site worked. A business that counts both as visits has a number that proves nothing, because a rise in the total could mean the ads spent more or the site improved, and the business cannot tell which.

The two kinds of visitor also behave differently, and that difference is useful once it is visible. A campaign visitor is often deciding fast, comparing options, and ready to call or leave; an organic visitor is often earlier in the decision, reading more pages and coming back later. Neither behaviour is better. The point is that the site should serve both — a clear next step for the visitor who is ready, and clear answers for the visitor who is still deciding — and that the business should know which group it is serving when it reads the numbers.

Tag every campaign link

Separation starts with the links. Every campaign link should carry a tag that marks it as a campaign visit, so the reports can split it from the organic visits without guessing. The tag costs nothing and takes minutes to add, and without it the business is left sorting visits by memory, which is how the two kinds get mixed in the first place. Check the tags the way a visitor would: click the ad, land on the page, and confirm the visit is marked as a campaign visit in the reports.

Keep the separation in every count that follows. Visits per channel, inquiries per channel, and the cost of each inquiry per channel: each number gets two columns, campaign and organic, and the columns are never added together. A month in which the campaign brought many visits and the organic channel brought few is a different month from one in which the reverse happened, even if the total is the same, and the business should be able to see the difference at a glance. The habit of two columns is the whole discipline.

What the campaign must prove

A campaign must prove itself in inquiries, not in clicks. The report will show the clicks gladly, and the clicks will usually look good — a campaign can always buy more of them. The question is what each click cost, and how many of the clicks became a call, a form, or a booking. Divide the spend by the inquiries the campaign brought, and compare that cost with the cost of an inquiry from organic search, which is the site's own work divided by the inquiries it brought. A campaign that brings cheap clicks and expensive inquiries is not working.

The campaign's landing page matters more than the ad. An ad that sends visitors to the home page wastes the click, because the home page is written for everyone and the visitor arrived with one specific question. The campaign should land on the page that answers that question, with the next step visible at once. If the campaign's inquiries are few, fix the landing page before raising the budget — more clicks to a page that does not convert is more money spent on the same result.

What the organic visits must prove

Organic visits must prove something different: that the site is being found by the right people, in the right towns, for the right work. Maryville is the seat of Blount County, and a Maryville business serves Blount County and the surrounding towns; the organic report should show visitors from those towns, landing on the service pages, and reaching the contact step. Visitors from far away who never call are not a sign of success. They are a sign the pages are being found by the wrong people, or that the service area is not stated plainly enough.

The organic number to watch is not the total, but the inquiries it brought, and the pages that brought them. A Maryville service page that climbs in the results and brings inquiries is doing its job. A page that climbs and brings only visits is being found and not chosen, and the problem is on the page — a missing answer, a buried phone number, a service area that is not stated plainly. Fix the page before spending more on being found, whether the spending is money or time.

Two columns, one decision a month

Once a month, read the two columns side by side: campaign inquiries and their cost, organic inquiries and the pages that brought them. The decision the columns support is a simple one — where should the next effort go? If the campaign brings inquiries cheaply and the organic channel is flat, the site needs work. If the organic channel brings inquiries steadily and the campaign brings clicks that never call, the campaign needs work, or pausing. If both bring inquiries, the business is doing well, and the job is to keep both honest.

Write the two columns down next to the spend and the pages changed, so the record shows what was tried and what it did. Over a few months the pattern will settle: which campaigns earn their keep, which pages earn inquiries without payment, and which channel deserves the next dollar or the next hour. That is the whole point of keeping them separate — not tidier reports, but a decision the business can defend, made from numbers that mean what they say.
