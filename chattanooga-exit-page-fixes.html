Chattanooga Exit Pages: Find Them, Fix Them

A Chattanooga business website gets more traffic than a small-town site — 181,099 people in the city itself, and a whole county around it — and that volume changes the work. On a quiet site, a leaking page is obvious. On a busy one, it hides inside totals that look healthy, because a page can lose nine visitors out of ten and still show a big exit count that nobody questions. This guide is about reading rates instead of totals, opening the three worst pages, and repairing the step the visitor could not find.

Count rates, not exits

The exit total for a page is a function of its traffic, and on a Chattanooga site the traffic is large enough to make every total look large. A service page with a thousand visits and nine hundred exits is leaking badly. A page with a hundred visits and ninety exits is leaking just as badly, and the totals will never say so — the first page looks ten times worse, and it is not. The number that matters is the share: of the people who reached a page, how many left from it without calling, booking, or asking?

Sort the service pages by that share, highest first, and the leaks will stand out from the pages that are merely popular. Then put the share beside the inquiries for the same page, in the same month. A page with a high exit share and steady inquiries is not necessarily broken — it may be answering a quick question well. A page with a high exit share and no inquiries is the one to open first. Rates, not totals, and always read next to the inquiries: those two habits are the whole method at city scale.

The three pages to open

Write down the three service pages with the highest exit shares, and open each one the way a stranger would — on a phone, in a hurry, with the question the page's title promises to answer. The page will usually show its own problem within seconds: the number is at the bottom, the question goes unanswered, the next step is missing, or the service area is never stated. Do not change anything yet. Read first, because a change made before reading is a guess, and the exit share will not say whether the guess was right.

Before deciding what to fix, check where the page's visitors came from. Chattanooga is the seat of Hamilton County, and a Chattanooga business serves the city and the county around it — Hixson, Ooltewah, Soddy-Daisy, Collegedale, and the towns beyond. A page drawing visitors from places the business does not serve has a targeting problem, not a page problem, and no amount of rewriting will fix it. A page drawing the right towns and losing them has a page problem, and that one can be repaired.

What the page was missing

The usual cause is the missing next step. A page can explain a service beautifully and never say what to do about it — call, book, ask — and a visitor who is not told will not invent the step themselves. The repair is small: a tap-to-call button at the top, a booking link beside the description, a short form that asks only for what the business will use. Put the step where the eye lands, and say what happens after it is taken, because a visitor who does not know what happens next will not take it.

The second cause is the question the page never answers. The visitor arrived with one thing in mind — the hours, the towns served, what a first visit involves — and the page talked about the business instead. Add the answer in plain words, at the top, and the exit share will fall. Repair one thing per page, and repair the most likely cause first. A page changed in three places at once teaches nothing, because the business cannot tell which change did the work — and on a busy site, three simultaneous changes will move the numbers in ways no one can untangle.

The good exits at city scale

Not every exit is a failure, and at Chattanooga's volume there will be many good ones. A visitor who leaves from the hours page has the hours. A visitor who leaves from the directions page is on the way. A visitor who leaves from the contact page after copying the number is about to call. Those pages should be kept accurate and easy to read, and otherwise left alone — trying to hold a visitor on a page that has already answered their question only adds clutter to a page that was doing its job.

The test is what the visitor did after leaving, and in a city that step is often invisible to the reports: the call, the booking, the drive over. So read the exit share beside the inquiry count for the same month. Inquiries steady and exits high on the informational pages means the site is working. Inquiries falling and exits high on the service pages means the site is leaking. The exit share is a clue, not a verdict, and the inquiry count is what turns the clue into a decision.

The monthly habit at volume

Once a month, on the same day, sort the service pages by exit share, note the three at the top, and read each one as a stranger would. Repair one thing on one page, write down what changed and why, and leave the page alone for a month. The record matters more than the repair: after a few months it shows which pages kept losing visitors and which repairs actually worked, and that record is worth more than any single month's numbers.

Over time the pattern will settle. The pages that earn inquiries will stay steady, the pages that leak will be repaired one at a time, and the exits that remain will mostly be the good kind — visitors who found what they needed and left to act on it. That is what a business website in a city of 181,099 people is for: not to hold visitors, but to send them towards the phone, the booking, or the drive over. An exit that does that is not a failure. It is the site doing its job.
