Measuring Local SEO by Inquiries, Not Rankings

A local SEO report usually opens with rankings: where the business appears for a handful of searches. Rankings are worth knowing, and they are not the result. The result is the phone call, the form, or the booking request from a person who found the business and decided to act. This guide is about measuring that — what counts as an inquiry, how to count it honestly, and what the count says about the work.

Rankings are context, not the result

A ranking says where a business appears when someone searches. It does not say whether anyone called, whether the caller needed the service, or whether the visit became a job.

A business can climb several places for a search term and gain nothing, because the page it climbed with does not answer the searcher, or because the searchers for that term were never buyers. The ranking is still useful: it shows whether the pages are being found at all, and whether the work is moving in the right direction.

But it is context around the result, not the result itself, and a report that leads with rankings will eventually be read as if the rankings were the point.

The habit to build is reading the report backwards: start with the inquiries, then look at the rankings only to explain them. If inquiries rose, the rankings may show which pages earned them. If inquiries fell, the rankings may show whether the business stopped being found, or whether it is still being found and the page stopped converting.

Either way the inquiry count decides whether the month was good. A business that measures this way stops paying for movement that brings nothing, and starts paying attention to the pages that bring people.

What counts as an inquiry

An inquiry is a person asking the business for something: a phone call that asks about a service, a form that describes a job, a booking request with a date in it. The test is whether a person on the other end wanted a response. A misdial is not an inquiry.

A form filled in by a spammer is not an inquiry. A click is not an inquiry, and a visit that ends without contact is not an inquiry either, however long the visitor stayed. Counting loosely feels good for a month and teaches nothing; the count has to mean the same thing every month or it cannot be compared.

The business should write down its own definition and keep it in one place: a call counts when the caller asks about a service or a booking; a form counts when it names the work and leaves a way to reply; a booking request counts when it carries a date.

Everything else goes in a separate column, marked as noise, so it can be checked without polluting the count. The definition will differ from one business to the next — a plumber's inquiry is a call, a consultant's is a form — and that is fine.

What matters is that the definition stays fixed, so a rise in the count means more people asked, not that the counting changed.

Three kinds of inquiries, counted separately

Calls, forms, and bookings are three different things, and they should be counted in three columns, not one. A call is immediate and often urgent: the caller wants an answer now. A form is considered: the writer has read the page and has a question the page did not answer.

A booking request is the closest to a sale, because it carries a date and a commitment. A business that adds the three together loses the information in the split — whether the phone is doing the work or the forms are, and which one needs fixing.

The split also says something about the website. A site with many visits and few calls may have a phone number that is hard to find on a mobile screen. A site with many calls and few forms may have a form that asks for too much.

A site with forms that never become bookings may be promising something the business cannot schedule. Each pattern points at a different fix, and the fix is cheap when the pattern is visible. Count the three kinds separately for a month, and the website's weak spot usually names itself.

The monthly count, and what to compare it to

Count once a month, on the same day, from the same sources: the phone log, the form inbox, and the booking system. Write the three numbers down next to the previous month's, and next to the work that was done — which pages were added, which details were fixed, which reviews were earned.

The comparison that matters is with the business's own past, not with a competitor's numbers, because no two businesses count inquiries the same way and a competitor's count is not available anyway.

Two ratios turn the count into a decision. The first is inquiries per visit: of the people who found the site, how many asked for something? A rising ratio means the pages are doing their job better.

The second is the cost of each inquiry, when money is being spent on ads or on the site itself: the spend divided by the inquiries it brought. A campaign that brings cheap clicks and expensive inquiries is not working, whatever the click report says.

Both ratios need the honest count from the earlier sections; without it, they are arithmetic on noise.

When the numbers say the work is working

Local SEO works slowly, and the inquiry count is how a business knows it is working before the rankings make it obvious. Inquiries that rise a little each month, from searches the business serves, are the sign: the pages are being found by the right people, and the pages are turning those people into callers.

The rise will not be smooth — a quiet week, a holiday, a storm will move the numbers — so judge it over three months, not one, and look for the direction rather than the size of any single jump.

There is one pattern that should prompt a change rather than patience: rankings climbing while inquiries stay flat. That means the business is being found and not chosen, and the problem is on the page, not in the search results — a missing answer, a buried phone number, a service area that is not stated plainly.

Fix the page before spending more on being found. And the reverse is just as telling: inquiries rising while rankings barely move means the work is already paying, and the rankings are simply late to the news. In both cases the inquiries, not the rankings, are the measure that decides.
