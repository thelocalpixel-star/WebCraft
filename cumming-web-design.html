A growing county, a remote designer

Forsyth County has grown fast, and Cumming with it: new subdivisions, new shops along the GA-400 corridor, new businesses serving a county that keeps adding people. A new business in Cumming needs a website the way it needs a sign — and the designers serving it work from Atlanta and beyond, by phone and online, without an office in Cumming. The pages that answer Cumming searches should say that plainly, instead of implying a storefront that does not exist.

Growth changes what the website has to do. A business opening in a growing county is found by people who just moved here — people who search, compare and decide on their phones, without asking a neighbor first. The website is the first impression for a customer who has no local history to lean on, and it has to answer their questions before they drive anywhere.

How a remote project works

The project starts with a brief by phone: the business, the services, the customers, and what the website has to do. The designer builds a first draft — real pages, real words — and the business reviews it online. Changes are made in rounds, each agreed before the next begins, and the site launches only when the business approves it. The whole project can be done without anyone leaving their office.

The business’s part of the work is the review. A prompt, specific review — this photo is wrong, this sentence says the wrong thing, this page is missing — is what turns a good draft into a site that sounds like the business. A remote project does not fail because the designer is far away. It fails because the review never happened, and the site launched with words nobody in the business had read.

The five pages that answer the first five questions

A Cumming business’s first five pages should answer the five questions a new customer asks: what do you do, where do you do it, what does it cost to start, why should I choose you, and how do I reach you. One page per question, each ending with the same next step — a phone call or a short form. A page that tries to answer all five at once answers none of them well.

The order matters less than the honesty. A services page that lists services the business does not really sell is worse than a short one that lists only the real ones. An about page with a real photo and a real story beats a long paragraph of claims. New customers in a growing county are comparing several businesses at once; the site that answers plainly wins the call.

What a new business should buy first

A new business should buy the website before the extras. The five pages, the phone number, the contact form — those come first. The blog, the booking system, the online store and the extras wait until the five pages work, because an extra that nobody finds is money spent on a room nobody enters. A business that is still explaining what it does does not need a blog; it needs a home page that explains it in one screen.

The same rule applies to the budget. Five hundred dollars once, for a site the business owns, is a different decision from a monthly plan that charges every month whether the site works or not. Buy the site first, measure what it brings, and add the extras when the inquiries say they are needed — not when a sales call says they are.

There is no monthly requirement and no setup fee beyond the $500. A Cumming business comparing quotes should ask for the same three numbers from every designer: the build price, the monthly price, and the three-year total, all in writing, with the ownership and the exit terms beside them. The cheapest first month is rarely the cheapest three years, and the provider that will not write all three down is selling a monthly number, not a website.
