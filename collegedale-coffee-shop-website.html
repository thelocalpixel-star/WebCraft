A Website for a Collegedale Coffee Shop

A coffee shop in Collegedale is three businesses in one: a study room for the university, a meeting place for the town, and a morning stop for the commuters on the way to Chattanooga. The website has to serve all three, and it has to do it in the order the customer needs: hours and address first, then the menu, then the room. This guide is about what a Collegedale coffee shop website needs — for a university town in Hamilton County where the term calendar moves the mornings and the regulars are students, staff, and neighbours.

A study room that sells coffee

Between the dorm and the library, a Collegedale coffee shop is a third place. Students come to study, not to browse. They bring laptops, textbooks, and a deadline. The website has one job for them: tell them, in seconds, whether this is the place to work for the next three hours. Hours, seats, power, wifi, quiet. Everything else is secondary.

So the front page should read like a sign on the door, not a brochure. Open now or closed. How many seats, and whether there is somewhere to plug in. Whether the room is quiet in the morning and busy after lunch. A student deciding between two shops will pick the one that answers those questions first. The coffee matters — but the coffee is the reason they stay. The information is the reason they come.

Hours that follow the term

Write the hours as a calendar, not a single line. Term time has one set of hours. Exam weeks stretch them. Breaks shrink them. Holidays close the shop. Each change should appear on the site before it happens, with the date it starts. A student planning a study session on Sunday night needs to know Monday's hours on Sunday night — not on Monday morning, at the door.

Keep one more thing in view: the last hour. When do the orders stop? When do the chairs go up? Students who arrive at eight for a nine o'clock close need to know whether eight is a real hour or a polite fiction. And when the term ends, say so in advance — a week of reduced hours, announced on the site, beats a locked door and a confused regular. The hours are a promise. Keep them, and keep them current, everywhere the shop is listed.

The menu as a real page

Build the menu as a page a phone can read. Real text, real prices, every drink listed with its sizes. A student searching for a specific drink should land on the page, not on a photograph of a menu board. Photographs cannot be searched. They cannot be read aloud. They go out of date the moment a price changes. A text page can be updated in a minute, and it can be found by anyone looking for what the shop sells.

Add the details a student actually asks about. Which drinks come dairy-free, and what the alternative costs. Which pastries are gone by ten. What the seasonal drink is, and when it ends. And the prices — all of them, on the page. A shop that makes a student guess at the price has already lost the student who is counting their money. The menu page is not decoration. It is the page that turns a search into a visit.

Events, groups and the room after hours

After hours, the room is a different asset. A study group needs a table for six. A club needs a corner for twenty. A tutor needs a quiet table on a Tuesday. The website should say whether the room can be booked, how many it holds, and how to ask — a short form, a phone number, a named person. A group that cannot find the answer will book somewhere that gives it.

Events deserve their own page, kept current. A tasting, a live set, a study night during exams — each one with a date, a time, and what to expect. Old events should come down the day after they happen. A page full of last month's dates tells a visitor the shop has stopped paying attention. And when the room is closed for a private event, say so on the site that morning, so nobody walks across campus for a closed door.

Regulars, subscriptions and the morning rush

Mornings are the test. Between eight and nine, the line is the shop. A student who orders ahead and collects in a minute will be back tomorrow. A student who waits ten minutes for a drink they could have ordered from their dorm will not. The website should make ordering ahead the easiest thing on the page — one tap, the usual drink, a pickup time. Everything else can wait until after the rush.

For the regulars, offer a standing order: the same beans, the same schedule, the same price, with the terms in plain words. How often it comes. What it costs. How to pause it for a break, and how to stop it. No hidden steps, no email required to cancel. A subscription that is easy to leave is a subscription people trust enough to join — and in a university town, trust is the only loyalty programme that works.
