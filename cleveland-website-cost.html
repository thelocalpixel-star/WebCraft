How Much Does a Website Cost in Cleveland?

A Cleveland business asking what a website costs gets a different answer from every provider — because the price depends on whether you pay once or every month. Here are the published Cleveland prices, side by side, with three-year totals.

By WebCraft · Published November 17, 2026 · Web design guide

In this guideThe one-time price: $500, and what it includesThe monthly price: from $100 a month in ClevelandThe agency range: $750 to $2,500, plus monthly careThree-year totals, side by sideWhat to check before you sign

The one-time price: $500, and what it includes

WebCraft builds a website for $500, one time. The site is yours — the pages, the words, the files — and there is no monthly fee unless you choose the optional hosting and care at $20 a month. A one-time build covers the pages a small business needs, a mobile layout, and a contact path that works on a phone. It does not cover ongoing changes, unless the care plan is added.

The one-time model suits a business whose website rarely changes: the services are stable, the hours are stable, and the site needs to exist and work, not to be rebuilt every quarter. The risk is the opposite of the monthly model — the business pays once and owns the result, so a bad build is a bad build it already owns. That is why the questions before hiring matter as much as the price.

The monthly price: from $100 a month in Cleveland

IronOrbitMedia, a web studio based in Cleveland, builds the website free with any monthly plan: Hosting at $100 a month, Get Found at $250 a month, and Growth at $500 a month, with no long contract and cancellation any time. The website becomes yours after 12 months on Hosting, 6 months on Get Found, or 3 months on Growth, counted from the day the site goes live — as published on the studio’s site in November 2026.

The monthly model suits a business that would rather pay as it goes and have the hosting, upkeep and search work bundled into one bill. The trade is the total: a plan that looks small each month adds up over years, and the business should know the three-year number before it signs, not after. Ownership that arrives after months on a plan is still ownership — but only if the business stays on the plan long enough to earn it.

The agency range: $750 to $2,500, plus monthly care

Trueline, also based in Cleveland, publishes builds from $750 to $2,500, with monthly care plans from $49 to $179 a month — as published on its site in November 2026. Hoagland, in nearby Chattanooga, publishes custom websites from $300 one-time, with hosting at $50 a month flat. These are the published ranges, not quotes: the final number depends on the pages, the features and the scope the business agrees.

The range exists because websites are not one thing. A five-page site for a service business and a store with online ordering are different jobs, and a provider that quotes one price for both is guessing. The honest question is not which price is lowest, but which price buys the site this business actually needs — and what the monthly care covers once the site is live.

Three-year totals, side by side

The fair comparison is three years, because that is how long a small business should expect a website to last. WebCraft: $500 once, or $1,220 with the $20-a-month care plan for three years. IronOrbitMedia: $3,600 on Hosting, $9,000 on Get Found, $18,000 on Growth over three years. Trueline: $2,514 to $8,944, depending on the build and the care plan. Hoagland: $300 once, plus $1,800 of hosting over three years, for $2,100 in total.

The cheapest first year is rarely the cheapest third year. A $500 one-time site costs $500 in year one and nothing in year two unless care is added; a $100-a-month plan costs $1,200 in year one and the same again in year two. Neither is wrong. The business that plans to keep the same site for years should compare the three-year numbers. The business that wants everything handled monthly should compare what the monthly fee actually covers.

What to check before you sign

Price is the last question, not the first. Ask who owns the domain name, and whether it is registered in the business’s name. Ask who owns the files and the words, and what happens to them if the business cancels. Ask what the monthly fee covers — hosting, updates, changes, support — and what costs extra. Ask how changes are requested, and how long they take.

Then ask for the number in writing: the build price, the monthly price, and the three-year total, all on one page. A provider that will not put all three in writing is a provider selling a monthly number, not a website. The Cleveland prices above are published and checkable; any quote a business receives should be held to the same standard — written down, comparable, and honest about what happens when the business leaves.
