A Website for a Cleveland Coffee Shop

A coffee shop in Cleveland is decided on in the morning, in seconds, usually on a phone: is it open, where is it, and what does it serve? The website that answers those three questions first wins the customer; the website that opens with its story loses them to the next shop in the results. This guide is about what a Cleveland coffee shop website needs — hours and address first, a menu as a real page, order ahead for pickup, and the room itself — for a city of 47,356 people where the morning rush runs on Lee University's calendar and the downtown streets.

Hours and address first, always

A coffee shop customer asks two questions before anything else: are you open, and where are you? The website must answer both before the visitor scrolls — the hours for today, in plain words, and the address with a link to directions, at the top of every page. A shop that makes a customer hunt for its hours has already lost the customer who is standing on the street deciding where to go. Hours that are wrong are worse than hours that are hidden: a customer who arrives to a closed door because the website said open will not check the website again.

Keep the hours identical everywhere the shop appears: the website, the search listing, the social profiles, the door. Hours change more often than any other detail — a seasonal change, a Saturday added, a holiday closure — and they are the detail a customer checks right before driving over. If the hours are about to change, put the change on the website before it takes effect, not after. And say plainly what happens at closing: last orders, last seating, the time the doors actually lock.

The menu as a real page, not a photo

The menu should be a real page on the website, written as text, with prices and options — not a photograph of a printed menu, and never a PDF. A photograph cannot be searched, cannot be read by a phone's screen reader, and cannot be found by a search engine; a customer searching for a specific drink will never find a photo of a menu. A real page can be found, read, and linked to, and it can say what the photo cannot: what is in each drink, which sizes exist, what costs extra, and what is available today.

The menu page should also say what the shop does not do, plainly. If there is no decaf after a certain hour, say so. If the oat milk costs extra, say so. If the seasonal drink ends on a date, say so. Customers forgive a short menu; they do not forgive a menu that promised something the shop cannot serve. Keep the page current — a menu that lists a drink the shop stopped serving is a promise broken before the customer arrives — and put the prices on it. A coffee shop that hides its prices makes the customer guess, and the customer will guess at the shop down the street instead.

Order ahead for pickup

The morning customer is in a hurry, and the website should serve that hurry. An order-ahead button — visible on the home page, one tap from the menu — lets the customer order before leaving the house and pick up without waiting. The button should say what happens next: how long the order takes, where to collect it, and what to do if something is wrong. A customer who orders ahead and then waits ten minutes at the counter will not order ahead again.

Order ahead also changes what the shop needs from its website. The site must say, plainly, whether ordering is available right now: a shop that takes orders only during opening hours should say so, and a shop that stops taking orders before closing should say when. The pickup point should be named — the counter, the side door, the shelf — because a customer who does not know where to stand will stand in the wrong place and slow everyone down. The details are small, and they are the whole difference between a feature that works and a button that frustrates.

Beans, subscriptions and the room itself

A coffee shop sells more than drinks, and the website should sell the rest: the beans, the subscriptions, the room itself. Beans need a page that says what they are, where they come from, how they are roasted, and what they cost — with a way to buy them or subscribe, and the terms of the subscription in plain words: how often, how much, how to pause, how to cancel. A subscription whose cancellation is hidden is a subscription that will be cancelled once, angrily, in public.

The room is the other product. A Cleveland shop near Lee University, where about 3,714 students shape the town's calendar, will have regulars who work from its tables — and the website should say what the room offers them: the wifi, the outlets, the seating, the hours the room is quiet and the hours it is busy. Real photos of the space matter here more than anywhere, because the customer is choosing a place to sit as much as a drink to order. Photograph the room as it is, not as it was on opening day.

Events, regulars and the morning rush

A coffee shop is a neighbourhood room, and the website should act like one: the events, the groups that meet there, the mornings that matter. A tasting, a local musician, a study group, a community meeting — each one deserves a line on the site, with a date, a time, and what to expect. Events give a regular a reason to come back this week rather than next, and they give a newcomer a reason to choose this shop over the one with the shorter queue.

The morning rush is the test of the whole website. A customer who finds the hours, reads the menu, orders ahead and collects without waiting will come back tomorrow — and will tell someone else. A customer who cannot find the hours, cannot read the menu, and cannot order ahead will go to the shop that lets them. The website's job is not to be beautiful. It is to be fast, accurate, and honest, on the mornings when it matters most. In Cleveland, that is every morning.
