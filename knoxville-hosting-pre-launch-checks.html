Pre-Launch Checks for a Knoxville Relaunch

Launching a first website is a debut: nothing exists yet, and everything is new. Launching a replacement website is a move: the old site already has addresses people visit, a map listing that points at it, and links from other sites that lead to it. A Knoxville business relaunching its website should check the move, not just the new site — because a beautiful new site that breaks every old address loses the customers the old site was already bringing.

A relaunch is a move, not a debut

A first launch has no history to protect. A relaunch has years of it: pages that show up in search, a phone number customers have saved, a map listing that has collected reviews, links from directories, suppliers and customers that all point at the old pages.

The new site does not start from zero — it starts from the old site’s reputation, and a launch that throws that away has to earn it all back from scratch, one search at a time.

The launch checklist for a relaunch is therefore different from the checklist for a first site. The new site still needs its number tapped, its form tested, its hours checked and its links clicked. But it also needs the old site’s addresses carried over, the map listing pointed at the new pages, and the incoming links updated.

Skip those three, and the launch looks finished on the day it goes live and starts losing visitors the week after.

The old addresses: redirect every page that already ranks

Every page on the old site has an address, and some of those addresses appear in search results. When the new site replaces them, each old address should send visitors to the matching new page — a redirect, set up before launch, one for every page that mattered.

A visitor who clicks an old search result and lands on a dead page does not search again for the business; they click the next result, which is a competitor.

The list of addresses to redirect comes from the old site itself and from the search results that already show it. The homepage, the service pages, the contact page, and any page that brings visitors — each one gets a redirect to its new equivalent.

Pages that no longer exist should redirect to the closest new page, not to the homepage, because a visitor looking for a service does not want to be sent back to the start. The redirects are set up before launch, tested on a copy of the new site, and checked again on launch day.

The map listing points at the new site

The Business Profile — the listing that shows on the map and in search — points at the website, and a relaunch should update that link on the day the new site goes live.

The listing should also be checked for the details that changed with the relaunch: the phone number, the hours, and the description of what the business does. A listing that still points at the old site, or shows the old hours, sends customers to the wrong place with the business’s own blessing.

The same check covers the business’s Facebook page and any directory where the business listed its website address. Each one should point at the new site, and each one should be checked by clicking the link, not by assuming it was updated.

A relaunch that updates the website and forgets the listings has rebuilt the front door and left the signposts pointing at the old one.

The links pointing in: update them or lose them

Other sites link to the business: directories, suppliers, the chamber, a customer’s blog, a local news article. Those links point at the old addresses, and after a relaunch they should be updated where the business can update them — the chamber listing, the supplier page, the directory the business controls.

Where the business cannot update them, the redirects from the old addresses catch the visitors instead. Either way, the links are checked, not assumed.

The check is a search for the business name and a look at the first page of results. Every result that links to the old site is a link to check. The ones the business controls get updated. The ones it does not control get caught by the redirects.

A link that leads to a dead page is a customer sent to a locked door by the business’s own history, and a relaunch is the one moment when all of those doors can be fixed at once.

The launch-week watch

Keep the old hosting running for at least a week after the switch, and watch four things in that week: the website loads from a phone on mobile data, not just office wifi; the contact form sends and the reply arrives; email arrives at every address; and the search results show no sudden errors.

A move on the same domain with the same addresses and the same content is invisible to Google — rankings drop when addresses change without redirects, or when a staging copy left behind a setting that blocks search engines.

The launch-week watch also includes the phone. Call the number on the new site, from a phone, and see who answers and how fast. A relaunch that brings more calls the business does not answer is a website working for the competitor.

Launch day is the cheapest day to find out; every day after it, the callers are real, and they are calling because the new site worked.
