Writing Service Pages for Cartersville

The corridor test: a customer compares you with Atlanta

Cartersville sits on I-75 between Atlanta and Chattanooga, which means a customer searching for a service sees Cartersville businesses and Atlanta businesses on the same results page. The Atlanta business has a bigger website and more reviews. The Cartersville business has to answer one question the Atlanta business cannot: why choose the local one. A service page that does not answer that question is a service page that loses to Atlanta.

The answer is not price, and it is not a claim. It is the practical difference: the local business can be there today, knows the area, and answers its own phone. Those three things are worth more to a customer with a leaking pipe or a broken air conditioner than a bigger portfolio. The service page should say them plainly, in the first paragraph, before the customer scrolls back to the Atlanta result.

Say why local wins, in one paragraph

One paragraph is enough. “We serve Cartersville and Bartow County. We answer our own phone, we can usually be there the same day, and we know the houses in this area — the older ones near downtown and the newer ones toward Emerson and Acworth.” That paragraph does more work than a page of claims, because every sentence in it is a fact a customer can check and a reason to choose local.

The paragraph should not mention Atlanta by name. A service page that talks about its competitors is a service page that sends the customer to look at them. The local argument stands on its own: faster, nearer, and answered by a person. Say that, and let the Atlanta pages make their own case.

Write the service the way a customer asks for it

A customer in Cartersville does not search for “comprehensive HVAC solutions”. They search for “AC repair Cartersville” or “furnace not working Bartow County”. The service page should use those words — the customer’s words — in its headline and its first sentence. A page written in industry language is a page written for other businesses, not for customers.

The customer’s words also decide how many pages the site needs. Each service a customer would search for on its own gets its own page: AC repair, furnace replacement, duct cleaning. A business with three services needs three pages, each written the way a customer would ask for that service. One page with three paragraphs is a menu, not a service page.

The proof only a Cartersville business can give

Proof is a detail only a business that works in Cartersville would know. A roofer can say how a roof handles a Georgia summer and what the spring storms do to gutters in Bartow County. An electrician can say what the older houses near downtown usually need, and what the newer builds do not. Those details are proof, and they are also the words a Cartersville customer uses when they search.

Proof can also be a simple fact: how long the work usually takes, what it includes, what the guarantee covers, and what happens if something goes wrong. A service page that answers those four questions is a service page a customer can act on. A page that only says the business is experienced and reliable is a page a customer skips.

One next step, and what happens after it

A service page should end with one next step, and it should say what happens after it. “Call and we will give you a free estimate, usually the same day” is a next step. “Contact us for more information” is not one. The visitor should know what they get by acting, and when they will get it.

The next step should be the one the business answers fastest. If the phone is answered in person, the number is the next step. If the form is checked first, the form is the next step. Offering four ways to get in touch is offering none of them — a visitor who is offered four choices will pick none and leave. One step, clearly stated, is a service page that works.
