A Cartersville Analytics Monthly Routine

A Cartersville business serves a spread-out county — Adairsville, Euharlee, Emerson, Kingston and the city itself — and its website is the one place all of those towns meet. A monthly routine keeps that meeting honest: the same day, the same order, the same three numbers, and one page fixed before the next month. This guide is that routine, written for a business in the seat of Bartow County that would rather spend its time serving customers than watching charts.

Put the routine on the calendar

A routine that lives in someone's head will be done twice and then forgotten. Put it on the calendar: one day a month, the same day, less than an hour, in the same order every time. The day matters less than the sameness, because a routine done on the same day each month produces numbers that can be compared, and a routine done whenever someone remembers produces numbers that cannot. Treat the day like any other recurring job, and do it even in a busy month — especially in a busy month, because that is when the numbers move most.

Do the routine in the same order every time: inquiries first, then channels, then pages, then the fix. The order is the discipline. A routine that starts with the visit total will end up judging the month by visits, however good the intentions were. A routine that starts with the inquiries will judge the month by what the business actually sold, and the rest of the numbers will fall into their proper place — as explanations, not verdicts.

Count what the business sold, first

The logbook opens with the month's sales of attention: the calls logged, the forms received, the bookings taken. Enter them in three columns — calls, forms, bookings — and write the definition of each at the top of the page, so the counting never drifts. A call counts when the caller asks about a service or a booking. A form counts when it names the work and leaves a way to reply. A booking counts when it carries a date. Everything else goes in a separate column, marked as noise, so it can be checked without polluting the count.

The three columns tell different stories, and the logbook keeps them apart for that reason. A month heavy on calls and light on forms may mean the form asks for too much. A month heavy on forms and light on bookings may mean the site promises something the business cannot schedule. A month where all three rise together is a good month, and the logbook should say what changed that month — which page was fixed, which detail was corrected — so the business knows what to repeat.

Channels, and the towns behind them

The logbook's second entry is the channel for each inquiry: search, a direct visit, a referral, or a paid campaign. Enter the inquiries per channel, not the visits per channel, because the channels behave differently — search may deliver the most visits while direct visits deliver the most bookings, and a campaign may deliver clicks that never call. A channel that delivers visits and no inquiries for two months running gets a note in the margin, and the remedy depends on the cause: the wrong people, or the wrong page.

In Bartow County the geography belongs in the entry. A Cartersville business serves the whole county, and the logbook should record which towns the inquiries came from — Adairsville, Euharlee, Emerson, Kingston, and the city itself. If the visitors come from far outside the county and never call, the pages are found by the wrong people, or the service area is not stated plainly enough. Check where the visitors came from before deciding what to fix — a targeting problem and a page problem have different cures, and the logbook should say which one it was.

Pages that earned, pages that collected

The logbook's third entry is the page each inquiry travelled through. The reports show where visitors landed and which pages they passed before getting in touch, and the two lists differ — a visitor may land on the front page and call from a service page, or land on a service page and call from the contact page. The page that carried the step the visitor used is the page that earned the inquiry. Write it down, and study it: it is the model for the pages that have not earned one yet.

The pages to repair are the ones with visits and no inquiries, and the logbook should name them plainly. Open each as a stranger would: is the next step visible without scrolling, does the page answer the question its title promises, does it say which towns the business serves? A Cartersville service page that hides the number, or talks about the business instead of the job the searcher typed, will keep collecting visits and losing callers. One repair per page, one page per month, and the logbook will show the list of earning pages growing while the list of collecting pages shrinks.

The record is the routine's memory

The logbook closes with the repair: which page was changed, what was changed, and why. Five lines a month — the date, the tally by kind, the channels, the pages that earned, the page repaired — and the record becomes the business's own history: what was fixed, when, and whether the inquiries moved afterwards. Without the record the routine is a feeling; with it the routine is evidence, and the business can see which months helped and which months were just busy.

Then stop. The routine takes less than an hour because local search rewards consistency, not obsession. Checking the numbers every day will not move them, and changing the website every week will not help the business learn what worked. Do the routine, write it in the logbook, and go back to the work the business actually sells. The site will be checked again next month, in the same order, and the small improvements will keep adding up while the business gets on with serving Bartow County.
