Crossville Hosting Access After a Handover

Every website has a person who set it up, and every business eventually faces the day that person moves on — an employee leaves, a designer retires, a volunteer stops volunteering. When the logins leave with them, the business is locked out of its own website, and getting back in is a slow exchange of emails with a host that wants proof of ownership from someone who cannot prove it. A Crossville business should have a handover document before that day arrives. This guide is what goes in it.

The lockout nobody plans for

A lockout rarely announces itself. The website needs a change — a new phone number, a closed day, a price — and nobody can log in. The person who set it up used their own email address, or a password nobody wrote down, or an account under a name the business does not recognise. The host asks for proof of ownership, and the business cannot prove it, because the proof is an email address it does not control. Days pass, the change does not happen, and customers see a website that is wrong.

The lockout is not a technical problem. It is a paperwork problem, made on the day the website was built and discovered months or years later. The fix is also paperwork: a short document, kept where the owner can find it, that names every account the website depends on and who can reach it. The document takes twenty minutes to write. The lockout it prevents takes a week to fix, if it can be fixed at all.

The document: four accounts, one page

The handover document names four accounts, on one page. The hosting account: which provider, which email address it is registered under, and who holds the password. The domain registrar: where the domain name lives, when it renews, and who can reach it. The website itself: how to log in to edit the pages, and who has that login. And the email: where the business’s mailboxes live, and who controls them. Four accounts, four lines each, one page — and the business owns its website again.

The document should also say who holds each password, and where the passwords live. “The designer has it” is not an answer; it is a favour the business may not be able to call in. Each password should be in a password manager the business controls, with a second person able to reach it. A document that names a person who has left is a document that is already out of date — and the quarterly check, ten minutes, four times a year, is what keeps it current.

The password manager the business owns

Passwords for a business website should live in a password manager the business owns — not in one person’s memory, not in a notebook in a drawer, and not in the designer’s personal account. The owner holds the manager, a second person has access to it, and every website login goes inside it: hosting, domain, website editing, email, and the payment card the hosting renews on. When a person leaves, the business changes one password in the manager and the handover is done.

The manager also solves the problem of the personal email address. Hosting accounts are often registered to the email of the person who set them up, and that address leaves with them. The accounts should be registered to an email the business controls — a business address, not a personal one — so that password resets and renewal notices reach the business, not a former employee. Changing the account email is a five-minute job while the current holder is still there to confirm it, and a week-long argument after they have gone.

The backup that proves ownership

A backup is usually thought of as protection against a broken website, but it is also protection against a lockout. A business that holds a copy of its own website files and content — downloaded this month, stored somewhere the host does not control — can rebuild its site on new hosting even if it can never get back into the old account. The backup is proof the business owns the site, in the most practical sense: it holds the thing itself.

The backup belongs in the handover document too: where the copy is stored, when it was last taken, and who took it. A copy from a year ago is a copy of a website the business no longer has, so the copy should be refreshed monthly, on the same day, by the same person — and the person who takes it should be named in the document, so the job does not die quietly when they leave. A backup and a handover are the same job, done twice: once for the files, once for the keys.

The handover, done in one afternoon

A handover takes one afternoon, and it should be done while the person who set the site up is still there to answer questions. Change the account emails to ones the business controls. Move the passwords into the business’s password manager, with a second person given access. Download a copy of the files and the content, and store it where the host does not control it. Write the document, one page, four accounts, and put it where the owner can find it.

Then test it: log in using only the document, from a computer that has never seen the accounts before, and change one small thing — a word on a page — and change it back. If the document gets the business in, the handover is real. If it does not, the gaps are found in an afternoon, while the person who can fill them is still in the room. A Crossville business that does this once will never spend a week locked out of its own website.
