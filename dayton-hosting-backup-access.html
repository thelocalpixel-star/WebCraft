Dayton Hosting Backups and Access

Most small businesses discover their hosting backup the way they discover their insurance: on the day they need it. A Dayton business should know two things about its hosting before that day arrives — where the backup is, and who can get into the account. If the answer to either question is one person, or nobody is sure, the business has a problem that costs nothing to fix today and a great deal to fix later.

A backup is only real if you can restore it

A host that says it keeps backups is saying very little. The question is whether the business can get one back, how fast, and how far back it goes. A backup from yesterday is useful when today’s update breaks the site. A backup from a month ago is useful when the business discovers the break a month late. A backup nobody has ever tried to restore is a promise, not a plan — and the first time to test a restore is not the morning the site is down.

The test takes half an hour and is worth doing once. Ask the host, or the person who manages the site, to restore the backup to a test address and show that the restored copy works — the pages load, the form sends, the phone number is right. If the restore works, the business has a backup. If it does not, the business has learned something cheaply instead of expensively. A backup that has never been tested is a backup the business should assume does not work.

The two copies that matter

A website has two parts worth backing up, and they live in different places. The files are the design, the pages and the images — the visible site. The content is the words, the form entries and the settings — the part that changes when the business updates a price or adds a page. A backup that holds only the files can rebuild the look of the site, but not the words. A backup that holds only the content can rebuild the words, but not the look. The business needs both, from the same date.

For a small Dayton site, the practical rule is simple: one automatic backup from the host, and one copy the business holds itself — a download of the files and a copy of the content, stored somewhere the host does not control. Two copies in two places, from the same week, is enough. Ten copies in one place is not, because one place can fail all at once. The second copy does not need to be fancy. A folder on the office computer, updated once a month, is a second copy.

Who holds the keys — and who should

Access to a hosting account usually belongs to one person: the one who set it up. That person may be the owner, an employee, or a web designer who has long since moved on. The business should know, in writing, who that person is, what email address the account is registered under, and how the password is stored. If the answer is “the designer has it”, the business does not have access — it has a favour it may not be able to call in.

The fix is a short document, kept where the owner can find it: the hosting provider’s name, the account email, the domain registrar, and the person who holds each password — or better, a password manager the owner controls, with the hosting and domain logins inside it. Two people should be able to reach the account, not one. The document takes twenty minutes to write and saves a business that would otherwise spend a week locked out of its own website.

The day the person who set it up leaves

Every business eventually faces the day the person who set up the website leaves — an employee moves on, a designer retires, a volunteer stops volunteering. If the logins leave with them, the business is locked out of its own site, and the recovery is a slow exchange of emails with a host that wants proof of ownership from someone who cannot prove it. The time to prevent that is before it happens, while the person is still there to hand things over.

The handover is a checklist, not a meeting. Change the account email to one the business controls, not a personal address. Move the passwords into the business’s password manager. Download a copy of the files and the content. Write down the domain registrar and the renewal date. Four steps, one afternoon, and the business owns its website again — whichever person set it up, and wherever they go next.

The ten-minute check, once a quarter

Backups and access are not a one-time job, because both decay. Passwords change, people leave, hosts change their backup policy, and a backup from a year ago is a backup of a website the business no longer has. Once a quarter, someone should spend ten minutes on the check: can we still log in, is there a backup from this month, and does the document with the logins still name the right people.

The quarterly check also catches the slow drift that no one notices day to day — the domain renewal date creeping closer, the hosting plan renewing at a higher price, the backup that stopped running when the card expired. Ten minutes, four times a year, is the cheapest insurance a small business website has. The businesses that do it never need it urgently. The businesses that do not, need it all at once.
