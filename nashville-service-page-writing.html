Writing Service Pages for Nashville

Nashville customers search by neighbourhood. They type “plumber East Nashville” or “roofing Donelson”, not “plumber Nashville”, because the city is big enough that the neighbourhood matters. A service page should name the service and the neighbourhoods the business actually serves, in the customer’s words, without claiming the whole metro. This guide covers the neighbourhood line, the customer’s words, and the proof that sounds like Nashville.

The neighbourhood line

Nashville is not one place to a customer. East Nashville, Donelson, Brentwood, Germantown and Madison are different places with different houses, different customers and different searches. A service page that says “Nashville” and stops there is a page that could belong to any business in the city. A page that names the neighbourhoods it serves is a page a customer in one of those neighbourhoods can trust.

The line is also the honest limit. A Nashville business should name the neighbourhoods it will actually drive to, and no more. Claiming the whole metro brings enquiries from places the business cannot reach, and a customer who is told no after calling is a customer who will not call back. Three neighbourhoods named honestly beat twenty named hopefully.

Name the service the way Nashville asks for it

A customer in Nashville does not search for “comprehensive home services”. They search for “AC repair East Nashville” or “fence installation Donelson”. The service page should use those words in its headline and its first sentence, because the headline is the first thing a visitor reads and the first thing Google reads too. A page written in industry language is a page written for other businesses.

The customer’s words also decide the page’s shape. The headline names the service and the neighbourhood. The first sentence says what the service includes and who it is for. The rest of the page is proof and the next step. A service page that starts with the company’s history is a service page that has the order backwards.

One page per service, one neighbourhood list per page

Each service a customer would search for on its own gets its own page, and each page carries the same neighbourhood list — the places the business serves for that service. A business with three services needs three pages, not one page with three paragraphs, because each service has its own customers, its own questions and its own search. A page that tries to cover three services covers none of them well.

The neighbourhood list should be the same on every page, because the service area does not change with the service. If the business serves East Nashville for plumbing, it serves East Nashville for drain cleaning too. One list, written once, kept accurate, and repeated — that is the service area, and a customer who reads it knows whether to call.

Proof that sounds like Nashville, not a template

Proof is a detail only a business that works in Nashville would know. A roofer can say how a roof handles a Middle Tennessee summer and what the spring storms do to gutters in Donelson. A plumber can say what the older houses in East Nashville usually need, and what the newer builds in Brentwood do not. Those details are proof, and they are also the words a Nashville customer uses when they search.

Proof is not a list of adjectives. Experienced, reliable and trusted are words every business uses, and they prove nothing. Proof is what the work includes, how long it takes, what the guarantee covers, and what happens if something goes wrong. One paragraph of that is worth more than a page of claims — and it is the paragraph a customer remembers.

End with the next step a Nashville customer takes

A service page should end with one next step, and it should be the step a Nashville customer actually takes. For most service businesses that is a phone call, and the number should be tappable on a phone, on every page, without scrolling. For some it is a form, and the form should ask for the service, the neighbourhood and a way to reply — three fields, no more.

The next step should also say what happens after it. “Call and we will give you a free estimate, usually the same day” is a next step. “Contact us for more information” is not one. A Nashville customer who has read a service page that names their neighbourhood and answers their question should not have to guess what to do next — the page should tell them, clearly, once.
