Pre-Launch Checks for a Gatlinburg Site

A Gatlinburg business does not wait for visitors to plan. The visitor is already in town, standing on the street, deciding on a phone, on mobile data, right now — where to eat, what to do, who to call. A website about to launch for that visitor should be checked the way the visitor will use it: on a phone, on slow data, with one thumb, in a hurry. This guide is that check, done before launch, while the fix is still cheap.

The visitor is already in town

Most website advice assumes a visitor at a desk, planning ahead. A Gatlinburg visitor is not at a desk. They are in town, on foot or in a car, deciding in the next ten minutes. They search, they tap, they decide — and they decide fast, because the next result is one tap away. A site that loads slowly, hides its number, or makes the visitor hunt for the hours loses that visitor before the business ever knows they existed.

The launch check should therefore start from the visitor’s position, not the business’s. Open the new site on a phone, outside, in the town, on mobile data — not on office wifi, not on a desk, not on a quiet morning. Load the homepage, find the number, find the hours, find the directions. If any of those takes more than a few seconds, the site is not ready, however good it looks on a desk. A launch-day fix takes ten minutes. A customer-found fix takes a customer.

The slow-data test

Load the whole site on a throttled connection — the phone’s data saver on, or the slowest connection the business can find — and watch what happens. A site that loads quickly on office wifi and slowly on mobile data is a site carrying images far larger than a phone needs, or a site on hosting that cannot keep up. The fix is to size the images for the web before launch, and to know what the hosting covers if the site slows down later.

The test should cover every page a visitor might land on from search, not just the homepage. A visitor who finds a service page through search should get the same fast load as a visitor who starts at the front door. Every page is a front door for someone who found it through search, and every front door needs to open quickly. While testing, check the site on the slowest phone the business has, not the newest one — the visitor’s phone is rarely the newest one.

The directions and the hours, checked from the street

Tap the directions link on the new site and check where it leads. Does the map point at the right place, on the right street, with the right entrance. A map link that points at the wrong town, or at a business that moved, sends a visitor to the wrong place by the business’s own website. The check should be done from the street, on a phone, following the directions the site gives — because the business knows where it is, and the visitor does not.

Read the hours on the new site and ask whether they are true today — not in general, not last summer, but today. A business that keeps different hours in different seasons needs a website that says which season it is in. “Open daily” is not an answer when the business closes early on a particular day. One honest line — “Closed Monday for a private event” — saves a trip and keeps the customer for the next visit. A launch is the right time to set this habit, because the hours checked once are the hours the business will remember to check again.

The one-thumb test

The whole site, on a phone, with one hand, in sunlight. Can the visitor read the text without zooming. Can they tap the number, the directions and the booking or form button without hitting the wrong thing. Does the site load fast enough that they do not give up waiting. A site that passes at a desk and fails on a phone fails where most visitors actually are — and a visitor standing on the street deciding whether to call is not going to wait for a slow page, or pinch and zoom to read a number.

The one-thumb test also covers the form. Fill it in as a visitor would, with a real email address, and send it — then watch for the reply, not just the thank-you page. A form that shows a thank-you page and sends nothing is the most common launch-day failure, and only a real test message finds it. Three fields — the service, the town, and a way to reply — one promise about when the answer comes, and a test message that actually arrives. That is the whole check.

The launch-night check

The final check should be done by someone who did not build the site, on their own phone, in town, on launch night. Give them the address, no instructions, and one job: find what the business does, where it is, and how to get in touch — in five minutes, without asking anyone. If they can answer all three without looking, the site has done its job. If they cannot, the site has not, however good it looks.

The tester should also say what confused them, where they hesitated, and what they would have tapped if they had been a real visitor. The hesitation is the finding. A site that needs instructions is a site that is not ready, and the tester’s confusion is the most honest review the site will ever get. Fix what they find that night, while the launch is still fresh — because tomorrow the visitors are real, and they are already in town.
