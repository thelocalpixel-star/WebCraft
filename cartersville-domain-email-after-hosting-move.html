Cartersville Domains After a Hosting Move

When a business moves hosting, the website gets all the attention and the domain name gets none — and the domain name is the move. The domain is the address every customer types, every listing points to, and every search result shows. A move that copies the website perfectly and mishandles the domain sends customers to the wrong place, or nowhere at all.

The domain and the hosting are two different things

A business pays for two things that look like one: the hosting, which stores the website files, and the domain, which is the name customers type to find them. They are held by different companies, renew on different dates, and are controlled in different places. The hosting lives with the host.

The domain lives with a registrar — the company the name was registered with — and the domain’s settings, the records that say where the website and the email live, are controlled wherever the domain’s name servers point.

The confusion between the two is the most common cause of a broken move. A business that thinks its host owns its domain cannot move the domain without the host’s help. A business that thinks moving the hosting moves the domain will find the website moved and the domain still pointing at the old host.

Before any move, someone should write down, in one place, who holds the hosting, who holds the domain, and who controls the domain’s settings. If any of those three answers is “I don’t know,” that is the first thing to find out.

Who holds your domain right now

The check takes ten minutes and is worth doing before any move is planned. Look up the domain’s registration and read three things: who the registrar is, whose name and email the domain is registered under, and when it expires.

The domain should be registered in the business’s name, with a business email address, at a registrar the business can reach directly. If it is registered in the designer’s name, or the host’s name, or under a personal email address that nobody checks, the business does not fully own its own address.

If the domain is not in the business’s name, the time to fix it is before the move, while the hosting is still paid and the current holder is still answering. Transferring a domain to the business’s own registrar account takes days when the current holder cooperates, and weeks when it does not.

A business that discovers it does not own its domain in the middle of a hosting move has two problems at once, and the domain is the worse of the two.

The transfer lock and the sixty-day wait

Domains cannot always be moved when the business wants them moved. After a domain is first registered, or after it is transferred to a new registrar, it is locked for sixty days — a rule that stops a domain being stolen by a quick transfer.

The lock is real, it is not negotiable, and a move planned inside those sixty days has to wait. A business planning a hosting move should check the lock first, because a move planned for next week that cannot happen for two months is a plan, not a crisis, if it is known in advance.

The lock is also a reason to separate the two moves. A hosting move — changing where the website files live — does not need a domain transfer. The domain can stay with its current registrar, and only its settings change to point at the new host.

The domain transfer, if the business wants one, can happen later, after the sixty days, on its own timetable. One change at a time, tested before it goes live, is the whole method — and the domain is the change that should never be rushed.

The switch: lower the TTL first, then point the domain

The switch itself has a safe order. A day or two before the move, lower the domain’s TTL — the setting that controls how long the internet remembers the old address — so that when the change is made, it spreads in minutes rather than hours.

Then build the site on the new host completely, test the copy on a temporary address, and only then change the domain’s settings to point at the new host. The switch is the last step, not the first, because everything that can be tested should be tested before customers see it.

The records to change are the ones that say where the website lives and where the email goes, and they must be copied exactly — character for character. A single mistyped character sends email nowhere, and the error gives no warning.

After the switch, the test is to send a message to every address the business uses and confirm it arrives, to load the website from a phone on mobile data, and to fill in the contact form and confirm the reply arrives. A test message that arrives proves the records are right.

The week after: email, forms, and the old host kept running

Keep the old hosting running for at least a week after the switch. Some visitors and some mail servers will still hold the old address in their memory, and the old server quietly catching them is what keeps the move invisible.

Cancelling the old hosting on the day of the switch is the one mistake that turns a smooth move into a week of lost messages.

In that week, watch four things: the website loads from a phone on mobile data, not just office wifi; the contact form sends and the reply arrives; email arrives at every address; and the search results show no sudden errors.

A move on the same domain with the same addresses and the same content is invisible to Google — rankings drop when addresses change without redirects, or when a staging copy left behind a setting that blocks search engines. Check those two, and the move is done.

The domain, the email and the website all survived, because the domain was treated as the move itself.
