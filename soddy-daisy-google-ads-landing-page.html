A Soddy-Daisy Google Ads Landing Page

A Soddy-Daisy customer who clicks an ad has already decided to look. The landing page has one job: confirm the service, the area and the next step — before the visitor goes back to the search results.

The homepage is the wrong landing page

A homepage has to introduce the whole business: every service, the company story, the service area, the contact details. That is its job, and it does it well. But a person who clicked an ad for one specific service does not need the introduction. They need confirmation that this page does the job they searched for, in the town they live in, at a price they can start a conversation about.

Every extra service on the landing page is another reason to leave. A Soddy-Daisy homeowner searching for a roof repair who lands on a page that also sells gutters, siding, decks and painting has to find the roof repair among the rest — and most visitors will not bother. The ad bought a visit for one job; the page should spend it on that job.

One page, one job

A good landing page answers four questions in order: what service is this, where do you do it, what does it cost to start the conversation, and how do I reach you. Everything on the page should serve one of those four. A photo of real work helps. A paragraph about the company’s founding does not, and neither does a list of services the visitor did not ask about.

Write the page for the person who is already convinced enough to click. They do not need to be sold on the idea of the service — they searched for it. They need to be sold on this business, for this job, today. That is a shorter argument, and a page that makes it quickly wins the call that a longer page loses.

Put the phone number where a thumb can reach it

Most Soddy-Daisy searches happen on a phone — from the driveway, the job site, the kitchen table. The phone number belongs at the top of the page, big enough to tap without zooming, and it should stay visible as the visitor scrolls. A number hidden in the footer is a number the business did not really want called.

The form matters just as much, and it should ask for as little as possible: name, phone number, and a short description of the job. Every extra field is a reason to close the page. A form that asks for the customer’s address, preferred date, budget range and how they heard about you is a form that was designed for the business, not the customer.

Proof a small town can check

In a small town, proof is local and checkable. A photo of a finished job on a street the visitor knows beats a stock photo of a house that could be anywhere — and a stock photo presented as your own work is a trust problem waiting to happen. Use real photos of real work, with the customer’s permission, and say what the photo shows.

Name the service area honestly: Soddy-Daisy, Dayton, Hixson and the nearby communities you actually serve. Hours that match everywhere — website, map listing, voicemail — matter more than a slogan. A small-town customer who has been given the wrong hours once does not come back to check whether you fixed them.

Test the page before you pay for the clicks

Before the first dollar of ad spend, open the page on your own phone. Time how long it takes to load on a mobile connection. Tap the phone number and confirm it dials. Fill in the form and confirm the message reaches the inbox you actually monitor. Then hand the phone to someone else and watch them try to find the service, the area and the number.

Fix what the test finds before the campaign starts, not after the budget is spent. A slow page, a broken form or a wrong number will waste every click the ads buy, and the business will blame the ads for what the page did. The test costs an hour. The clicks it saves cost far more.
