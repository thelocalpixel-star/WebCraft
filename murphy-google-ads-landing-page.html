A Landing Page for Your Murphy Google Ads

A Murphy business paying for Google Ads clicks should send those clicks to a page built for the advertised service, not to the homepage. Murphy is a small mountain town, and the visitor deciding on a phone has less patience than anywhere: the service, the area, and a phone number a thumb can reach, all in the first screen.

One page, one job, one town

The ad promises one service, so the page should deliver that service and nothing else. A Murphy contractor advertising deck repair should land the visitor on deck repair in Murphy and the towns around it — not on a homepage that also sells roofing, painting, and a company history. Every extra job on the page is a reason to leave.

One town matters as much as one job. Murphy sits in Cherokee County, near the Hiwassee River and the Georgia line, and the businesses that work here serve Murphy, Andrews, and Hayesville, or the Blue Ridge side of the line. A page that names the towns it serves tells the visitor the business can actually come.

The first screen answers three questions

A visitor on a phone wants three things before scrolling: what the service is, whether the business serves their town, and how to reach a person. Put the service in the headline, the area in the first line, and the phone number where a thumb lands without hunting. Anything that pushes those three things down the page is costing calls.

Proof belongs on that first screen too, but only proof a stranger in Murphy can check: a real photo of recent work nearby, a service area named in plain words, or a review from a customer in the same town. A stock photo and a slogan prove nothing, and a visitor who has three other businesses one tap away will not wait to be convinced.

Visitors and locals need different proof

Murphy has two kinds of customers: the people who live here year-round and the visitors passing through the mountains. A local wants to know you will still be here next month and that you know the roads, the weather, and the houses. A visitor wants to know you can help this week, before they drive home. The page should speak to both, and the proof should say which is which.

The call to action can stay the same for both — call now — but the reassurance changes. For a local, it is the service area and the years in the county. For a visitor, it is the response time and the fact that the business answers the phone. Write the page so either reader finds their reason in the first screen.

The form should ask for less, not more

If the page asks for a form instead of a call, keep it short: name, phone number, the service, and the town. Every extra field is a reason to abandon it, and a form that asks for an address, a budget, and a preferred date before the business has even said hello is a form that will sit empty.

The form also needs an owner. Somebody should read it the same day, call back the same day, and say when the work can happen. A form nobody reads for two days costs more than no form at all, because the visitor who filled it in has already called the next business on the list.

Test the page on your own phone before you pay for clicks

Before the first ad runs, open the page on your own phone, on the kind of connection a visitor has on a mountain road, and try to do the one job it exists for. If you cannot reach the service, the area, and the phone number in under a minute, fix the page before you pay for the clicks that land on it.

Test it again after the first week of ads, with the real numbers in front of you: how many visitors, how many calls, how many forms. If the page converts poorly, change the page before you change the budget. A good page turns a click into a call; a bad page turns the same click into a bounce, and the business blames the ads for what the page did.
