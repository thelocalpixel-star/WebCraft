A Landing Page for Your Murphy Google Ads

Google Ads clicks are paid for, so they should land on a page built for the advertised service — not on the homepage. Murphy is a small mountain town in Cherokee County, near the Hiwassee River and the Georgia line, and a visitor deciding on a phone here has less patience than anywhere else.

One page, one job, one place

One ad, one service, one page. A Murphy contractor advertising deck repair should land the visitor on deck repair in Murphy and the towns around it — not on a homepage that also sells roofing, painting, and a company history. Every extra job on the page is a reason to leave.

The town matters as much as the job. Murphy sits in Cherokee County, near the Hiwassee River and the Georgia line, and the businesses that work here serve Murphy, Andrews, and Hayesville, or the Blue Ridge side of the line. Name the towns, and the visitor knows the business can actually come.

Three answers in the first screen

A visitor on a phone wants three answers before scrolling: what the service is, which towns it covers, and how to reach a person. The headline should carry the service, the first line the area, and the phone number should sit where a thumb lands. Anything that pushes those three answers down the page is costing calls.

The first screen should carry proof too — but only proof a stranger in Murphy can check: a real photo of recent work nearby, a service area named in plain words, or a review from a customer in the same town.

A stock photo and a slogan prove nothing, and a visitor who has three other businesses one tap away will not wait to be convinced.

Two readers, two kinds of proof

Murphy has two kinds of customers: the people who live here year-round and the visitors passing through the mountains. A local wants to know you will still be here next month and that you know the roads, the weather, and the houses. A visitor wants to know you can help this week, before they drive home.

The page should speak to both, and the proof should say which is which.

The call to action stays the same for both — call now. What changes is the reassurance: for a local, the towns served and the years in the county; for a visitor, the response time and the fact that the business answers the phone.

Either reader should find their reason in the first screen — the local and the visitor both.

Ask for less on the form, not more

A form, if the page uses one instead of a call, should ask for four things only: name, phone number, the service, and the town. A visitor on a mountain road, with a weak signal, will not fill in an address, a budget, and a date before the business has even said hello.

A form needs an owner, and the owner needs a rule: read it the same day, call back the same day, and give a date for the work. Two days of silence costs more than no form at all — the visitor has already called the next business, and in Cherokee County the next one is a tap away.

Test the page before you pay for clicks

Before paying for the first click, try the page yourself: open it on your own phone, on the signal a visitor gets on a mountain road, and do the one job it exists for.

If the service, the area, and the phone number are not all reachable in under a minute, fix the page before paying for the clicks that land on it.

After the first week of ads, judge the page by its numbers: visitors, calls, forms. If it converts poorly, fix the page before raising the budget.

The page decides what a click becomes: a call, or a bounce. When the page is the problem, the business still blames the ads.
